How to solve this chapter

First ask which legal problem is being tested: (A) occupational hazardous-drug exposure and USP <800>, (B) federal compounding/outsourcing under 503A/503B, or (C) nuclear/radiation pharmacy. Then identify the actor, activity, legal authority, license, timing, and record/reporting obligation.

Current regulation guard: The 247 CMR 13.00 nuclear regulation enacted in 2005 remains the listed enacted version; Massachusetts also hosts a proposed revision. Do not use draft nuclear regulation language as though it had taken effect. Also, the current NIOSH 2024 list uses two tables. USP <800> FAQ updated January 21, 2026, is educational rather than the official subscriber-only chapter text.

Quick memory: HD = exposure control; 503B = registered compounding facility and cGMP; nuclear = radioactive material, qualified nuclear pharmacist, calibration and special 72-hour/3-year records.

1. Hazardous drugs vs. controlled substances

Part: Hazardous Drug Safety — USP <800> · Korean origin: w4-c-1 · Source review November 9, 2026

Hazardous drugs (HDs) are identified because exposure during handling may harm personnel, patients, or the environment. Controlled substances are classified under the federal/state scheduling systems addressing abuse, dependence, prescribing, possession, and diversion. A medication may fit both categories, only one, or neither.

ClassificationWhat question does it answer?Examples of controls
Hazardous drug (NIOSH / USP <800>)Can handling expose workers or the environment to toxic risk?Containment, protective equipment, procedures, handling lists
Controlled substance (DEA / MA 94C)What prescribing, storage, diversion and inventory controls apply?Schedule-specific records, security, ordering and dispensing rules
Hazardous wasteWhat environmental rules govern disposal of the discarded material?EPA/state waste categorization and licensed disposal
Radioactive materialWhat radiological safety rules apply?NRC/MA radiation license, shielding, monitoring, decay and disposal

USP <795> primarily addresses nonsterile preparation quality, USP <797> addresses sterile preparation quality, and USP <800> adds exposure prevention for HD handling (receiving, storage, compounding, administration, transport, and disposal). Sterile chemotherapy compounding must protect both the product from microorganisms and the worker from drug exposure.

Example: A controlled anxiolytic appearing on a hazardous-drug handling list still needs both controlled-substance safeguards and HD procedures; scheduling alone does not determine the required hood.

MPJE exam trap: HD status does not mean Schedule II–V. A drug may be hazardous but not federally controlled, while a controlled drug is not necessarily hazardous.

Authorities: USP <800> · MA Board HD advisory

2. The current NIOSH list and the pharmacy-specific HD list

Part: Hazardous Drug Safety — USP <800> · Korean origin: w4-c-2 · Source review November 9, 2026

The current NIOSH List of Hazardous Drugs in Healthcare Settings, 2024 is publication 2025-103, released December 2024. It supersedes the 2016 edition and uses two tables, not the prior three. Check NIOSH notices after publication: a July 2025 notice added additional products with manufacturer special-handling information.

Important 2026 clarification: Table 1 is no longer titled “antineoplastic.” NIOSH identifies antineoplastic agents by AHFS class 10:00; some appear in Table 2. But the specific USP <800> full-containment antineoplastic requirement is directed to the NIOSH Table 1 antineoplastics requiring manipulation. Avoid assuming every drug in Table 1 is antineoplastic or every antineoplastic in Table 2 triggers the identical provision.

Every entity must maintain a drug- and dosage-form-specific hazardous-drug list for the products it actually handles. Incorporate NIOSH-listed drugs and evaluate newly marketed drugs and manufacturer instructions even if the drug has not yet appeared on the published list. Review and document the facility list at least every 12 months.

Handling exampleDifferent exposure risk
Counting intact tablets from a unit-dose packageUsually less manipulation
Crushing tablets or opening capsules for a suspensionMore powder release; reassess full containment and licensing
Cleaning a spill or disposing of wasteExposure may arise outside the compounding hood
Receiving damaged cartonsMay require controlled unpacking and spill response

Example: A newly purchased drug has explicit handling warnings in its FDA labeling but has not yet appeared in an old pharmacy list. The pharmacy should evaluate and update its list rather than wait for the next NIOSH publication.

MPJE exam trap: The current NIOSH document is called the 2024 list despite publication number 2025-103. Do not memorize the old three-table numbering as current.

Authorities: NIOSH 2024 list (pub. 2025-103) · USP <800> FAQ, Jan 2026, Q9–12 · MA HD advisory

3. Assessment of risk (AOR): what it can and cannot waive

Part: Hazardous Drug Safety — USP <800> · Korean origin: w4-c-3 · Source review November 9, 2026

An assessment of risk (AOR) identifies the exposure risk for a specific HD and dosage form, permitting alternative containment strategies/work practices only when USP <800> allows that choice. The assessment must document the HD type, dosage form, potential exposure, packaging, and planned manipulation. Review it at least every 12 months and after material changes.

Examples of alternatives include unit-of-use packaging, wearing appropriate gloves, using a dedicated counting tray, and assigning particular handling duties. The assessment is not a waiver of HD safety rules.

SituationCan the AOR avoid all required containment?Reason
Counting intact finished tablets for a qualifying non-antineoplastic HDPotentially, if drug/form assessment supports documented alternativesExposure is evaluated for that activity
Manipulating a NIOSH Table 1 antineoplastic HDNo general AOR escape from full containmentUSP <800> full-containment pathway
Compounding with a NIOSH HD active pharmaceutical ingredient (API)No general AOR escape from full containmentPowder exposure and compounding risk
Crushing an HD tablet to compound an oral suspensionNot justified by an AOR made only for intact-tablet countingManipulation changes the exposure route

Example: An AOR permits intact tablet counting with dedicated equipment. A prescriber then orders a liquid made by crushing those tablets. The pharmacy must reevaluate the activity, facility containment and applicable complex-nonsterile license; the old counting AOR is not sufficient.

MPJE exam trap: An AOR must address each drug and dosage form; a blanket assessment covering “all chemo drugs” is not enough. With no qualifying AOR, full protections apply.

Authorities: MA USP <800> community advisory · USP FAQ Q11–14

4. Containment controls, rooms, and designated person

Part: Hazardous Drug Safety — USP <800> · Korean origin: w4-c-4 · Source review November 9, 2026

C-PEC is the containment primary engineering control (e.g., appropriate biological safety cabinet or containment hood) that helps prevent HD contamination escaping the immediate preparation area. C-SEC is the surrounding containment room. A standard positive-pressure laminar airflow workbench protects the sterile product but does not necessarily protect the worker from HD exposure.

Where full containment is required, a C-SEC must maintain negative pressure relative to adjacent areas; USP FAQ gives the normal negative differential range 0.01–0.03 inch water column, continuously, with applicable air-change/exhaust and classification conditions. Specific nonsterile and sterile room requirements differ; a full sterile HD suite typically needs appropriate classified air and containment concurrently.

Massachusetts treats HD compounding as requiring the appropriate complex nonsterile or sterile specialty authority when those activities are performed. A pharmacy cannot place a powder hood in an ordinary room and assume the licensure, ventilation, or testing requirements are satisfied.

The designated person for USP <800> oversees implementation of SOPs, compliance, risk assessment, training, and environmental safety. The Massachusetts HD advisory says this person need not automatically be a licensed pharmacist or physically on-site. This does not remove separate duties assigned to the Manager of Record or licensed pharmacist under other MA rules.

MPJE exam trap: Do not confuse positive first air for product protection with negative-pressure HD containment. An ordinary nonhazardous hood is not automatically a C-PEC.

Authorities: USP <800> FAQ, facilities · MA HD advisory · USP official chapter record

5. PPE, qualification, and automatic tablet counters

Part: Hazardous Drug Safety — USP <800> · Korean origin: w4-c-5 · Source review November 9, 2026

For HD compounding, two pairs of chemotherapy gloves meeting ASTM D6978 are required along with appropriate gowns, head/hair coverings, and two pairs of shoe covers under the applicable USP <800> provisions. In sterile HD compounding, the outer gloves must also be sterile. Glove material alone (e.g., “nitrile”) does not prove chemotherapy permeability testing.

Gowns must resist HD permeation; disposable PPE must not be reused. Face, eye and respiratory protection depend on the task and exposure risk. A surgical mask is not a substitute for a required fitted respirator; an N95 must be fit-tested. Closed-system drug-transfer devices (CSTDs) are recommended for compounding, while administration of NIOSH Table 1 antineoplastic drugs requires a compatible CSTD when the dosage form allows.

Employees must receive role-based training before independently handling HDs; competency is assessed initially and at least every 12 months. Personnel who also compound sterile preparations must independently satisfy USP <797> competency schedules (e.g., every 6 or 3 months, as applicable).

Automatic counters: NIOSH Table 1 antineoplastic tablets and capsules must not be placed in automated counting/packaging machines. Use for other HDs is assessed with the facility's AOR; Massachusetts recommends avoiding their use because retained powdered residues can contaminate equipment.

MPJE exam trap: Two pairs of chemotherapy gloves are required for HD compounding. A double-gloving rule, the annual HD competence check, and sterile-compounding qualification cycles are separate facts.

Authorities: USP FAQ, PPE and automated counting · MA HD advisory

6. Deactivation, decontamination, cleaning, disinfection

Part: Hazardous Drug Safety — USP <800> · Korean origin: w4-c-6 · Source review November 9, 2026

USP <800> distinguishes four steps that solve different problems. These processes may be performed in the required sequence using validated/appropriate chemicals, contact times, protective equipment, and compatible materials.

OperationPurpose
DeactivationMake hazardous-drug residue less hazardous, when possible
DecontaminationRemove remaining HD residue from the surface
CleaningRemove dirt, organic and inorganic matter
DisinfectionReduce microbial contamination (particularly important before sterile compounding)

Appropriate HD deactivation agents depend on product labeling; where no agent is specified, the 2026 USP FAQ discusses EPA-registered oxidizers (e.g., peroxide or sodium-hypochlorite formulations). One 70% alcohol wipe does not automatically accomplish all four functions. Chemical residue also must not be introduced into a sterile product.

Damaged shipment example: A carton leaks suspicious drug powder during receiving. Do not open it at the normal unpacking counter or wipe visible powder with bare hands. Restrict access, follow spill/receiving SOPs, use appropriate gloves and containment, and arrange for safe return or disposal.

MPJE exam trap: “Disinfected” refers to microorganisms, not necessarily removal or chemical deactivation of hazardous residue.

Authorities: USP FAQ, deactivation/cleaning · MA HD handling guidance

7. Spills, waste classification, transportation

Part: Hazardous Drug Safety — USP <800> · Korean origin: w4-c-7 · Source review November 9, 2026

An HD spill program should specify who may respond, spill-kit contents and limits, restricting access, PPE/respirator choices, exposure response, cleanup/disposal steps, documentation and follow-up. Personnel should not attempt an uncontrolled spill beyond their training or available equipment.

Waste or materialDifferent authorities / concerns
Hazardous-drug residue and contaminated PPEUSP <800>, EPA/RCRA where applicable, MA and local disposal requirements
Controlled-substance inventory destined for destructionDEA and MA controlled-substance handling, non-retrievable destruction and records
Biohazardous medical wasteApplicable biological/infectious waste rules
Radioactive wasteRadiation authority, license conditions, NRC/105 CMR 120 standards

A supplier or reverse distributor must be legally able to accept the exact type of material. A company approved to receive controlled substances is not automatically authorized to take radioactive or RCRA-regulated hazardous waste. Packaging/transport must reduce leakage, breakage, aerosol release, or accidental exposure.

Example: A spilled antineoplastic powder can create occupational exposure even if it is not DEA-controlled. Disposal requirements still depend on whether it meets state/federal hazardous-waste classifications.

MPJE exam trap: Hazardous-drug, hazardous waste, controlled-substance destruction, and radioactive waste are different legal classifications. The name “reverse distributor” does not settle all four.

Authorities: MA HD advisory · USP <800>

8. 503A traditional compounding vs. 503B outsourcing

Part: FDA Outsourcing — Section 503B · Korean origin: w4-c-8 · Source review November 9, 2026

Section 503A is the traditional patient-specific pharmacy/physician compounding pathway, with specified exemptions for qualifying drug products. Section 503B permits outsourcing facilities to compound sterile drugs (and possibly nonsterile drugs) for individual prescriptions or certain orders without named patients, such as hospital office stock. A 503B facility is defined by one geographic location or address and must comply with federal registration and reporting rules.

Question503A traditional pharmacy503B outsourcing facility
Patient-specific prescriptionGenerally essential, with limited anticipatory preparationMay or may not obtain identified-patient prescriptions under federal law
Quality standardEligible 503A products exempt from specified cGMP provisionscGMP applies
FDA registration503A status alone does not create 503B registrationFederal 503B registration, fee, reports, risk-based inspections
FDA product approvalNo; exemption if conditions metNo; conditional new-drug-approval exemption if conditions met
Drug Supply Chain Security ActSeparate analysisQualifying drugs may receive FD&C Act §582 exemption
Massachusetts obligationsMA pharmacy/compounding licensingMA outsourcing registration under 247 CMR 21 as applicable

Important: A facility merely appearing on FDA's registered 503B list does not mean FDA has approved its products, determined compliance with cGMP, or that the specific product qualifies for federal exemptions. Check actual facility, inspection/recall history, product eligibility, and Massachusetts authorization separately.

Example: A hospital buys qualifying sterile stock from a 503B outsourcing facility for future administration. A retail 503A pharmacy cannot simply infer that it may distribute unlimited anonymous office stock under the same federal exemption.

MPJE exam trap: “503B is FDA-registered” does not mean “FDA-approved drug.” Registration, CGMP compliance, product eligibility, and MA registration are four different questions.

Authorities: FDA 503A/503B comparison · FDA 503B registration FAQs · 247 CMR 21.00

9. 503B bulk ingredients, commercial copies, wholesaling

Part: FDA Outsourcing — Section 503B · Korean origin: w4-c-9 · Source review November 9, 2026

A 503B outsourcing facility may use a bulk drug substance only under a qualifying route: the substance is on FDA's 503B clinical-need bulks list, or the compounded drug is on FDA's shortage list at the times of compounding, distribution, and dispensing, subject to applicable policies. The bulk substance must come from an FDA-registered manufacturer, have a valid COA, and comply with applicable compendial monographs.

Other restrictions address drugs withdrawn as unsafe/ineffective, impermissible essentially identical copies, demonstrably difficult-to-compound products, and REMS medications with ETASU where comparable controls are required. Not on the 503B list does not automatically mean a substance can never be used—verify relevant statutory route and FDA interim policy—but does not grant a general shortcut.

Wholesaling restriction: Under section 503B(a)(8), the drug generally cannot be sold/transferred by another entity after the outsourcing facility compounded it. The statute distinguishes lawful administration in a healthcare setting and dispensing under a valid prescription from unrestricted intermediary wholesale resale. FDA's June 2023 wholesaling interpretive guidance was published as draft.

Shortage example: “Our local pharmacy is out” is not the same as the product appearing on FDA's official shortage list; the drug and dates must be verified individually.

MPJE exam trap: The FDA shortage-list condition for 503B bulk API references compounding, distribution and dispensing. Local stock-out or a past shortage does not create ongoing blanket permission.

Authorities: FDA 503B bulk drug substances · FDA drug shortages policy · FDA federal comparison

10. 503B registration, product reporting, and labels

Part: FDA Outsourcing — Section 503B · Korean origin: w4-c-10 · Source review November 9, 2026

The federal annual 503B re-registration window is October 1–December 31. Initial registration triggers a product report; subsequent drug product reports are submitted every June and December in the required structured product labeling (SPL) format unless waived.

Federal filingWindow or coverage
503B annual registration renewalOctober 1 – December 31; pay annual establishment fee
June product reportJune 1–30; products compounded December 1 – May 31
December product reportDecember 1–30; products compounded June 1 – November 30

Section 503B label: As applicable, show “This is a compounded drug,” outsourcing facility name/address/phone, lot or batch, established drug name and strength, dosage form, quantity/volume, date compounded, expiration date, storage/handling, available NDC, required ingredient information, “Not for resale,” and “Office Use Only” if distributed/dispensed other than for an identified patient prescription. Container requirements include route of administration and adverse-event reporting information.

The 503B statutory expiration date terminology should not be casually substituted with the BUD terminology used in USP <797> for traditional pharmacy CSPs. Each product's quality and supporting stability requirements still matter.

MPJE exam trap: Federal renewal is annual, FDA product reporting is twice annually, and Massachusetts outsourcing registration expires under a separate odd-numbered-year cycle.

Authorities: FDA registration and product reporting · FDA 503B labeling provisions · FDA 503B FAQs

11. 503B serious and unexpected adverse-event reports

Part: FDA Outsourcing — Section 503B · Korean origin: w4-c-11 · Source review November 9, 2026

An outsourcing facility must report a serious and unexpected adverse drug experience involving its compounded prescription drugs to FDA as soon as possible, but within 15 calendar days after first receiving the information. It must also investigate and submit relevant follow-up information within 15 calendar days of receiving new information, or as otherwise requested by FDA.

Serious includes death, life-threatening events, inpatient hospitalization or extension, significant disability/incapacity, congenital anomalies, and other medically important events. Unexpected is judged against applicable labeling, not whether the reporter personally finds the outcome surprising. The underlying 21 CFR 310.305 recordkeeping framework includes a 10-year retention requirement for adverse drug experience records.

Example: A hospital reports a new serious unexpected reaction associated with a 503B product today. The outsourcing facility cannot postpone the initial FDA report until all root-cause testing is complete. It reports within the required 15-calendar-day clock and follows up when additional information arrives.

Massachusetts pharmacy SADE/defect/other state reporting requirements use their own triggers and timelines. Do not merge them into the federal 503B rule or assume satisfying one report automatically satisfies another.

MPJE exam trap: The 503B federal clock is 15 calendar days after initial information receipt, not 15 business days, and follow-up has a separate clock.

Authorities: FDA adverse event reporting guidance · 503B federal provisions

12. Massachusetts outsourcing registrations — 247 CMR 21

Part: Massachusetts 503B Requirements · Korean origin: w4-c-12 · Source review November 9, 2026

Federal 503B registration alone does not authorize every shipment into Massachusetts. Under 247 CMR 21.00, a Massachusetts resident outsourcing facility needs the Board's outsourcing registration, applicable MA controlled substance registration (MCSR), current FDA 503B registration, and ordinarily proof of an FDA inspection within the preceding two years. Nonresident facilities have separate application and home-state good-standing conditions and generally need similar recent FDA inspection evidence.

Provisional registration: The Board may grant certain resident applicants a provisional registration without the required recent FDA inspection. A provisional registrant may compound sterile drugs but cannot distribute or dispense them in or outside Massachusetts until it has had the relevant FDA inspection and obtained full MA outsourcing registration. Nonresident outsourcing facilities are not eligible for provisional registration.

MA requirementKey number/condition
Full resident/nonresident registration expirationDecember 31 of the applicable odd-numbered year after issuance
Written Board notice for FDA inspection, Form 483, warning letter, specified changesWithin 14 days (247 CMR 21.09)
Relocation applicationAt least 90 days before desired move unless Board approves otherwise
Resident ownership transfer advance noticeAt least 60 days before transfer under 247 CMR 21.08
At relocated facilityCannot distribute/dispense until new location FDA-inspected as required

Example: A new resident facility has provisionally registered, compounded a batch, and wants to sell it to a hospital immediately. Not allowed under 247 CMR 21.03(3) before the stated inspection and full-registration conditions.

MPJE exam trap: FDA registration, Massachusetts full registration, and provisional MA registration are not interchangeable. The MA odd-year expiration is not the FDA Oct–Dec annual renewal window.

Authorities: 247 CMR 21.02–21.12 (enacted) · MA official regulation page · FDA registration Q&A

13. Nuclear pharmacist and nuclear pharmacy licensure

Part: Nuclear Pharmacy — 247 CMR 13 & USP <825> · Korean origin: w4-c-13 · Source review November 9, 2026

A nuclear pharmacy handles radiopharmaceuticals: drugs with a radionuclide, used for diagnosis or treatment. It involves pharmacy practice and radiological safety; an ordinary pharmacist license or general compounding training alone does not confer the full authorization.

Current enacted Massachusetts rule: 247 CMR 13.00 (2005) governs nuclear pharmacies. A new proposed 247 CMR 13.00 revision is posted on the Board's draft-regulations page but is not enacted merely by being available for review. The official MA nuclear pharmacist application also reflects the existing education/experience route.

Qualification under enacted 247 CMR 13.06Requirement
Massachusetts pharmacist registrationCurrent and in good standing
Formal academic radiopharmaceutical training200 contact hours; at most 60 may be laboratory hours
Supervised practical experienceAt least 3 months full time OR 500 actual on-the-job hours under qualifying supervision/program
Board recognitionDocumentation/affidavit and Board determination/approval of qualified nuclear pharmacist status

A permitted nuclear pharmacy must be directed/managed appropriately and have a qualified nuclear pharmacist personally present while open; it must meet applicable radiation controls, facility security, shielding/monitoring, and state/NRC radioactive-material license conditions. M.G.L. c.112 §39B prohibits operating the same nuclear pharmacy as a general retail or institutional pharmacy. USP <825> governs radiopharmaceutical preparation/compounding/dispensing in its scope, alongside the applicable radiation rules.

Example: A MA pharmacist with no nuclear qualification wants to independently dispense a diagnostic radionuclide product. General pharmacist licensure alone is insufficient.

MPJE exam trap: 200 classroom/contact hours and 500 practical hours are two different training components, and the laboratory portion may account for no more than 60 of the formal contact hours.

Authorities: 247 CMR 13.06 (enacted) · M.G.L. c.112 §39B · MA nuclear pharmacist application · MA draft 247 CMR 13 revision · USP <825>

14. Nuclear prescriptions, shielding labels, records, 72-hour rule

Part: Nuclear Pharmacy — 247 CMR 13 & USP <825> · Korean origin: w4-c-14 · Source review November 9, 2026

Radiopharmaceutical dispensing adds radiation-specific information to ordinary pharmaceutical identification. Under 247 CMR 13.05(12), the outer shielding container must identify the standard radiation symbol and “Caution – Radioactive Material,” radionuclide, chemical/common name, activity at the calibration time (mCi/µCi or Bq), liquid volume if applicable, required warnings, and expiration date/time. Additional labeling/records may be needed under the product, USP <825>, or radiation license.

Patient identification exception: Under 247 CMR 13.05(6)(d), if a patient name/address is not known when the radiopharmaceutical prescription is received, the nuclear pharmacy must obtain that information within 72 hours after dispensing; the treatment facility's address may be sufficient if the residential address is unavailable. This is a narrow nuclear rule, not a general right to dispense any ordinary prescription without identifying the patient.

The nuclear pharmacy must keep readily retrievable acquisition and disposition records for at least 3 years (247 CMR 13.05(5)). Unused prescribed radiopharmaceuticals must be returned/disposed of under the applicable radiation requirements and documented appropriately. The enacted rule also provides narrowly limited emergency bulk-supply and documentation provisions; do not misread those as ordinary pharmacy wholesale permissions.

Example: The nuclear pharmacy receives a valid hospital radiopharmaceutical prescription before the patient's name becomes available. The applicable 72-hour-after-dispensing exception can operate, but all other prescription, identification, radiation, and record requirements still apply.

MPJE exam trap: Do not confuse the 72-hour nuclear patient-name follow-up with general patient identification or other 72-hour medication rules. 3 years applies to these nuclear acquisition/disposition records.

Authorities: 247 CMR 13.05(5)–(12) · M.G.L. c.112 §39B · USP <825>

10 original MPJE-style practice questions

Choose one of four answers, then select Check answer to reveal explanations and update your score. Use Try again to restart. These are original educational questions, not recalled exam items.

Question 1. A medication is on the NIOSH hazardous drug list but is not federally scheduled. Which statement is correct?
  1. It cannot be hazardous without a DEA schedule.
  2. NIOSH hazard and federal controlled-substance status are independent classifications.
  3. All NIOSH hazardous drugs are Schedule II.
  4. Only radioactive drugs can have both statuses.

Correct answer: B.

A: Incorrect. USP <800> occupational exposure classification is separate.

B: Correct. A drug can be hazardous without being a federal controlled substance.

C: Incorrect. Scheduling is not determined by NIOSH classification.

D: Incorrect. Many nonradioactive drugs can satisfy either or both systems.

Question 2. Which document superseded the NIOSH 2016 hazardous-drug list, and how many tables does it use?
  1. The NIOSH 2024 list, publication 2025-103, using two tables.
  2. NIOSH 2018, with four tables.
  3. USP &lt;797&gt; 2023, with three tables.
  4. The DEA Schedule I–V list, using five tables.

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. The 2024 list (pub. 2025-103) is organized in two tables.

B: No such cited replacement structure applies.

C: USP <797> is a sterile-compounding standard, not the replacement NIOSH list.

D: DEA schedules do not replace the NIOSH hazard list.

Question 3. A pharmacy's AOR permits counting intact HD tablets. Can the same AOR justify crushing the tablets to compound a suspension in an ordinary room?
  1. Yes, if the patient requested the suspension.
  2. Yes, once an AOR exists for the drug, all manipulations are exempt.
  3. No; compounding/manipulation changes exposure risk and may require full containment and specialty licensure.
  4. Yes, if the tablet is not federally controlled.

Correct answer: C.

A: A patient request does not waive containment.

B: An AOR must be drug-, form-, and manipulation-specific.

C: Correct. Crushing and compounding can require different full-containment controls.

D: Controlled-substance scheduling does not determine HD containment.

Question 4. A pharmacy compounder is preparing a hazardous drug. Which glove statement reflects USP &lt;800&gt;?
  1. Any single pair of standard nitrile gloves is always sufficient.
  2. A surgical mask substitutes for chemotherapy gloves.
  3. Glove testing is unnecessary if a hood is used.
  4. Two pairs of chemotherapy gloves meeting ASTM D6978 are required for HD compounding, with other task-specific PPE.

Correct answer: D.

A: Nitrile material alone is not proof of chemo-testing.

B: Masks and gloves address different exposure routes.

C: Engineering controls do not remove PPE requirements.

D: Correct. Double chemotherapy gloves and other required PPE apply.

Question 5. What is the best distinction between a 503A pharmacy and a 503B outsourcing facility?
  1. Both are exempt from cGMP.
  2. 503B may produce qualifying office stock without identified patient prescriptions but is subject to cGMP; 503A generally relies on patient-specific prescriptions.
  3. All 503B products are FDA-approved.
  4. 503A automatically grants federal 503B registration.

Correct answer: B.

A: 503B facilities remain subject to cGMP.

B: Correct. Their prescription pathways and regulatory obligations differ.

C: Outsourcing registration is not premarket product approval.

D: 503A and 503B registration are not automatic equivalents.

Question 6. When is an outsourcing facility's regular FDA product report due?
  1. Every June and December, in addition to reporting upon initial registration.
  2. Only once every two years.
  3. Every day that a batch is shipped.
  4. Only after FDA issues a Form 483.

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Federal product reporting is semiannual, while registration renewal is annual.

B: This confuses Massachusetts license cycles with federal reporting.

C: Individual shipments are not the standard report window.

D: Product reports do not depend on a Form 483.

Question 7. A 503B outsourcing facility first receives information about a serious and unexpected adverse drug experience. Its federal initial reporting deadline is:
  1. Within 15 business days.
  2. Within 72 hours of compounding.
  3. As soon as possible, no later than 15 calendar days after initial receipt.
  4. At the next June product report.

Correct answer: C.

A: The federal window is calendar, not business, days.

B: A compounding date is not the federal AE clock.

C: Correct. Follow-up reporting has its own new-information clock.

D: The semiannual product report is a different requirement.

Question 8. A Massachusetts resident outsourcing facility holds only a provisional registration because it lacks the required recent FDA inspection. What may it do?
  1. Distribute drugs to hospitals without restriction.
  2. Ship products outside Massachusetts but not inside.
  3. Sell compounded sterile preparations if its cGMP SOPs are signed.
  4. Compound sterile drugs, but not distribute or dispense them until the inspection and full MA registration requirements are met.

Correct answer: D.

A: Provisional registration does not permit distribution.

B: The prohibition applies within and outside Massachusetts.

C: A signed SOP is not full MA registration.

D: Correct. See enacted 247 CMR 21.03.

Question 9. Under enacted Massachusetts 247 CMR 13.06, qualified nuclear pharmacist training includes:
  1. 200 formal contact hours (no more than 60 lab), plus 3 months full time or 500 hours supervised practical experience.
  2. Only a general pharmacist license and a one-hour radiation orientation.
  3. 500 classroom hours without practical training.
  4. The same 3 CE hours used for complex nonsterile compounding.

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. These are separate formal and practical requirements, plus Board recognition.

B: General licensure alone does not qualify the pharmacist.

C: The statute does not substitute all practical work with classes.

D: Complex nonsterile CE is a separate rule.

Question 10. If a patient name is unavailable when a valid nuclear radiopharmaceutical prescription is received, what special MA rule may apply?
  1. No patient identity is ever needed.
  2. The name/address is obtained within 72 hours after dispensing under the nuclear rule.
  3. The pharmacy has 10 business days to identify all patients.
  4. This applies to all ordinary controlled-substance prescriptions.

Correct answer: B.

A: Identification remains required within the specified window.

B: Correct. 247 CMR 13.05(6)(d) provides this narrow exception.

C: Ten business days is not the stated nuclear prescription allowance.

D: The exception is for the defined nuclear pharmacy pathway only.
