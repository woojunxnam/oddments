Five questions before dispensing CII

1. Real original? Proper EPCS, signed paper, qualifying emergency oral or one of three fax-original routes. 2. Legal? Authorized prescriber and legitimate medical purpose. 3. Valid when? Applicable issuance date, Do Not Fill Before, first-fill date and Massachusetts-specific deadlines. 4. How much? State single-fill 30/90-day drug category plus opioid-specific restrictions. 5. Special handling? CII cannot be refilled, although federal rules permit some sequential prescriptions and partial fills under different conditions.

Corrected from older summaries: Massachusetts's 2007 Joint Policy, corroborated by the Board's December 2025 newsletter, permits the 30-day validity window of an eligible sequential CII prescription to run from a Do Not Fill Before date. A blanket rule treating every later-dated CII as expired 30 days after original signing would be misleading. The 2024 quantity-policy expansion is separate.

1. The basic Schedule II rule: signed original, no refills, pharmacist responsibility

Original Korean unit: w2-b-1 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

Schedule II (CII) includes oxycodone, morphine, fentanyl, hydrocodone products, amphetamine and methylphenidate. Federal 21 CFR 1306.11(a) generally requires a proper, practitioner-signed original written prescription; a properly created and signed EPCS can satisfy the electronic route. An ordinary faxed copy of a paper CII is not normally the original needed for dispensing. A narrowly defined oral emergency and three fax-as-original pathways are important exceptions described below.

CII prescriptions can never be refilled. Federal 21 CFR 1306.12(a) and M.G.L. c.94C §23(b) prohibit refills even when someone writes “Refills: 2” on the form. Legitimate separate future-dated-fill prescriptions, an authorized partial filling of the same original prescription, or a genuinely new prescription are not refills. Pharmacists must also determine legitimate medical purpose, practitioner authority and corresponding responsibility under 21 CFR 1306.04.

Massachusetts endorsement: §23(c) requires pharmacist signature endorsement. DCP's electronic-prescribing guidance explains that a pharmacist's final verification of a CII e-prescription fulfills that endorsement rather than requiring someone to add a fake handwritten signature on the stored eRx. The pharmacist still performs normal final review and retains required records.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: An oxycodone paper prescription lists two refills. The pharmacist may dispense an otherwise valid original order once, but the listed refills are invalid. Another proper prescription is required for later dispensing unless a lawful partial-fill remainder applies.

MPJE exam trap: CII refill = prohibited. Sequential original prescriptions ≠ refills; lawful partial fills ≠ refills.

Official references: 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII written, oral emergency and fax-original exceptions · 21 CFR 1306.12 — no refills; multiple CII prescriptions · M.G.L. c.94C §23 — CII validity, refills, supply and endorsement · 21 CFR 1306.04 — legitimate medical purpose and corresponding responsibility · DCP Circular 19-12-108 — electronic prescribing and endorsement

2. Three dates—and the Massachusetts 30-day validity rule

Original Korean unit: w2-b-1 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

Identify these dates before judging a CII prescription: (1) date written/signed (when the prescriber actually issued the document), (2) Do Not Fill Before / earliest permitted fill date (a restriction directing the pharmacy not to dispense earlier), and (3) actual dispensing date. None is automatically interchangeable with the days' supply written for the medication.

M.G.L. c.94C §23(a) makes a Massachusetts written/electronic Schedule II prescription invalid 30 days after its applicable issuance date. When there is no valid Do Not Fill Before instruction, the normal 30-day window is based on the prescription's issue date. However: for a qualified sequential CII prescription, Massachusetts's Joint Policy 2007-01 interprets the statutory “Date of Issuance” for this purpose as the prescriber's Do Not Fill Before date. The December 2025 Massachusetts Board of Pharmacy newsletter confirms that the 30-day clock for an MA prescriber's prescription starts from the date of issuance/date written or Do Not Fill Before date, as applicable. Do not reject every sequential CII merely because its later fill date is more than 30 days after the original date written.

This special clock does not permit post-dating the written/signed date: the prescription still must truthfully identify the actual signing date. It also does not allow early filling before a written Do Not Fill Before date, or authorize the pharmacist to casually change that date. Different five-day limits apply to qualifying nonresident prescriptions and are treated in the next chapter.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: Three distinct Schedule II prescriptions are signed on November 9, 2026, with future 'Do Not Fill Before' dates for the second and third. If they meet federal and MA sequential-prescription requirements, an MA prescriber's second prescription is not categorically invalid because its permitted fill date is 30+ days after November 9. Apply the Board's later-date interpretation.

MPJE exam trap: Most important correction: MA 30 days is not always counted from the date written when a compliant sequential CII includes Do Not Fill Before.

Official references: M.G.L. c.94C §23 — CII validity, refills, supply and endorsement · MA Joint Policy 2007-01 — sequential CII orders and Do Not Fill Before · Massachusetts Board of Pharmacy December 2025 newsletter, p. 1 · 21 CFR 1306.12 — no refills; multiple CII prescriptions

3. Single-fill quantity: when Massachusetts allows 90 days versus 30 days

Original Korean unit: w2-b-2 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

2024 law change: M.G.L. c.94C §23(d) and Board/DCP Policy 2024-05 permit a single filling of up to 90 days for specified Schedule II/III classes when no other statute prohibits it: (1) drugs used to treat opioid use disorder (OUD), such as appropriate buprenorphine products; (2) non-opioid CII/CIII controlled drugs, such as methylphenidate and testosterone; and (3) drugs for implantable infusion pumps.

All other Schedule II/III prescriptions generally have a maximum of 30 days per single fill. This includes ordinary opioid pain prescriptions unless a different special rule controls. A 90-day ceiling is not a mandate to supply 90 days or a license to give more tablets than prescribed. The prescriber-authorized quantity, clinical judgment, any narrower law and specific drug/access limitations all remain in effect. Methadone used for OUD ordinarily follows the federally regulated opioid treatment program route; the state quantity allowance does not convert OTP treatment into an unrestricted community pharmacy prescription.

Outdated rule to remove from memory: under Policy 2024-05, an ordinary pharmacist no longer needs to confirm an ADHD or narcolepsy diagnosis merely to justify a non-opioid controlled medicine under this broad 90-day category. Older stimulant-only/60-day study sheets may therefore misstate the applicable maximum.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: The prescriber issues a legitimate 90-day methylphenidate prescription. It can fit the non-opioid CII 90-day single-fill category if the other requirements are met. A similar 90-day oxycodone prescription for routine pain is not approved by that same exception.

MPJE exam trap: 90 days per single fill (qualified drug category) ≠ 90 days to use the prescription. 90 days is a maximum, not an automatic amount.

Official references: M.G.L. c.94C §23 — CII validity, refills, supply and endorsement · DCP/Board Policy 2024-05 — CII and CIII quantity limits (2024) · 21 CFR 1306.04 — legitimate medical purpose and corresponding responsibility

4. A separate opioid limit: first outpatient adult prescriptions and minors

Original Korean unit: w2-b-2 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

Massachusetts M.G.L. c.94C §19D(a) generally limits an opioid prescription for an adult receiving an opiate for outpatient use for the first time to seven days. For minors, it generally limits an opiate prescription to seven days at any time and requires a risk/need discussion with the parent or guardian. These rules are prescriber issuance restrictions, not the Schedule II 30-day validity clock.

§19D(b) exceptions: if the prescriber professionally determines a greater amount is required for an acute medical condition, chronic pain, cancer-related pain, or palliative care, the prescriber may issue the needed quantity and must document the relevant condition and why a non-opiate alternative was not appropriate. §19D(c) expressly excludes medicines designed for treatment of substance abuse or opioid dependence from this seven-day issuance rule.

The pharmacist should not assume that every opioid prescription is capped at seven days regardless of patient history or indication. Conversely, a 30-day maximum under §23(d) does not override a more specific seven-day issuance restriction when it applies. Scope, prescription authenticity and drug-specific limits still matter.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: An opioid-naïve adult receives outpatient oxycodone for routine acute pain with no documented qualifying exception. A new 30-day prescription is not automatically valid merely because general Schedule II pain supplies can be up to 30 days.

MPJE exam trap: Seven-day §19D initial-outpatient/minor rule ≠ all Schedule II drugs; exceptions are condition-specific and documented.

Official references: M.G.L. c.94C §19D — outpatient opioid 7-day restrictions and exceptions · M.G.L. c.94C §23 — CII validity, refills, supply and endorsement · DCP/Board Policy 2024-05 — CII and CIII quantity limits (2024)

5. Federal multiple Schedule II prescriptions: the six essential conditions

Original Korean unit: w2-b-3 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

Although refills are prohibited, 21 CFR 1306.12(b) allows an individual practitioner to issue multiple independent CII prescriptions for an aggregate up to 90-day supply when all of these apply:

  1. Each prescription has a legitimate medical purpose and is within usual professional practice.
  2. The prescriber places a written earliest-fill / Do Not Fill Before instruction on each later prescription (the first can be immediately fillable).
  3. The prescriber concludes the arrangement does not pose an undue risk of abuse or diversion.
  4. Applicable state law permits issuing multiple prescriptions.
  5. All other federal and state prescribing requirements are met.
  6. Each prescription reflects the real date it was written and signed, rather than a falsely future-dated written/signature date.

These are separate prescriptions, not a refill order, a 90-day expiration or a promise that an outpatient must be seen exactly every 90 days. Under §1306.12(b)(2), the rule neither requires nor encourages issuing multiple prescriptions. Clinicians make follow-up decisions based on medical judgment and applicable standards.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A physician writes three separate 30-day oxycodone prescriptions on one visit, the second marked Do Not Fill Before the beginning of month two and the third month three. If the other requirements are satisfied, federal law recognizes the sequential-issue process rather than treating the later orders as refills.

MPJE exam trap: One 90-day CII prescription and three separate 30-day CII prescriptions are different legal mechanisms. Multiple original prescriptions still need real signatures/dates and restrictions.

Official references: 21 CFR 1306.12 — no refills; multiple CII prescriptions · 21 CFR 1306.04 — legitimate medical purpose and corresponding responsibility · MA Joint Policy 2007-01 — sequential CII orders and Do Not Fill Before · M.G.L. c.94C §23 — CII validity, refills, supply and endorsement

6. Massachusetts sequential CII example: why '30 days from date written' is wrong

Original Korean unit: w2-b-3 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

Current Massachusetts interpretation: The written 2007-01 Joint Policy treats a valid later Do Not Fill Before date as the §23 Date of Issuance for sequential CII validity. The December 2025 Board newsletter explicitly repeats the same operational conclusion. This is a meaningful exception to reading the 30-day statutory limit by looking only at the date the prescriber physically wrote the prescriptions.

Example prescriptionActual date writtenDo Not Fill BeforeMA validity assessment
First CII RxNov 9, 2026None30-day statutory window from date written
Second CII RxNov 9, 2026Dec 9, 2026May be filled from valid later fill date; 30-day window uses Dec 9 as applicable
Third CII RxNov 9, 2026Jan 8, 2027May be filled from valid later fill date; 30-day window uses Jan 8 as applicable

The example assumes all federal multiple-prescription, professional practice, schedule-specific and state requirements are met. This does not change the fact that the doctor signed all three orders on November 9, 2026; it also does not let the pharmacist move the earliest date at will. Don't confuse CII sequential-prescription validity with MA's more restrictive initial-fill deadlines for certain out-of-state prescriptions addressed in the next chapter.

Source date and hierarchy: §23 is the statute, the 2007 Joint Policy interprets it in the special sequential context, and the Board's December 2025 newsletter confirms the interpretation. The 2024 quantity policy addresses a separate single-fill days' supply issue. Neither replaces the other.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A compliant third 30-day methylphenidate prescription was actually signed November 9 but clearly says Do Not Fill Before January 8. Do not reject it automatically on December 10 solely because November 9 was more than 30 days earlier; use the applicable later date and other validity checks.

MPJE exam trap: Do Not Fill Before date ≠ future-dated signature date. Board's 2025 guidance confirms the later-date clock for qualified multiple CII prescriptions.

Official references: MA Joint Policy 2007-01 — sequential CII orders and Do Not Fill Before · Massachusetts Board of Pharmacy December 2025 newsletter, p. 1 · 21 CFR 1306.12 — no refills; multiple CII prescriptions · M.G.L. c.94C §23 — CII validity, refills, supply and endorsement

7. What counts as a genuine emergency oral Schedule II prescription?

Original Korean unit: w2-b-4 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

21 CFR 290.10 defines an emergency for oral CII authorization. The prescribing practitioner must determine all three: (1) immediate administration of the CII drug is necessary for proper patient treatment; (2) no appropriate alternative therapy is available, including suitable non-CII drugs; and (3) it is not reasonably possible to provide the required signed prescription before dispensing.

21 CFR 1306.11(d) then permits the pharmacist to receive oral authorization from the prescribing individual practitioner. The pharmacist must immediately record the prescription information required by §1306.05 except the prescriber's signature, verify an unfamiliar practitioner in a reasonable good-faith manner, and limit quantity to what is adequate for the emergency period.

Do not invent a fixed emergency supply duration: federal law does not say every emergency CII must be exactly three days or seven days. The seven-day written follow-up deadline is a separate rule from the supply adequate for that patient's emergency. Massachusetts eRx temporary technology failure can allow a valid paper format under specified circumstances; a simple computer outage alone does not prove the three conditions for a telephone CII emergency.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A patient requires immediate opioid treatment, a non-CII alternative would not be appropriate, and the prescriber cannot reasonably deliver a signed order before dispensing. With appropriate authorization and documentation the pharmacist may dispense only the emergency-period amount, not automatically an unrestricted 30-day supply.

MPJE exam trap: Need + no alternative + cannot present signed prescription. 'Office closed' or 'patient prefers phone' alone is not a legally defined emergency.

Official references: 21 CFR 290.10 — federal three-part emergency definition · 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII written, oral emergency and fax-original exceptions · 21 CFR 1306.05 — content and signing of controlled prescriptions · 105 CMR 721.060 and 721.070 — MA emergency and e-prescribing exceptions

8. Emergency CII follow-up: federal seven days, no business-day substitution

Original Korean unit: w2-b-4 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

For an authorized emergency oral CII, 21 CFR 1306.11(d)(4) requires the prescribing practitioner to cause a signed written or compliant electronic follow-up for the same emergency quantity to be delivered within seven days after the oral authorization. The paper form must bear “Authorization for Emergency Dispensing” and the oral order's date; if mailed, it must be postmarked within the seven-day period. The pharmacist attaches or cross-references the follow-up to the oral record (electronically, where appropriate).

Missing follow-up: the pharmacist must notify the nearest DEA office if the prescribing individual practitioner fails to provide the required follow-up. Merely putting “not received” in a local record does not complete this duty. The follow-up documents the original emergency order; it must not be dispensed again as new additional tablets.

Federal seven days means calendar days, not seven business days. Massachusetts 105 CMR 721.060(B)(4) uses a seven-business-day formulation but expressly requires compliance with federal law, including any shorter federal period. Separately, the wording of M.G.L. c.94C §20(c) states an ordinary oral-to-electronic two-day follow-up rule, with a qualifying exception for written follow-up within seven days or a shorter federal period. Its precise interface with the 2024 regulation is not fully resolved by the text alone; do not teach the state 'seven business days' wording as if it universally cancels any shorter applicable statutory duty.

Central-fill restriction: 21 CFR 1306.11(d)(5) does not permit a central-fill pharmacy to prepare a CII on emergency oral authorization received from an individual practitioner or retail pharmacist. This is an explicit limit distinct from the ordinary retail emergency process.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: The prescribing physician phones an emergency CII order Monday afternoon. The pharmacist documents the order and tracks the federally required follow-up within seven days of the telephone authorization, not seven business days from when the patient later collects the medication; no second dispensing occurs on receiving the document.

MPJE exam trap: Seven days is the federal CII follow-up deadline, not the emergency days' supply. Missing follow-up → notify DEA. Central fill is excluded.

Official references: 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII written, oral emergency and fax-original exceptions · 105 CMR 721.060 and 721.070 — MA emergency and e-prescribing exceptions · M.G.L. c.94C §20(c) — oral controlled prescriptions and follow-up

9. The three narrow CII fax-as-original exceptions

Original Korean unit: w2-b-5 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

Ordinarily, a fax of a paper CII lets the pharmacy prepare it, but the pharmacist needs the original manually signed paper prescription before actual dispensing (21 CFR 1306.11(a)). Three defined exceptions let the fax itself function as the original:

Federal sectionWhen fax can be the originalWhat to watch
§1306.11(e)Schedule II narcotic prescription for a drug to be compounded for direct administration by parenteral, IV, IM, SC or intraspinal infusionNot every injectable CII; compounded/direct-administration infusion requirements matter
§1306.11(f)Any qualified Schedule II prescription for a resident of a Long-Term Care Facility (LTCF)Not limited to narcotic drugs; true LTCF patient and authorized transmission required
§1306.11(g)Schedule II narcotic for a patient in a Medicare-certified/Medicare-paid or state-licensed hospice programPractitioner or agent must note that the person is a hospice patient

The hospice route is narcotic-only, whereas the LTCF exception covers Schedule II generally when the other conditions are met. An ordinary outpatient's faxed oxycodone is not converted into an original merely because the patient is elderly, disabled, lives at home or has difficult transportation.

These exceptions address how the original is transmitted. They do not cancel CII no-refill rules, professional legitimacy, state issuance clocks, identification, partial-fill requirements or other laws.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A hospice patient has an appropriately annotated fax for a Schedule II narcotic from a qualifying hospice program: under §1306.11(g), the fax may be the original. A nonhospice outpatient's routine fax for an identical medication generally still needs a signed original.

MPJE exam trap: Fax original mnemonic: INFUSION = compounded CII narcotic; LTCF = any CII; HOSPICE = CII narcotic + hospice notation.

Official references: 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII written, oral emergency and fax-original exceptions · 21 CFR 1306.05 — content and signing of controlled prescriptions · 105 CMR 721.060 and 721.070 — MA emergency and e-prescribing exceptions

10. Putting it together: EPCS, pharmacy handoffs, and the partial-fill boundary

Original Korean unit: w2-b-5 · Official-law review: November 9, 2026

First decide if the order is genuine EPCS, a manually signed paper original, a permissible oral emergency or a permitted fax-as-original. Entering a fax image into a computer does not transform the original into EPCS. Massachusetts e-prescribing exceptions do not waive the federal CII limits. A pharmacist should validate each applicable rule at the time of dispensing, not just when the order arrives.

Do not confuse refill and partial filling. A refill dispenses a new authorized repeat of the order and is never allowed for CII. A partial fill supplies part of the quantity on the same valid original; federal 21 CFR 1306.13 and Massachusetts M.G.L. c.94C §18 impose multiple pathways with distinct time limits (such as stock shortage versus patient-requested versus LTCF/terminal illness). These are handled in the next English chapter rather than assuming all remainders are automatically lawful.

Also separate out-of-state prescription validity from the MA-prescriber 30-day rule: Massachusetts has distinct nonresident prescriber acceptance and timing limits under §18, which the Board's December 2025 newsletter also discusses. These requirements are addressed in the next chapter. Keep documentation showing which original, partial-fill pathway and dispensing date actually apply.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A prescriber sends a standard faxed oxycodone order for an ordinary outpatient. The pharmacist may stage the prescription but cannot dispense before receiving a proper original, absent another specific legal route. If the patient later requests half of a valid original, the pharmacist applies the separate partial-fill requirements rather than treating it as a refill.

MPJE exam trap: Ordinary fax ≠ original CII. CII partial filling ≠ refill. Nonresident CII validity ≠ blanket MA-prescriber 30-day clock.

Official references: 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII written, oral emergency and fax-original exceptions · 21 CFR 1306.13 — CII partial filling (separate study chapter) · M.G.L. c.94C §18 — nonresident prescriber and partial-fill distinctions · M.G.L. c.94C §23 — CII validity, refills, supply and endorsement · Massachusetts Board of Pharmacy December 2025 newsletter, p. 1 · 247 CMR 9.00 — Massachusetts pharmacist professional standards

Memory aids

CII = No Refill. 30 is a validity clock in ordinary MA cases; a properly written Do Not Fill Before may shift the operative 30-day window for sequential originals. 90 is either a qualified per-fill ceiling or an aggregate federal multiple-prescription ceiling—these are not the same mechanism. Emergency 3+7: three tests for a true emergency, then federal seven-day follow-up. Fax I–L–H: qualifying compounded Infusion narcotic, LTCF resident, and Hospice narcotic with notation.

Numerical caution: don't mistake an ordinary pain prescription's 30-day maximum for a first-outpatient opioid's applicable seven-day restriction. Never convert seven calendar days of federal emergency follow-up into seven business days because a Massachusetts regulation has different wording.

Continue to the separate original Korean CII partial fill and out-of-state chapter for pharmacy shortage, patient-requested remainder, LTCF/terminal illness and nonresident prescriber pathways. Their deadlines are not interchangeable with this chapter's ordinary CII original-fill clock.

Question 1. A patient brings a valid, signed Schedule II prescription for oxycodone with '2 refills' printed on it. Which statement is most accurate?
  1. The first fill may be lawful, but CII refills are prohibited despite the printed notation
  2. The pharmacist may dispense three times because the prescriber signed
  3. The first fill is always invalid solely because refills are printed
  4. Schedule II prescriptions are refillable for six months

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Federal §1306.12(a) and MA §23(b) prohibit CII refills.

B: Prescriber notation does not override CII law.

C: The refill notation is invalid; it does not by itself establish that the otherwise valid original cannot be filled.

D: The six-month/five-refill rule concerns other schedules, not CII.

Question 2. Which statement best distinguishes a Massachusetts Schedule II prescription's validity period and days' supply?
  1. Any drug with a 90-day supply may first be filled for 90 days after signing
  2. All CII medicines must be dispensed for exactly 30 days
  3. A qualified non-opioid CII may have up to a 90-day single fill, while the MA prescription has a separate 30-day validity rule
  4. The §23(d) supply exception permits pharmacy staff to increase the quantity without prescriber authorization

Correct answer: C.

A: A supply maximum is not an expiration period.

B: Some categories allow different limits, and a prescriber may prescribe less.

C: Correct. Different clocks and drug categories apply.

D: A maximum does not authorize unilateral quantity increases.

Question 3. A Massachusetts physician signs three 30-day CII prescriptions on November 9, 2026; the third has a compliant 'Do Not Fill Before January 8, 2027' instruction. What is the best validity approach?
  1. The third must be rejected solely because January 8 is over 30 days after November 9
  2. The physician should falsely date the third prescription January 8
  3. The third can be filled at any time before January 8, including today
  4. Under MA Joint Policy 2007-01 and 2025 Board guidance, the later Do Not Fill Before date can begin the applicable 30-day validity window if all multiple-Rx conditions are met

Correct answer: D.

A: The Board expressly recognizes a later-date validity clock for qualified sequential CII orders.

B: The date written/signed must be the true date.

C: Do Not Fill Before prohibits earlier dispensing.

D: Correct; don't ignore current Board interpretation.

Question 4. Which is a condition for issuing multiple CII prescriptions totaling up to 90 days under 21 CFR 1306.12(b)?
  1. The patient must visit the prescriber only once every 90 days
  2. Later prescriptions have written earliest-fill instructions, and the prescriber determines there is no undue diversion risk
  3. The prescriber may post-date signatures to later months
  4. Every prescription in the sequence is legally a refill of the first

Correct answer: B.

A: Federal law does not mandate a 90-day visit schedule.

B: Correct. Prescriber judgment and state permissibility are also required.

C: The date written/signed must be truthful.

D: They are separate original prescriptions, not refills.

Question 5. A prescriber orders an opioid for an adult's first outpatient use. Which Massachusetts rule is most relevant?
  1. §19D generally limits the order to seven days unless the specified and documented exception applies
  2. All patients must receive a full 30-day supply
  3. The seven-day rule applies only to gabapentin
  4. The rule does not apply to minors

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. §19D sets the adult first-outpatient limit and a separate rule for minors, with exceptions.

B: 30 days is not automatically authorized for this first-use situation.

C: The rule concerns opiates, not gabapentin.

D: For minors, the seven-day rule generally applies to each opioid prescription absent an exception.

Question 6. Which combination correctly describes a federal 'emergency situation' for oral Schedule II prescribing?
  1. The patient is inconvenienced by a closed clinic
  2. The pharmacy stock is low, and the patient asks for quicker pickup
  3. Immediate treatment is necessary, no appropriate alternative exists, and a signed prescription cannot reasonably be presented before dispensing
  4. The prescriber prefers telephone ordering for all opioid patients

Correct answer: C.

A: Inconvenience alone does not meet the three conditions.

B: Low stock is not a prescriber emergency definition.

C: Correct. These are the conjunctive requirements of 21 CFR 290.10.

D: A routine preference is not an emergency.

Question 7. What amount may be dispensed under a valid federal emergency oral Schedule II authorization?
  1. Exactly a seven-day supply in every case
  2. Only the amount adequate to treat the patient during the actual emergency period
  3. An unrestricted 90-day supply whenever state law allows other CII drugs
  4. The number of refills requested by the patient

Correct answer: B.

A: Seven days is the separate follow-up document clock, not universal emergency supply.

B: Correct. The emergency period controls quantity.

C: The general 90-day supply categories do not waive the emergency quantity limit.

D: No CII refills are permissible.

Question 8. A pharmacist receives an authorized emergency oral CII, but the required signed follow-up has not arrived. Which federal rule applies?
  1. The pharmacy may treat the unsigned note as an indefinite valid prescription
  2. The seven-day limit is measured in business days after the patient's first pickup
  3. The follow-up can be dispensed again as an independent additional order
  4. Follow-up is due within seven days from oral authorization; if the practitioner fails to deliver it, the pharmacist must notify the nearest DEA office

Correct answer: D.

A: A compliant follow-up is required.

B: Federal §1306.11(d) says seven days after oral authorization, not business days or pickup.

C: The follow-up documents the original emergency fill; no double dispensing.

D: Correct. The duty to report a missing follow-up is explicit.

Question 9. Which faxed Schedule II prescription can serve as an original under federal law?
  1. Any written CII narcotic for a hospice patient in a qualifying program when correctly identified and annotated
  2. Any faxed oxycodone for an ordinary outpatient
  3. Any CII medication for a patient over age 65
  4. Any fax with an office logo and DEA number

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct if §1306.11(g) hospice conditions are met; LTCF and qualifying compounded infusion have separate exceptions.

B: Ordinary outpatient CII fax is usually not the original.

C: Age alone is not a fax-as-original pathway.

D: A logo and DEA number do not replace the legal original requirement.

Question 10. A central-fill pharmacy receives a telephone Schedule II emergency authorization from a retail pharmacist. May it prepare that CII using the federal emergency-oral pathway?
  1. Yes, all central-fill pharmacies have unlimited emergency oral powers
  2. Yes, if a technician repeats the order twice
  3. No; §1306.11(d)(5) expressly excludes central fill from preparing emergency oral CII orders from retail pharmacists or individual practitioners
  4. Yes, because it is a partial fill

Correct answer: C.

A: The rule provides an express central-fill exclusion.

B: Repeating the order does not change the rule.

C: Correct. Central-fill restrictions differ from ordinary retail pharmacy processing.

D: Partial-fill law does not waive the emergency oral central-fill prohibition.
