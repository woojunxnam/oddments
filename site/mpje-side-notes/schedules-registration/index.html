How to solve any controlled-drug classification question

Identify the generic substance + strength + formulation + indication + applicable legal category first. Then ask whether the prescriber and pharmacy each have the professional authority, state registration, federal registration and location-specific permission required. Last, apply the separate rules for prescription validity, refills, days supplied, ID, PMP and product distribution.

Massachusetts Schedule VI is a state category for prescriptions outside Schedules I–V. It does not exist under the federal CSA. Schedule VI gabapentin has additional monitoring duties. Current federal cannabis classifications are product/license specific after an April 28, 2026 rule, not a blanket “all marijuana is I” or “all marijuana is III” answer.

Verification date: November 9, 2026. The source Korean notes were previously checked October 20–23. This English guide specifically rechecks the federal 2026 cannabis change and identifies a conflicting pair of official Massachusetts DCP address instructions for prescribers who do not purchase or store drugs. Neither uncertain address reading is presented as a universal law.

1. The legal schedule is not the whole dispensing decision

Original Korean unit: w2-a-2 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

Start with four separate questions: (1) What is the drug's legal schedule today? (2) What is the exact formulation, amount, and indication? (3) Does the prescriber or facility have appropriate professional authority and registrations? (4) What dispensing, identification, reporting, refill, and product rules apply? A Schedule II label alone cannot answer all four.

Under M.G.L. c.94C §2(a), Massachusetts adopts Schedules I–V and also has Schedule VI for prescription drugs not included in the first five. Schedule VI is controlled under Massachusetts terminology even if the drug is not federally scheduled. The federal CSA has no Schedule VI.

Federal approval, state scheduling, controlled-substance registration and scope of practice are independent authorizations. A drug being listed under Schedule III does not automatically mean any licensed retail pharmacy may lawfully dispense every formulation or every intended use.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A patient hands over a prescription for gabapentin. It is a Massachusetts Schedule VI medication, but it has extra PMP and identification duties as an Additional Drug. Classifying it as ordinary Schedule VI without checking those duties would be incomplete.

MPJE exam trap: Federal Schedule VI does not exist. Massachusetts Schedule VI does not mean 'no prescription required.'

Official references: M.G.L. c.94C §2: six schedules · Massachusetts DCP: MCSR program overview · Massachusetts DCP: pharmacy reporting and data submission

2. Schedules I and II: which drugs and why indication matters

Original Korean unit: w2-a-2 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

Schedule I: examples under the federal schedule include heroin, LSD, and MDMA. These drugs do not move through the ordinary retail patient-prescription process. Certain research activities may be separately authorized; that is not a community-pharmacy prescription permission. The special 2026 marijuana product categories are discussed in Topic 6, not flattened into the old statement that all marijuana is I.

Schedule II: examples include oxycodone, morphine, hydromorphone, fentanyl, methadone, methylphenidate, amphetamine, and hydrocodone products. Hydrocodone combination products are not automatically Schedule III because they contain acetaminophen: hydrocodone combinations are generally CII. Some non-narcotic controlled stimulants such as methylphenidate are also CII.

In Massachusetts, M.G.L. c.94C §23(a)-(b) makes CII prescriptions invalid 30 days after issuance and prohibits CII refills. Under §23(d), the single-fill supply limitation can be up to 90 days for certain specified indications/categories (including certain non-opioid CII/III agents and OUD treatments), otherwise generally 30 days, unless another law imposes a more restrictive limit. The validity clock and days-supply limit are different.

Methadone: a lawful retail prescription for pain is different from continuing OUD treatment. Methadone for OUD generally follows the licensed opioid treatment program (OTP) route, not an ordinary retail refill from a general office prescriber. Narrow federal exceptions should not be mistaken for routine community dispensing.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A prescriber sends a 90-day methylphenidate prescription for a legitimate attention-deficit disorder indication. Do not automatically reject it solely for exceeding 30 days; first check whether the Massachusetts non-opioid CII exception and all other restrictions apply. Conversely, no CII prescription can simply receive a refill.

MPJE exam trap: CII prescription valid 30 days ≠ a universal 30-day supply maximum. Methadone for pain ≠ methadone maintenance for OUD.

Official references: 21 CFR 1308.11: Schedule I · 21 CFR 1308.12: Schedule II · M.G.L. c.94C §23: validity, supply, refills · SAMHSA: methadone for OUD generally through an OTP

3. Schedule III: buprenorphine, ketamine, testosterone and conditional codeine

Original Korean unit: w2-a-2 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

Federal Schedule III includes buprenorphine, ketamine, testosterone/anabolic steroids, perampanel, and certain FDA-approved products containing gamma-hydroxybutyric acid (GHB). Note that the GHB qualification is specifically for FDA-approved products; do not claim that every illicit GHB preparation is CIII. Testosterone is controlled even though it is not an opioid. Buprenorphine is Schedule III, but its indication, formulation and restricted-distribution requirements still matter.

Codeine combination trap: under 21 CFR 1308.13(e)(1), qualifying CIII codeine combinations may have not more than 1.8 g/100 mL or 90 mg per dosage unit, together with a qualifying additional active ingredient (such as a recognized therapeutic amount of a nonnarcotic ingredient). Codeine strength alone is not enough: the legal combination and composition must fit the specified subparagraph. Codeine by itself is generally CII.

Massachusetts §23(d) distinguishes specified CII/CIII single-fill categories that can allow up to 90 days from the 30-day default for other CII/CIII prescriptions, subject to other applicable restrictions. Do not conclude “any Schedule III prescription can be filled for 90 days.”

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A patient asks whether ketamine is Schedule II because it is a powerful anesthetic. The correct legal classification is Schedule III; potency or hospital use alone does not define a controlled-substance schedule.

MPJE exam trap: Buprenorphine is CIII, not CII; perampanel is CIII; an FDA-approved GHB product is CIII rather than the broad illegal GHB category.

Official references: 21 CFR 1308.13: Schedule III · M.G.L. c.94C §23: validity, supply, refills

4. Schedules IV and V: important lookalike drugs and refill limits

Original Korean unit: w2-a-2 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

Schedule IV examples include alprazolam, lorazepam, clonazepam, diazepam, temazepam, zolpidem, carisoprodol, tramadol, modafinil, phentermine and phenobarbital. Do not treat all C IV substances as benzodiazepines: tramadol is an opioid in CIV, while phenobarbital is a barbiturate. Special PMP consultation requirements that target particular drug types need not apply identically to every drug in the same schedule.

Schedule V includes pregabalin, lacosamide, brivaracetam, cenobamate, lasmiditan and ganaxolone. A qualifying codeine-containing cough product can be CV only if its composition meets the federal rule, including not more than 200 mg codeine per 100 mL/100 g and enough other active nonnarcotic ingredient to confer medical qualities. An eligible diphenoxylate/atropine dosage unit contains at most 2.5 mg diphenoxylate and at least 25 micrograms atropine sulfate; difenoxin has a different threshold.

Refills: federal 21 CFR 1306.22 sets a five-refill/six-month framework for Schedule III and IV. Massachusetts 247 CMR 9.04(13) separately makes a Schedule V prescription unfillable after six months and limits it to five refills. Do not misattribute the MA CV rule to federal III–IV text. A prescriber must have authorized the refills. The specific law governing a question matters.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A pregabalin prescription issued seven months ago lists refills remaining. It is CV; under 247 CMR 9.04(13) the Massachusetts pharmacy cannot fill/refill it after the six-month point just because an unused refill remains.

MPJE exam trap: Tramadol = CIV; pregabalin = CV; gabapentin = MA VI additional drug. The MA CV six-month/five-refill rule has its own source.

Official references: 21 CFR 1308.14: Schedule IV · 21 CFR 1308.15: Schedule V · 247 CMR 9.04: dispensing, refills and ID (Board regulation) · 21 CFR 1306.22: federal Schedule III–IV refill limits

5. Massachusetts Schedule VI, gabapentin and PMP reporting

Original Korean unit: w2-a-2 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

Massachusetts Schedule VI covers prescription medicines that are not in I–V, such as ordinary prescription antibiotics, statins and many antihypertensives. Gabapentin is MA Schedule VI, but DPH additionally designates it a drug of concern / Additional Drug for PMP purposes. The designation does not convert gabapentin into federal Schedule V. In contrast, pregabalin is federal CV.

The Massachusetts PMP requires covered pharmacies to report dispensations of Schedules II–V and gabapentin under 105 CMR 700.012. DCP states these data must be sent to the PMP Clearinghouse within 24 hours or the next business day following the most recent transmission under the data-submission rule. This does not imply every Schedule VI drug is PMP-reportable.

Under 247 CMR 9.04(14), the customer-identifier requirement encompasses Schedules II–V and Additional Drugs such as gabapentin; a documented serious-hardship route and specified waiver/modification circumstances exist. A pharmacist should not automatically turn away a patient without considering the actual permitted exception and documentation requirements.

Naloxone data: DCP encourages naloxone dispensing submissions through the PMP as a data collection process; it says naloxone reporting will not appear on an individual patient's PMP profile. Reporting naloxone does not itself make it an Additional Drug like gabapentin.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A pharmacy dispenses gabapentin 300 mg to an MA patient. Even though it is Schedule VI, the pharmacy still needs to evaluate the Additional Drug ID and PMP reporting requirements; ordinary simvastatin does not automatically share them.

MPJE exam trap: Not all Schedule VI drugs are PMP drugs. Naloxone PMP data ≠ gabapentin drug-of-concern status.

Official references: M.G.L. c.94C §2: six schedules · Massachusetts DCP: pharmacy reporting and data submission · 247 CMR 9.04: dispensing, refills and ID (Board regulation) · 21 CFR 1308.15: Schedule V

6. April 2026 cannabis rescheduling: product-specific, not blanket legalization

Original Korean unit: w2-a-2 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

This is a major 2026 correction to old schedule charts. A DEA final rule effective April 28, 2026 (91 FR 22714) placed particular products containing marijuana in Schedule III: qualifying FDA-approved products and marijuana/products subject to a qualifying state medical marijuana license. The relevant current text appears at 21 CFR 1308.13(g)(2)–(4) for marijuana, marijuana extracts and specified naturally derived delta-9-THC.

Do not overgeneralize. Schedule I still includes marijuana-related substances outside the Schedule III categories where applicable. There is also an ongoing broader federal rescheduling process, separate from this product-specific final rule. Some products or hemp materials may be excluded from CSA marijuana definitions under distinct statutory conditions; do not assume all cannabis-containing items share one legal schedule.

FDA approval and registration still matter: a state medical-marijuana license, a special federal category, ordinary pharmacy licensure, product labeling, and actual DEA authority are different. The 2026 rule did not authorize an ordinary Massachusetts retail pharmacy to handle or dispense every cannabis product, nor did it make marijuana recreationally or medically available as an ordinary Schedule III prescription to all pharmacists. DEA established specific handling/registration arrangements for affected state licensees.

Dronabinol formulation trap: 21 CFR 1308.13(g)(1) specifically lists synthetic dronabinol in sesame oil soft-gelatin capsules in an FDA-approved product as CIII. A different formulation is not automatically covered by that precise exception; check its actual schedule.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A customer says, 'Marijuana moved to Schedule III, so you can dispense any cannabis edible from the pharmacy now.' The pharmacist must reject this reasoning: schedule, state medical licensing, DEA registration, FDA product status and actual dispensing authority must all be verified.

MPJE exam trap: As of November 9, 2026: 'all cannabis Schedule I' is too broad, and 'all cannabis Schedule III' is also wrong. April 28 final rule has a product/licensing boundary.

Official references: DEA final rule (91 FR 22714), effective April 28, 2026 · 21 CFR 1308.13: Schedule III · 21 CFR 1308.11: Schedule I · DEA marijuana rescheduling regulatory actions

7. Professional license vs MCSR vs DEA: three distinct permissions

Original Korean unit: w2-a-3 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

1. Professional license: determines whether someone may practice as a pharmacist, physician, NP, and so on, and what professional scope state law permits. 2. Massachusetts Controlled Substances Registration (MCSR): enables specified Massachusetts controlled-substance activities under M.G.L. c.94C §7 and 105 CMR 700.004. 3. DEA registration: covers federal schedules and the federally permitted activity/locations. One credential does not replace the other two.

A Massachusetts practitioner who performs only Schedule VI prescribing/handling may need an MCSR but not a DEA number. A practitioner authorized to handle Schedules II–V generally needs both appropriate MCSR and DEA registration, within professional scope. The DCP emphasizes that the authorized schedules must match. The ordinary pharmacy's own licensed/registered dispensing role is different from a pharmacist independently prescribing under a protocol.

Example distinction: a pharmacist acting solely as an employee who fills valid prescriptions under a registered retail pharmacy's authority is not necessarily required to obtain a personal DEA registration just to perform that routine work. A pharmacist performing independently registrable activities (for example, if specifically authorized to prescribe) must follow all applicable individual and institutional registration pathways and scope requirements.

Checking a DEA number's format or check digit is never proof that it is currently active, valid for the requested schedule, at the right location or in the prescriber's professional scope. Verify identity and authority with appropriate sources when there is reason for concern.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: An MA pharmacist has an active pharmacy professional license and independently prescribes a qualifying Schedule VI medicine under an applicable state protocol. Their pharmacist license alone does not answer whether an individual MCSR is required; confirm the specific protocol and DCP registration before prescribing.

MPJE exam trap: MCSR without DEA may support permitted MA VI-only activity. MCSR + DEA are normally both needed for independent II–V activity. Credentials do not themselves expand professional scope.

Official references: Massachusetts DCP: MCSR practitioner registration · Massachusetts DCP: MCSR program overview · Massachusetts DCP: apply or renew practitioner MCSR · 105 CMR 700: MCSR registration and Massachusetts controlled substances · 21 CFR 1301.22: employee/agent and institutional exceptions

8. Employee pharmacists, registered pharmacies and institutional exceptions

Original Korean unit: w2-a-3 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

Federal 21 CFR 1301.22 contains specific exemptions for certain agents/employees acting within the employing registrant's authorized activities. The general employee-practitioner exemption can allow administering or dispensing under the registered employer/principal if state law permits; it is not a blanket personal prescribing authorization. A separately specified rule permits certain hospital/institution practitioners to use the institution's DEA number with appropriate unique suffix/conditions.

For exam questions, do not assume every community pharmacy staff pharmacist has their own personal DEA number, or that anyone with a facility's DEA number may prescribe outside their role. A pharmacy entity must have its own applicable registration and licensure. Institutional practitioners must meet all requirements of their specific exemption; a hospital medical resident's suffix is not an ordinary retail employee registration scheme.

Massachusetts MCSR rules also have professional, facility and other activity categories. A pharmacist prescribing under a state protocol should not extrapolate DEA employee-dispensing exemptions to unrelated state MCSR obligations. Separately evaluate prescribing, ordering stock, administering and dispensing rather than grouping all activities together.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A staff pharmacist working in a registered retail pharmacy dispenses a legitimate oxycodone prescription under the pharmacy's registration. A DEA question should focus on the registered dispensing entity and applicable employee exception, not automatically require the employee to have an independent practitioner DEA number.

MPJE exam trap: Employer DEA registration can support specified employee dispensing, but does not automatically authorize independent prescribing or operating another unregistered pharmacy.

Official references: 21 CFR 1301.22: employee/agent and institutional exceptions · 21 CFR 1301.12: DEA location registrations · Massachusetts DCP: MCSR practitioner registration · 105 CMR 700: MCSR registration and Massachusetts controlled substances

9. Separate locations: DEA rule and Massachusetts's nuanced MCSR address guidance

Original Korean unit: w2-a-3 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

Federal 21 CFR 1301.12(a) ordinarily requires separate DEA registrations for each principal place of business/professional practice where controlled substances are manufactured, distributed or dispensed. Two retail pharmacy branches do not share a single DEA registration merely because the same company owns them.

Limited prescriber-office exception: 21 CFR 1301.12(b)(3) may permit a practitioner registered at one location in the same state to prescribe from another office without a separate DEA registration when controlled substances are not stored there and not routinely administered or otherwise directly dispensed. This does not exempt a second drug store that stocks controlled substances. Separate registration can be required when activities/storage change.

Massachusetts nuance: 105 CMR 700.004(F) governs MCSR places/activities, and DCP Circular Letter DCP 23-04-116 states practitioners responsible for purchasing/storing controlled substances need an MCSR at each such location. The circular says practitioners not responsible for purchasing/storing can choose a reachable contact address, including a PO box. However, a separate DCP MCSR practitioner webpage says PO boxes are not accepted for applications. These current official explanations conflict on that narrow address question. Do not memorize a universal PO-box answer; verify the operative DCP application policy for the exact practice category. The underlying principle—more stringent registration when drugs are purchased/stored in multiple locations—is not in conflict.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A physician only writes prescriptions from a second in-state office and keeps no controlled substance inventory there. Check the federal prescribing-only exception; do not apply it to a second pharmacy storefront that stores and dispenses inventory.

MPJE exam trap: DEA registration is location/activity based. Official MA DCP guidance conflicts on PO boxes for no-stock practitioners; avoid a categorical answer until DCP clarifies.

Official references: 21 CFR 1301.12: DEA location registrations · DEA registration FAQ: separate practice locations · 105 CMR 700: MCSR registration and Massachusetts controlled substances · Massachusetts DCP: DCP 23-04-116 and MCSR location/address guidance · Massachusetts DCP: MCSR practitioner registration

10. Validity of registrations, renewal cycles and practical verification

Original Korean unit: w2-a-3 · Legal verification: November 9, 2026

Do not begin an activity merely because you submitted an application. Under 21 CFR 1301.13(a), activities requiring DEA registration must wait until registration is granted and the certificate issued. A DEA registration number may be valid in shape but invalid because it is expired, for another location, a different activity, or for inadequate schedules.

For federal retail pharmacy, practitioner and hospital/clinic registrations, 21 CFR 1301.13(d) generally provides 36-month re-registration cycles; the initial term may be 28–39 months depending on DEA's assigned schedule. Other categories of registrants may have different cycles. Do not assume every Massachusetts MCSR always renews annually; the DCP distinguishes individual practitioners, facilities and different registration paths. A pharmacy license renewal date is also not the DEA renewal date.

Practical verification sequence: confirm the prescriber's professional license/scope, the drug's schedule and specific formulation, the MCSR/DEA status and covered schedules where required, any site/indication-specific conditions, and then the prescription's own validity/refill/supply/ID/PMP rules. Registration authorizes the activity, but it is not the patient's prescription.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: The pharmacy receives a prescription from a prescriber whose DEA number passes the check-digit test. Because the prescriber recently moved states, the pharmacist appropriately verifies current DEA registration and state authority rather than approving it solely on a mathematical check.

MPJE exam trap: DEA format check ≠ active registration. Registration ≠ scope, and renewal/application ≠ permission to practice before the authorization is granted.

Official references: 21 CFR 1301.13: registration issuance and expiration · 21 CFR 1301.12: DEA location registrations · Massachusetts DCP: MCSR practitioner registration · Massachusetts DCP: apply or renew practitioner MCSR · M.G.L. c.94C §23: validity, supply, refills

10 original MPJE-style multiple-choice questions

Question 1. A Massachusetts prescriber has authorization to prescribe only nonscheduled prescription antibiotics. Which registration statement is most accurate?
  1. A federal Schedule VI DEA registration is required
  2. An MCSR may be required for Massachusetts Schedule VI prescribing, but no DEA registration is required solely for that VI-only activity
  3. No professional license is ever necessary for VI medications
  4. Schedule VI means nonprescription drugs only

Correct answer: B.

A: The CSA has no federal Schedule VI.

B: Correct: Massachusetts treats non-I–V prescription medications as VI and DCP describes an MCSR-only path.

C: Professional prescribing authority remains necessary.

D: VI is a category of prescription drugs in MA.

Question 2. A patient asks why hydrocodone/acetaminophen tablets cannot receive a Schedule III refill. What is the best answer?
  1. Hydrocodone combinations are generally Schedule II and CII refills are prohibited
  2. Any combination with acetaminophen is Schedule V
  3. All opioids are Schedule IV
  4. Hydrocodone is an uncontrolled Schedule VI drug in Massachusetts

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Addition of acetaminophen does not change hydrocodone's generally CII schedule.

B: Not a lawful universal classification.

C: Opioids occur in different schedules; tramadol is CIV.

D: Hydrocodone is federally controlled.

Question 3. Under federal 21 CFR 1308.13, when may a qualifying codeine combination fall into Schedule III?
  1. Whenever it contains any amount of codeine, with no other conditions
  2. Whenever it contains less than 200 mg codeine per 100 mL even without another active ingredient
  3. Only if the patient requests the lower schedule
  4. When it meets the specified codeine limit (up to 1.8 g/100 mL or 90 mg/dosage unit) AND the legally specified additional-ingredient requirements

Correct answer: D.

A: Composition and quantity matter.

B: This is not the CIII test, and the other-ingredient condition still matters.

C: A patient's request never changes a legal schedule.

D: Correct. Both quantitative and formulation criteria are required.

Question 4. A Massachusetts pharmacy receives a refill request for pregabalin 7 months after the original prescription was issued. Three refills are still listed. What is correct?
  1. Refill it because unused refills always override the date
  2. Refill it because CV prescriptions have no Massachusetts expiration rule
  3. Do not fill/refill; 247 CMR 9.04(13) limits Schedule V to six months and no more than five refills
  4. Apply the CII thirty-day issuance rule instead

Correct answer: C.

A: Remaining refills do not extend validity indefinitely.

B: Massachusetts specifically limits Schedule V.

C: Correct. The six-month and five-refill limits are from 247 CMR 9.04(13).

D: Pregabalin is CV, not CII.

Question 5. Which statement about gabapentin and pregabalin is correct in Massachusetts?
  1. Gabapentin is MA Schedule VI with Additional Drug/PMP duties, while pregabalin is federal Schedule V
  2. Gabapentin and pregabalin are both federal Schedule II
  3. Gabapentin becomes federal Schedule V solely because Massachusetts reports it to PMP
  4. All Schedule VI drugs must be reported as PMP Additional Drugs

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. MA's additional-drug reporting does not alter federal scheduling.

B: Neither is CII.

C: State PMP designation does not change federal scheduling.

D: The Additional Drug designation is not for all Schedule VI drugs.

Question 6. As of November 9, 2026, what does the federal April 2026 marijuana rule establish?
  1. All marijuana products became ordinary retail Schedule III prescriptions
  2. All cannabis in the United States remained Schedule I with no exceptions
  3. All cannabis products are completely unscheduled
  4. Particular FDA-approved products and products subject to qualifying state medical-marijuana licenses fall under the Schedule III provisions, without granting all pharmacies blanket dispensing authority

Correct answer: D.

A: The rule is limited to specified products/licenses and does not waive other requirements.

B: The 2026 final rule created Schedule III categories.

C: Some cannabis remains scheduled and federal and state controls still apply.

D: Correct. Product-specific legal classification is different from pharmacy authority.

Question 7. A prescriber wants to write Schedule II–V prescriptions in Massachusetts. Which general authority combination is required?
  1. Only an MCSR, with no professional license
  2. Only a DEA number, regardless of Massachusetts registration
  3. Appropriate professional authority plus matching Massachusetts MCSR and DEA registration for the relevant schedules
  4. An employer's pharmacy license automatically grants any employee all prescribing powers

Correct answer: C.

A: An MCSR alone does not replace federal DEA for II–V.

B: Federal registration alone does not replace MA state authority.

C: Correct. Registration and professional scope remain distinct.

D: A pharmacy license is not an unrestricted independent prescriber license.

Question 8. A retail chain opens a second pharmacy storing and dispensing Schedule II drugs. May it simply use the first store's DEA registration?
  1. Yes, any same-chain site may share one DEA registration
  2. No, a separately registered principal dispensing location is generally required
  3. Yes, if both pharmacies use the same pharmacist manager
  4. Yes, under the prescriber-only, no-stock office exception

Correct answer: B.

A: Common ownership does not override 21 CFR 1301.12.

B: Correct. A second retail inventory/dispensing site is not a prescriber-only office.

C: A shared manager does not exempt the site.

D: This exception does not cover a second pharmacy with stock.

Question 9. Which statement is most accurate about a pharmacist employed by a registered pharmacy?
  1. Every employee pharmacist must have an individual DEA registration just to dispense under the store's registration
  2. Any employee pharmacist can independently prescribe all Schedule II medications using the store's DEA number
  3. A written MCSR application alone is enough for independent prescribing
  4. Specified employee/agent exemptions may permit dispensing under an employer's registration, but do not automatically grant independent prescribing powers

Correct answer: D.

A: Individual DEA registration is not an automatic employee dispensing prerequisite in every setting.

B: Registration does not expand professional scope.

C: An application alone is not an issued registration.

D: Correct. Apply the specific 21 CFR 1301.22 provisions and state scope/registration.

Question 10. A prescriber has applied for DEA registration and presents a valid-looking number. What should the pharmacist remember?
  1. DEA-required activities cannot begin until registration is granted; number format alone does not prove current authorization
  2. Any plausible check digit proves current federal and state licenses
  3. The first DEA registration always expires exactly 12 months after issuance
  4. A valid DEA number automatically authorizes dispensing from any site in any state

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. 21 CFR 1301.13 requires issuance before the activity; location, scope and schedules also matter.

B: Check digits do not verify active status or authority.

C: Initial pharmacy/practitioner/hospital terms may be 28–39 months.

D: Separate state and place-of-business requirements may apply.
