REMS is drug-specific, and the effective date matters

To answer a pharmacy-law question, identify the exact product and formulation, the current FDA REMS version and effective date, required certifications/clinical checks, whether a pharmacy can dispense directly to the patient, and what must be documented. An older patient guide or retired registry is not a current dispensing authorization.

Current date: November 9, 2026. Clozapine REMS ended June 13, 2025, but ANC monitoring remains clinically important. FDA-approved modifications to isotretinoin iPLEDGE are scheduled to begin November 15, 2026, after this guide's review date; do not use them early. Always recheck REMS@FDA when actually dispensing.

1. Understand REMS, ETASU and the exact dispensing gate

Part: What a REMS Is · Original Korean note: w3-b-8 · Verified November 9, 2026

REMS stands for Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy. Under federal law (FD&C Act §505-1), FDA can require extra steps for medicines with important safety risks so their benefits outweigh their harms. REMS is not just a warning or a package insert. Some programs require a Medication Guide, clinician communication, Elements to Assure Safe Use (ETASU), a system to implement/verify those steps, and scheduled assessments.

ETASU may be very different by medicine: certified prescribers or pharmacies, enrolled patients, lab tests, signed agreements, dispensing authorization, limits to certain healthcare settings, or direct-to-provider distribution. Do not assume a single REMS enrollment, test, refill or 7-day window applies to every medicine.

Use this exam/workflow checklist: (1) identify the exact brand/formulation and indication; (2) check whether REMS is still active and the effective version; (3) determine the required prescriber, pharmacy, patient or healthcare-setting status; (4) complete the program's documentation, safety checks and prescription authorization; (5) dispense or supply only through the program's approved pathway. FDA REMS@FDA is the starting reference.

A boxed warning and FDA labeling risks can exist without an active REMS. Conversely, the presence of a Medication Guide does not by itself prove a pharmacy certification requirement.

FDA safety toolMeaningDo not assume
Boxed warningSerious risk highlighted in FDA labelingIt proves an active REMS or enrollment requirement
Medication GuideSpecific FDA-approved patient informationGeneric printed CMI replaces the Guide
REMS with ETASUAdditional safety/use conditions for specified productsAll programs require the same lab or registry
REMS discontinuedProgram-level conditions removedThe clinical risk or labeled monitoring vanished

Real-world example: A physician prescribes an unfamiliar specialty drug. It has a boxed warning, but the pharmacy has not checked current FDA REMS status. The pharmacist must verify whether actual special access/certification applies rather than assume every boxed warning means restricted distribution.

MPJE exam trap: REMS ≠ boxed warning ≠ Medication Guide. Check actual FDA status/date and each product's authorized dispensing path.

Official references: overview · basics · db · faq · medguide

2. Clozapine REMS removed June 13, 2025; ANC monitoring remains

Part: Removed REMS and Remaining Risks · Original Korean note: w3-b-9 · Verified November 9, 2026

FDA formally removed the Clozapine REMS effective June 13, 2025, after announcing temporary enforcement discretion earlier in February 2025. A pharmacist dispensing clozapine in November 2026 should not demand legacy REMS pharmacy enrollment, prescriber/patient registry enrollment, an ANC value uploaded into REMS or a clozapine REMS dispense authorization.

Severe neutropenia is still a real, potentially fatal risk. Clozapine FDA labeling retains a boxed warning/Medication Guide, and FDA recommends that clinicians continue ANC monitoring consistent with current prescribing information. General professional DUR, counseling, prescription legitimacy and patient-specific safety judgment still apply.

The historical timeline matters: February 24, 2025 was the beginning of FDA nonenforcement expectations; June 13, 2025 was the effective elimination of the REMS. Neither date means that clozapine itself was withdrawn or that laboratory safety evaluation no longer matters.

Before REMS eliminationAs of November 9, 2026
REMS pharmacy / prescriber / patient registrationNot required to dispense clozapine under eliminated REMS
Submission of ANC to REMSNot required
ANC clinical monitoringContinue according to current labeling and appropriate clinical care
Drug labeling and counselingStill applicable; risk has not disappeared

Real-world example: A patient needs a timely clozapine refill. The pharmacist does not refuse simply because the retired registry has no current ANC submission. The pharmacist still reviews relevant risks, applicable labeling and appropriate clinical monitoring.

MPJE exam trap: REMS removal ≠ stopping ANC clinical monitoring. The earlier no-enforcement announcement and final REMS removal have different dates.

Official references: clozapine · faq · medguide

3. Mifepristone REMS: certified prescriber, pharmacy and lawful shipping

Part: Certified Pharmacies and Access · Original Korean note: w3-b-11 · Verified November 9, 2026

The FDA Mifepristone REMS applies to the approved mifepristone products for medical termination of an intrauterine pregnancy through 70 days (10 weeks) gestation under the labeled regimen. This is separate from emergency contraception and from an unrelated mifepristone indication/formulation. Certified prescribers are required; the patient and prescriber complete the relevant Patient Agreement Form.

Mifepristone can be supplied by or under the supervision of a certified prescriber or by a certified pharmacy on a valid prescription from a certified prescriber. Pharmacy certification has an authorized representative and Pharmacy Agreement, processes to receive Prescriber Agreement Forms by email/fax and a shipping service with tracking information. In-person-only dispensing is not a current universal REMS requirement; appropriate certified-pharmacy delivery by mail is possible.

Massachusetts Board Policy 2023-05 separately requires pharmacy access to family-planning medicines, including mifepristone/misoprostol, with continuous sufficient supply or timely lawful means of access if not in stock. That state obligation does not waive federal REMS certification, prescriber requirements or product labeling. A pharmacy that cannot directly dispense still should support timely lawful alternatives under state guidance.

REMS elementWho or what is responsible
Prescriber certificationQualified provider approved under the Mifepristone REMS
Patient agreementSigned agreement/education as required by current program
Pharmacy certificationCertified location and authorized representative, not any ordinary pharmacy
DeliveryPermitted when REMS-certified process and required tracking/record standards met
Massachusetts access policyTimely lawful access in addition to federal safety requirements

Real-world example: An MA pharmacy receives a prescription for approved mifepristone from a certified prescriber. The pharmacist checks whether the pharmacy is certified under the REMS or arranges a lawful access option; the drug is not simply dispensed OTC, and the patient need not universally appear in person to obtain it from a certified mail pharmacy.

MPJE exam trap: Federal REMS and MA family-planning access duties both apply. Current Mifepristone REMS permits properly certified mail dispensing; ordinary OTC sale does not.

Official references: mife · mifeOverview · ma2023

4. Isotretinoin iPLEDGE: November 15, 2026 change is future as of today

Part: Active and Future REMS Changes · Original Korean note: ipledge-transition · Verified November 9, 2026

Isotretinoin for severe acne is in the shared iPLEDGE REMS to prevent fetal exposure and severe birth defects. Prescriber, pharmacy and patient eligibility/registration, patient-category-specific counseling and current authorization/window checks remain important. The rules are not identical for patients who can and cannot become pregnant.

FDA approved substantial modifications on February 9, 2026, but on June 16, 2026 delayed their planned implementation to November 15, 2026. As of November 9, 2026 the overall modification is NOT yet implemented. FDA's separate enforcement discretion for certain pregnancy testing, dating back to October 2023, should not be mistaken for the whole revised program becoming effective.

Planned changes include permitting prescriber-approved at-home pregnancy tests during/after treatment, while pretreatment pregnancy tests stay in medical settings; eliminating certain repeat-test waiting after a missed seven-day window; reducing monthly counseling documentation for people who cannot get pregnant; and removing the prescription window for that category. Removal of a window is not removal of any product-specific maximum supply limit.

On and after the planned implementation date, the pharmacy must check whether rollout actually occurred and use the currently operative iPLEDGE authorization screens and materials—not this dated study guide for real dispensing.

Rule/statusNovember 9, 2026 answer
iPLEDGE active?Yes for isotretinoin products
Feb 9 approvalApproval of revised REMS, not same-day implementation
Nov 15, 2026Planned future implementation as of verification date
Pretreatment pregnancy testStill performed in medical setting
Seven-day windowCurrent patient-category rules apply; new missed-window change planned
Window vs supplyRemoving prescription window ≠ eliminating maximum supply

Real-world example: On November 9 an isotretinoin patient brings a prescription and asks the pharmacist to apply the reduced November 15 requirements early. The pharmacist must use current effective iPLEDGE authorization and patient-category rules rather than a future change simply because FDA has already approved it.

MPJE exam trap: FDA approved Feb 9 ≠ implemented Nov 9. The stated new go-live date is Nov 15, 2026. Current pretreatment testing remains in a medical setting.

Official references: ipledge · ipfaq · db

5. Four status types: ERA removals, modifications and Caprelsa

Part: Removed REMS and Remaining Risks · Original Korean note: w5-b-9 · Verified November 9, 2026

FDA may maintain, modify, eliminate or approve for future implementation a REMS. Those are distinct legal states. In April 2025, the agency eliminated the REMS for ambrisentan (Letairis/generics), macitentan-containing products (Opsumit, Opsynvi) and aprocitentan (Tryvio). The current REMS enrollment/dispense steps for these programs are gone, but embryofetal risks and label warnings remain.

FDA modified rather than eliminated the REMS for bosentan (Tracleer/generics) and sparsentan (Filspari) in fall 2025. REMS restrictions specifically related to embryo-fetal toxicity were removed, but hepatotoxicity-related REMS protections remain; these are not the same as completely unrestricted prescribing.

FDA removed the Caprelsa (vandetanib) REMS September 25, 2025. Important QT prolongation and medication monitoring still depend on prescribing information. Do not apply the removal status of one endothelin antagonist to every other medicine in the same broad category.

Product familyVerified 2026 REMS statusResidual safety issue
ClozapineRemoved June 13, 2025ANC/neutropenia monitoring
Ambrisentan; macitentan/Opsumit/Opsynvi; aprocitentanREMS removed April 2025Pregnancy/fetal-risk labeling
Bosentan; sparsentanREMS modified fall 2025Liver-toxicity controls remain
Vandetanib (Caprelsa)Removed Sept 25, 2025Cardiac QT warning and clinical care
Isotretinoin (iPLEDGE)Active; changes scheduled Nov 15, 2026Embryo-fetal risk and required workflow

Real-world example: A pharmacy carries bosentan and hears that 'ERA REMS are gone.' Before dispensing, the pharmacist verifies that bosentan's remaining hepatotoxicity REMS still applies, unlike fully eliminated ambrisentan REMS.

MPJE exam trap: Same class ≠ same current REMS status. ERA programs can be removed or only partially modified; labeling risks remain.

Official references: era · caprelsa · clozapine · ipledge

6. TIRF fentanyl: discontinued production does not terminate REMS

Part: Special Controlled-Substance REMS · Original Korean note: w5-b-11 · Verified November 9, 2026

TIRF means transmucosal immediate-release fentanyl, including historical products such as Actiq, Fentora, Lazanda and Subsys. The FDA stated that the manufacturers planned to discontinue production September 30, 2024, and the TIRF REMS stopped accepting new prescriber, pharmacy and patient enrollments. FDA also stated that existing eligible patients may continue under REMS while supply is available.

Crucially FDA said the TIRF REMS remains in place while product NDAs/ANDAs remain approved, regardless of whether the products are being commercially manufactured. Therefore stopped production, no new enrollment, and FDA termination of the REMS are not synonyms.

TIRF access had restricted outpatient prescriber/pharmacy certification and opioid-tolerance safeguards; specific current patient, supply, setting and program authorization must be verified. Fentanyl transdermal patches are not TIRF, and generic name 'fentanyl' alone never determines the product route or REMS.

EventProper conclusion
TIRF manufacturing stopsDo not assume ordinary new stock is available
New TIRF REMS enrollment stopsNew participants cannot simply enroll as before
Existing TIRF REMS continuesWhile relevant approved applications remain as described by FDA
Fentanyl patch prescriptionDifferent dosage form; not a TIRF transmucosal product

Real-world example: An old exam handout says the TIRF REMS was abolished when fentanyl lozenge manufacturing stopped. That is wrong: FDA explicitly distinguished discontinued production from the continuing REMS for remaining approved applications.

MPJE exam trap: Manufacturing discontinuation is not REMS elimination. Fentanyl patch ≠ Actiq/Fentora transmucosal formulation.

Official references: tirf · faq

7. Sublocade versus Brixadi: provider-only depot buprenorphine

Part: Special Controlled-Substance REMS · Original Korean note: w5-b-12 · Verified November 9, 2026

Sublocade and Brixadi are FDA-approved extended-release subcutaneous buprenorphine depots for opioid use disorder and are Schedule III controlled products. Each has its own REMS to prevent serious harm or death from intravenous self-administration. Certified pharmacies/healthcare settings that supply the depot are required to provide it directly to a healthcare professional for professional administration—not as a take-home injection to the patient.

Sublocade has product-specific initiation/maintenance instructions and should not be taught as 'all patients need seven stable days' from older labeling. Brixadi weekly and Brixadi monthly are distinct formulations; weekly units may not be combined to make a monthly dosage. Review the current FDA label and REMS when answering product-specific case questions.

Other buprenorphine products are not the same: Butrans patch (pain), Belbuca buccal film (pain), and Suboxone/Zubsolv transmucosal buprenorphine/naloxone combination products have different approvals and distribution rules. Having the common generic ingredient does not mean they all use the Sublocade or Brixadi depot REMS.

This is a restricted dispensing/supply workflow, not a general authority for any retail pharmacist or technician to administer the injection. Check Massachusetts medication-administration authority separately.

DrugRoute and safety gate
SublocadeExtended-release SC injection; REMS-certified supply direct to healthcare provider
Brixadi weekly/monthlyDistinct SC depot formulations; certified provider-only supply
Butrans/BelbucaDifferent buprenorphine pain products; not depot REMS
Suboxone/ZubsolvBuprenorphine/naloxone transmucosal treatment, separate prescribing/dispensing rules

Real-world example: A patient asks a community pharmacy to hand them a Brixadi monthly injection to self-inject at home. The certified pharmacy must not provide it as take-home stock; the REMS requires a healthcare professional to receive/administer it.

MPJE exam trap: CIII does not mean any CIII buprenorphine product is interchangeable. Depot REMS directs drug to the provider, not patient. Brixadi weekly ≠ monthly.

Official references: brixadi · sublocade · db · maLegal

8. Spravato and oxybates: certified setting versus certified pharmacy

Part: Special Controlled-Substance REMS · Original Korean note: w5-b-13 · Verified November 9, 2026

Spravato is esketamine nasal spray (Schedule III) for specified adult depression indications. FDA's Spravato REMS requires certified healthcare settings, certified supplying pharmacies and supervised administration (patient self-administration under direct healthcare professional observation), with at least two hours of healthcare professional monitoring after each dose. In outpatient settings, patient enrollment is required. This is not an unrestricted bottle to take home.

Compounded ketamine preparations do not inherit Spravato's FDA approval or REMS certification simply because they share pharmacologic properties. The program does not make all ketamine or esketamine products interchangeable.

Xyrem (sodium oxybate) and Xywav (calcium/magnesium/potassium/sodium oxybates) use a shared FDA REMS with certified prescriber/patient and central pharmacy safeguards to reduce abuse/misuse/respiratory-depression risks. Lumryz is a distinct extended-release sodium oxybate product and has its own REMS distribution structure. Similar names/active moiety do not make their schedules, formulations, program authorization or dosing instructions interchangeable.

An FDA-approved oxybate drug's specific controlled-substance approval path should not be mistaken for illicit GHB rules or the products' distinctive dosing protocols.

DrugSpecial access and timing
Spravato esketamine nasal CIIICertified healthcare settings + pharmacies; monitored ≥2 hours at treatment session
Compounded ketamineNot automatically FDA-approved Spravato or REMS-compliant
Xyrem/XywavShared REMS and certified central dispensing system
LumryzDifferent extended-release oxybate product and REMS program

Real-world example: A patient presents a prescription for Spravato and asks the pharmacy to let them use it at home. The pharmacy should supply only through the REMS-certified healthcare setting process, rather than ordinary take-home dispensing.

MPJE exam trap: Spravato = supervised setting, two-hour observation. Xyrem/Xywav ≠ Lumryz's program. Drug ingredients alone do not define REMS.

Official references: spravato · spravatoFDA · oxy · lumryz

9. Opioid Analgesic REMS mail-back envelopes and disposal routes

Part: Opioid REMS and Disposal Responsibilities · Original Korean note: w5-b-16 · Verified November 9, 2026

The Opioid Analgesic REMS (OA REMS) focuses on education and safe use of opioid analgesics. FDA approved a modification October 31, 2024, requiring manufacturers of outpatient-dispensed opioid analgesics to make prepaid drug mail-back envelopes available upon request to outpatient pharmacies and other dispensers beginning March 31, 2025.

The crucial actor distinction: the manufacturer's duty to provide on pharmacy request is not the same as a newly imposed federal requirement that every pharmacy hand one envelope to every opioid patient on every fill. Pharmacies may use the program and other lawful take-back pathways; counsel patients on safe disposal, access to a collector and product-specific FDA guidance.

Keep patient unused medication mail-back distinct from a registered pharmacy's controlled-stock return/destruction (21 CFR Part 1317) and a collector receptacle. Dispensing/disposal under REMS does not replace DEA Form 41, shipping, reverse-distributor or controlled-substance accounting requirements when separately applicable.

Who / whatObligation / distinction
OA REMS manufacturerPrepaid mail-back envelopes available on outpatient dispenser request
Outpatient pharmacyCan request/provide envelopes; not automatically mandatory one-per-fill obligation
Patient's unused medicationAuthorized take-back, mail-back or disposal guidance
Pharmacy-owned controlled stockSeparate DEA record, reverse distribution or destruction requirements

Real-world example: A pharmacy offers opioid safety counseling but did not order REMS mail-back envelopes. It should not claim the FDA manufacturer request duty is identical to a law compelling the pharmacy to place one prepaid mailer in every prescription bag.

MPJE exam trap: Identify the manufacturer/request mechanism; patient mail-back is not pharmacy stock destruction.

Official references: opioids · opioidM · disposal

10 original MPJE-style multiple-choice questions

Question 1. A drug has a boxed warning. Which statement about REMS is most accurate?
  1. Every boxed warning automatically creates REMS pharmacy registration
  2. REMS and boxed warning are identical
  3. The pharmacy must look up the product's actual active REMS and its specific dispensing requirements
  4. Every REMS requires a monthly blood test

Correct answer: C.

A: Boxed warnings can exist without REMS.

B: A warning and a program are distinct.

C: Correct. Product/version/participant requirements control.

D: REMS safeguards differ by product.

Question 2. A pharmacy refills clozapine in November 2026. Which statement correctly reflects FDA's June 2025 change?
  1. Clozapine REMS enrollment/ANC upload is no longer required for dispensing, but appropriate ANC clinical monitoring continues
  2. All ANC testing is now prohibited
  3. REMS clozapine authorization is still mandatory
  4. Clozapine was withdrawn from the market

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. FDA eliminated the REMS effective June 13, 2025.

B: The neutropenia risk and clinical monitoring remain.

C: The REMS pharmacy authorization was eliminated.

D: FDA removed a REMS, not the medication itself.

Question 3. A certified pharmacy receives a mifepristone prescription from a certified prescriber. Which current REMS pathway may be used?
  1. Ordinary OTC sale without any prescriber
  2. Every pharmacy may dispense without certification
  3. Mifepristone must always be dispensed face to face
  4. Certified pharmacy dispensing, including qualifying trackable shipping, while satisfying the patient agreement and REMS requirements

Correct answer: D.

A: Mifepristone is not OTC under this route.

B: Pharmacy certification is part of REMS.

C: The in-person-only federal REMS requirement was removed.

D: Correct. The certified mail route is allowed, with required safeguards.

Question 4. On November 9, 2026, what is the status of FDA's February 2026 iPLEDGE modifications?
  1. They became effective February 9
  2. Approved but implementation delayed until November 15, 2026; current iPLEDGE remains operational
  3. Every isotretinoin product is now OTC
  4. REMS has been eliminated for isotretinoin

Correct answer: B.

A: Approval date differs from go-live date.

B: Correct. FDA June 16 notice postponed implementation.

C: Isotretinoin remains under restricted prescription control.

D: iPLEDGE remains active.

Question 5. What can be inferred when FDA eliminates the REMS for ambrisentan but modifies bosentan's REMS?
  1. Both drugs are completely free of warnings
  2. Both require old embryofetal REMS enrollment
  3. Ambrisentan REMS is eliminated; bosentan retains hepatotoxicity-related REMS requirements
  4. Bosentan was removed from US distribution

Correct answer: C.

A: FDA labeling still warns of risk.

B: The embryo-fetal REMS component was removed.

C: Correct. Different statuses for different ERAs.

D: Bosentan REMS was modified rather than the product withdrawn.

Question 6. A pharmacy teaches that stopping TIRF production in September 2024 also ended the TIRF REMS. What is correct?
  1. Production ended, new enrollment stopped, but FDA said REMS remains while relevant NDAs/ANDAs remain approved
  2. Fentanyl patches automatically became TIRF drugs
  3. Every fentanyl product was made OTC
  4. No existing patient can ever continue treatment

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Production, enrollment and REMS existence are distinct.

B: Patches are transdermal, not transmucosal immediate-release.

C: Fentanyl remains prescription controlled.

D: FDA described existing-patient continuation while stock lasts.

Question 7. A patient asks to take a Brixadi extended-release injection home for self-injection. How should a REMS-certified pharmacy respond?
  1. Give it to the patient as a normal take-home Schedule III prescription
  2. Give it to any unlicensed family member
  3. Convert weekly Brixadi units into monthly doses
  4. Supply Brixadi only directly to the healthcare provider for professional administration under its REMS

Correct answer: D.

A: Depot REMS prohibits direct patient dispensing.

B: Unlicensed family transfer is not the REMS pathway.

C: Weekly and monthly formulations are different.

D: Correct. Provider-only supply mitigates IV self-administration risk.

Question 8. What is the REMS setting and observation requirement for Spravato?
  1. Ordinary take-home nasal spray without monitoring
  2. Certified healthcare setting, supervised administration, and at least two hours' monitoring
  3. Pharmacist-only administration by remote phone
  4. No REMS because it is a nasal spray

Correct answer: B.

A: The REMS requires certified care setting.

B: Correct. Supervision and a two-hour observation period are key safeguards.

C: Phone-only supervision is not sufficient.

D: Nasal administration does not remove REMS.

Question 9. Which statement about Xyrem, Xywav and Lumryz is accurate?
  1. All three are the same dosage form and REMS process
  2. Lumryz is a controlled oxybate with no specialized distribution
  3. Xyrem and Xywav share a REMS; Lumryz is a different extended-release product with its own program
  4. Every oxybate can be substituted solely by generic ingredient name

Correct answer: C.

A: Formulations and programs differ.

B: Lumryz has its own REMS.

C: Correct. Match exact product and authorized channel.

D: Same moiety does not confer universal interchangeability.

Question 10. After the 2024 OA REMS change implemented in March 2025, who must make prepaid mail-back envelopes available on request to outpatient pharmacies?
  1. Manufacturers participating in the opioid analgesic REMS
  2. Every patient with any prescription
  3. USPS for every controlled-drug shipment
  4. A DEA agent at pharmacy checkout

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. FDA requires manufacturers to supply requesting dispensers.

B: Patients are not the REMS supplier.

C: USPS does not hold this manufacturer responsibility.

D: The DEA does not issue mailers at pharmacy checkout.
