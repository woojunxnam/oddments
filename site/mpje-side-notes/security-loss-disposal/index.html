Identify the actor and trigger before the deadline

Decide first whether an event concerns DEA-registered stock theft/loss, Massachusetts Board reportable confirmed pharmacy loss, DCP-registered facility loss/tampering, registered controlled stock for return/destruction, or patient-owned medication collected for disposal. Each has distinct rules; one report or transaction does not automatically satisfy another.

2026 law-status guard: Board Policy 2026-01 (adopted March 26, 2026) supersedes Policy 2022-01. DCP 105 CMR 700.008 requires a separate 24-hour report for covered registrants, while the DCP public facility guidance additionally calls for immediate telephone contact and written follow-up. No federal quantity-only shortcut cancels a confirmed reportable theft.

1. DEA theft and significant loss: two reporting clocks

Part: Reporting theft, losses and tampering · Original Korean note: w2-d-9 · Law review November 9, 2026

21 CFR 1301.76(b) requires a practitioner registrant to notify its local DEA Field Division in writing within one business day after discovering theft or a significant loss of any controlled substances. Separately, it must file a complete, accurate electronic DEA Form 106 within 45 days after discovery through DEA’s secure Diversion Control application. Do not wait for a complete internal investigation to begin the initial notice.

RequirementDEA clockKey starting event
Initial written notice to DEA Field Division1 business dayDiscovery of theft/significant loss
Complete electronic Form 10645 daysDiscovery of theft/significant loss
Investigate, document, take protective stepsPromptly; must not delay mandated noticeDiscovery and ongoing investigation

What counts as significant loss? DEA evaluates the lost amount relative to the business, drug type, people with access, recurring patterns, diversion potential, and local trends. There is no universal fixed dollar amount or tablet threshold. Confirmed theft is not treated simply as an insignificant routine count variance. Document the loss, evidence reviewed, initial notice, subsequent investigation, and filed Form 106 separately.

Real-world example: On Monday a pharmacy discovers unexplained missing oxycodone stock and evidence suggesting diversion. It should evaluate the theft/significant-loss trigger promptly and notify DEA under the one-business-day clock as applicable; it cannot wait for the 45-day Form 106 deadline.

MPJE exam trap: One business day is the initial DEA notice; 45 days is for completed DEA Form 106. Neither is a calendar-day substitute for the Massachusetts Board's 7-day or DCP's 24-hour rules.

Official authorities: 21 CFR 1301.76(b) · DEA secure Form 106 filing

2. Massachusetts Board Policy 2026-01: reportable vs. nonreportable events

Part: Reporting theft, losses and tampering · Original Korean note: w2-d-10 · Law review November 9, 2026

Board Policy 2026-01 was adopted March 26, 2026 and supersedes Policy 2022-01. It addresses confirmed loss/theft reporting for resident Board-licensed pharmacies and wholesalers. It is not automatically the correct notification rule for every nonresident pharmacy or DCP-registered healthcare facility.

Confirmed eventReport to MA Board?
Theft, employee pilferage or diversionYes, regardless of quantity or schedule
Significant loss of Schedule II–VYes
Significant loss of Schedule VI required to be PMP-reportedYes
Unconfirmed discrepancy not yet established as a reportable lossDo not report as a confirmed loss under this policy; investigate and document
Insignificant loss after internal investigation, unless otherwise reportableGenerally no Board RLCS; document on site and review adverse trends
Confirmed dispensing error instead of stock theft/lossNot reported under this loss policy; comply with 247 CMR 15 CQI and other applicable obligations

For a reportable confirmed loss/theft, submit the Pharmacy Loss of Controlled Substances Report (RLCS) and DEA Form 106 if applicable to the Board within 7 calendar days. The policy also refers to other DEA, state, and police reporting duties. DEA filing and MA Board filing are not automatically synchronized.

Real-world example: Inventory shows one missing Schedule VI drug that is not PMP-reportable, with no evidence of theft or diversion. This is not automatically a significant Schedule II–V loss. Investigate, document and classify before deciding the Board-policy trigger. If evidence confirms employee theft, the amount/schedule exception no longer protects it from reporting.

MPJE exam trap: The Board uses 7 calendar days and only the event classes defined by Policy 2026-01. A different federal clock of 45 days does not extend the Board deadline. An unconfirmed shortage still requires investigation.

Official authorities: Board Policy 2026-01 · 247 CMR 20.03(7) · 247 CMR 9.21(8)

3. Drug Control Program 24-hour reporting and overlapping duties

Part: Reporting theft, losses and tampering · Original Korean note: w2-d-11 · Law review November 9, 2026

Massachusetts 105 CMR 700.008 directs a covered DCP registrant to report the theft, loss, or suspected tampering of any controlled substance within 24 hours of discovery by the form provided or approved by the Drug Control Program. “Suspected tampering” is a trigger broader than confirmed theft.

Different licenses and facilities have potentially overlapping reporting duties. A Board-licensed retail pharmacy, a DCP-registered health-care facility, and a DEA registrant are not automatically identical types of registrant. Determine the registrations held and the facts of the incident before deciding which agency gets which report.

Authority / actorRelevant triggerReporting timing
DEA practitioner registrantTheft or significant loss under 21 CFR 1301.76(b)Written notice within 1 business day; Form 106 within 45 days
MA Board resident pharmacy/wholesalerConfirmed reportable loss/theft under Policy 2026-01Board RLCS and Form 106 if applicable within 7 calendar days
MA DCP registrantTheft, loss or suspected tampering under 105 CMR 700.008DCP report within 24 hours

Additional DCP public guidance: A Mass.gov page for licensed healthcare facilities asks for immediate telephone notification and a written follow-up within 7 days. That page uses different language from the newer §700.008 formal 24-hour requirement; do not substitute the older website summary for the enacted regulation. Covered facilities should meet the strict time-sensitive DCP requirement, follow their program's current reporting instructions, and separately assess any patient-harm/DHCQ reporting.

Real-world example: A hospital facility finds suspected opioid-vial tampering on Tuesday afternoon. Even before employee theft is proven, the DCP tampering report may be triggered. DEA/Board obligations must be considered separately depending on registration and event classification.

MPJE exam trap: Do not memorize 24 hours, one business day, 7 calendar days, and 45 days as a single deadline. DCP tampering and Board confirmed-loss triggers differ. A DCP report is not automatically a DEA Form 106.

Official authorities: 105 CMR 700.008, current official text · Mass.gov DCP facility instructions · MA Board 2026-01 · DEA 1301.76

4. Returning controlled-stock inventory and reverse distributors

Part: Disposal of registered pharmacy stock · Original Korean note: w2-d-13 · Law review November 9, 2026

Expired, recalled, damaged, or otherwise unusable controlled stock still remains controlled while on hand. The pharmacy continues security, recordkeeping, and, where applicable, perpetual inventory until a valid return, transfer, or destruction actually occurs.

Under 21 CFR 1317.05, a registrant may use an authorized reverse distributor, transfer to an eligible manufacturer/supplier for return or recall, destroy lawfully at its registered location, or request DEA disposal assistance as permitted. The registered recipient, authorized location, actual transfer documents, and Schedule I–II ordering rules must be considered. A patient returning personally dispensed medicine is not the same as a registered stock transaction.

SituationProper legal analysis
Return/recall of CII controlled stock to manufacturer/authorized recipientVerify eligible recipients and required Part 1305 order documentation
Return/recall of CIII–V stockMaintain required regulated transaction records
Expired medicine still sitting in pharmacy quarantineContinue to secure and account for stock; expiration does not make it ordinary trash
Registered reverse distributor receives stock for destructionReverse distributor must destroy/cause destruction within 30 calendar days after its receipt
Reverse distributor receives via carrierDelivery must go to its registered location and be personally received; no unauthorized rerouting

Example: A pharmacy sends expired fentanyl to a DEA-registered reverse distributor. The reverse distributor’s 30-calendar-day destruction clock begins when the distributor receives the stock. This is not a rule requiring every pharmacy to destroy every expired vial within 30 days of the manufacturer's printed expiry.

MPJE exam trap: The 30 days applies to the reverse distributor after receipt for destruction, not to every pharmacy after product expiration. Registrant-stock reverse distribution is different from collection of unknown patient-returned drugs.

Official authorities: 21 CFR 1317.05 · 21 CFR 1317.15(d) · 21 CFR Part 1305 · 247 CMR 9.21(9)–(10)

5. Non-retrievable destruction, DEA Form 41, and witnesses

Part: Disposal of registered pharmacy stock · Original Korean note: w2-d-14 · Law review November 9, 2026

Non-retrievable (21 CFR 1300.05) means the controlled substance has been permanently changed through irreversible methods so it is unavailable/unusable for all practical purposes. Removing a label, altering a stock ledger, or merely flushing an item is not satisfactory proof of lawful destruction.

21 CFR 1317.95 requires two employees of the relevant registrant to perform or observe specified handling/transport and personally witness destruction until non-retrievable, depending on where the destruction occurs. The rule does not always say “two pharmacists.” Follow particular recipient/transport/location requirements rather than applying one witness pattern indiscriminately.

ActivityDocumentation and controls
Registrant on-site destruction under §1317.95(d)Two employee witnesses and an accurate DEA Form 41 record under §1304.21(e)
Registrant causes destruction at permitted unregistered site under §1317.95(c)Two employees, qualified supervision/witness, and applicable Form 41 records
Transfer to another registrant for destructionDocument Part 1317 transfer/transport and applicable distribution/order records; do not automatically treat a shipping invoice as local on-site Form 41
Small leftover from a vial/syringe used for immediate administration at registered locationNarrow §1304.21(e) Form 41 exception; still record administration/wastage appropriately under §1304.22(c)

Example: After administering an injection, the practitioner finds a medically unusable residual in the syringe. The applicable immediate-administration wastage route may avoid a separate Form 41, but the facility still needs a proper administration/wastage entry. It does not cover an opened stock bottle sitting in the dispensary.

MPJE exam trap: Form 41 is a destruction record for the circumstances specified in §1304.21(e), not universal prior-approval paperwork. The two witnesses required by DEA are employees, not categorically two pharmacists. The immediate-administration exception is narrow.

Official authorities: 21 CFR 1300.05 non-retrievable · 21 CFR 1317.90 · 21 CFR 1317.95 · 21 CFR 1304.21(e)

6. Authorized collectors, patient medicines, receptacles and mail-back

Part: Collection of ultimate-user medication · Original Korean note: w2-d-15 · Law review November 9, 2026

Having a regular DEA retail-pharmacy registration does not automatically authorize a pharmacy to receive all controlled-substance waste from the public. Eligible retail pharmacies and other registrants electing to be collectors must modify DEA registration under 21 CFR 1301.51/1317.40. Participation in voluntary take-back collection is not a universal pharmacy duty.

Those allowed to give lawfully possessed Schedule II–V drugs for destruction include the ultimate user, an individual legally entitled to dispose of a deceased user's property, and a qualifying long-term care facility acting under Part 1317. Authorized collectors and law enforcement can accept such drugs under their permitted methods.

MethodPermitted framework
Collection receptacle at an authorized retail pharmacySecured/fixed receptacle, one-way opening, removable compliant inner liner; regularly monitored area
Receptacle at an LTCFOnly authorized retail pharmacy or hospital/clinic with on-site pharmacy may operate it; special §1317.80 conditions
Mail-back programUse approved nondescript, preaddressed, postage-paid, trackable packages from an authorized program
Providing unused approved mail-back packages to patientsA pharmacy may partner with a collector that runs a mail-back program without itself becoming the actual collector
Ordinary patient return to inventoryNo general permission to reintroduce collected controlled medicines as saleable stock

Once an inner liner is removed and sealed, it may not be opened, X-rayed, analyzed, or penetrated. The liner has a permanent unique ID and specified waterproof/tamper-evident/tear-resistant properties. Do not break it open to count tablets or reuse the contents.

Example: A patient asks a community pharmacist whether they can drop unneeded opioids in a regular stock-return box. The pharmacy must determine whether it has an authorized collector program or direct the patient to a lawful collection method; simply having a DEA number is not enough.

MPJE exam trap: Ultimate-user collection, returned patient medication, and registrant-owned inventory have different procedures. Authorized collection requires DEA registration modification, but a pharmacy merely handing out another authorized collector's mail-back envelopes need not automatically modify its own registration.

Official authorities: 21 CFR 1317.30 · 21 CFR 1317.40 · 21 CFR 1317.60 · 21 CFR 1317.75 · 21 CFR 1317.70 · DEA Disposal Q&A

7. Two different three-business-day LTCF clocks

Part: Collection of ultimate-user medication · Original Korean note: w2-d-16 · Law review November 9, 2026

At a qualifying long-term care facility (LTCF), a collection receptacle is operated by an authorized retail pharmacy or hospital/clinic with on-site pharmacy. It is not the same as an informal nursing-home medication bin.

LTCF clockStart pointWhat must occur
3 business days for a resident's discontinued medicationsPermanent discontinuation of use, transfer out, or deathLTCF must dispose by placing the drug in the authorized collection receptacle immediately, but no later than 3 business days
3 business days for a sealed inner linerWhen the inner liner is removed from the receptacleLTCF can temporarily store sealed liner for up to 3 business days in a secure locked cabinet/room pending transfer

Witness rules for liner work: Installation/removal/transfer/storage must be by or under supervision of two authorized collector employees OR one collector employee plus one designated supervisor-level LTCF employee, as specified in §1317.80(c). Not just any two people may do it.

Example: A resident's drug is discontinued on Tuesday. The LTCF must promptly deposit it under the first clock. When the collector removes the full liner on Friday, that second clock begins on Friday, not back on Tuesday. Always consider weekends and recognized business days.

MPJE exam trap: Same number, different event: discontinued-medication disposal clock and post-removal sealed-liner storage clock are separate three-business-day limits.

Official authorities: 21 CFR 1317.80(a)–(e) · Inner liner handling · DEA LTCF Q&A

8. Secured storage, pharmacy alarms, video, and access

Part: Massachusetts pharmacy security · Original Korean note: w4-a-5 · Law review November 9, 2026

247 CMR 9.21 requires applicable Massachusetts pharmacies to store all controlled substances in their secured prescription area. Schedules II–V must be in a securely locked/substantially constructed cabinet or similarly secured space or dispersed among Schedule VI drugs in a way that obstructs theft and diversion. The federal rule in 21 CFR 1301.75 also recognizes appropriate pharmacy dispersion of Schedule II–V.

Massachusetts security ruleRequirement
CII–V cabinets or dispersionSecured area/cabinet or effective deterrent dispersion among Schedule VI
AlarmCentrally monitored system able to detect breach, activated when pharmacy closed
SurveillanceCameras designed to record theft/diversion; 14-day baseline video retention
Known/suspected theft/diversion recordingRetain related recordings at least 2 years
Pharmacy located within other retail storeFloor-to-ceiling separation, locked and separately alarmed while pharmacy closed
Stock deliveriesOrders containing controlled drugs must be delivered directly to pharmacy
Manager of Record and on-duty pharmacistResponsible for pharmacy security and access control

Section 9.21 includes requirements for expired, quarantined and reverse-distribution-pending drugs. Section 9.21(11) exempts nonresident pharmacies from this specific section. Institutional sterile compounding pharmacies' storage/security are governed through the special 105 CMR 700 route described in §9.21(12), not a blanket waiver of controlled substance security.

Real-world example: A pharmacy inside a supermarket closes before the grocery store. It needs the required secure floor-to-ceiling barrier and separately armed alarm; the store remaining open does not satisfy pharmacy security on its own.

MPJE exam trap: Massachusetts does not require every CII to be in a separate DEA vault. The allowed Schedule II–V cabinet/dispersion option is subject to effective security, and the MA 14-day/2-year surveillance retention clocks differ.

Official authorities: Enacted 247 CMR 9.21(1)–(12) · 21 CFR 1301.75 · Mass.gov 247 CMR 9.00

9. Employment access restrictions for controlled substances

Part: Massachusetts pharmacy security · Original Korean note: w6-a-13 · Law review November 9, 2026

Under 21 CFR 1301.76(a), a registrant must not employ as an agent or employee with access to controlled substances someone who has been convicted of a felony related to controlled substances, had a DEA registration application denied, had DEA registration revoked, or surrendered a DEA registration for cause. The term “for cause” has a regulatory definition linked to investigation of a person's controlled-substance handling.

This restriction is narrower than “all historical misdemeanors bar every pharmacy job,” but cannot be disregarded by a hiring manager. Other Massachusetts screening/licensure and federal-program OIG exclusion checks are distinct and may add restrictions. 105 CMR 700.005(B) separately addresses employment screening for controlled-substance access.

Background findingDo not assume
Felony conviction involving controlled substancesPerson may freely have controlled-drug access
Previous DEA registration revokedPharmacist license automatically overrides federal security restriction
Past surrender for causeSame as routine voluntary lapse/expiration
Unrelated minor misdemeanorAutomatically equal to the exact federal categorical bar described in §1301.76(a)
OIG exclusionIdentical legal test to DEA controlled-substance access restriction

Example: An applicant has a past DEA registration surrendered following a controlled-substance diversion investigation. A pharmacy cannot assign unrestricted stock handling simply because the individual's state license is currently active; the DEA access restriction must be assessed.

MPJE exam trap: This is an employee with access to controlled substances regulation, not a universal ban on all employment after any offense. For-cause surrender is different from routine registration expiration.

Official authorities: 21 CFR 1301.76(a) · 105 CMR 700.005(B)

10. Hazardous drugs, RCRA hazardous waste, DEA schedules and sewering

Part: Environmental disposal and cross-topic distinctions · Original Korean note: w5-a-19 · Law review November 9, 2026

The words “hazardous drug” (NIOSH/USP <800>), “hazardous waste pharmaceutical” (EPA/RCRA), “controlled substance” (DEA schedules), and “radioactive waste” (radiation rules) have different statutory purposes. A medication can be one or several of these at once.

ClassificationFocus and consequence
NIOSH hazardous drug / USP <800>Protect workers and environment from exposure when handling drug
RCRA hazardous-waste pharmaceuticalProper segregation, generation, transport/treatment/disposal and EPA/state waste rules
DEA Schedule II–VPrevent diversion, maintain controlled inventory and follow Part 1317 destruction/collection
Radioactive pharmaceutical wasteNuclear/radiation licensing and radioactive-waste controls

EPA sewering ban: Under 40 CFR 266.505, healthcare facilities and reverse distributors may not flush or pour hazardous waste pharmaceuticals into sewers leading to publicly owned treatment works. This ban has applied nationwide since August 21, 2019, including very small quantity generators (VSQGs), regardless of a state's adoption timing for other Subpart P provisions.

Important nuance: 40 CFR 266.506 offers a conditional exemption from some RCRA hazardous-waste requirements for DEA-controlled substances when managed under its specified standards (including no sewering and DEA-compliant disposal). That does not waive the sewer ban, the DEA destruction rules, or separate hazardous-drug exposure controls. Not every NIOSH-listed product is automatically RCRA hazardous waste.

Example: A pharmacy has a discarded DEA-controlled medication also classified as RCRA hazardous waste. A DEA-compliant disposal method must also meet relevant environmental requirements; pouring the medicine down the drain is not made permissible simply because it is recorded as DEA destruction.

MPJE exam trap: Do not infer the RCRA waste code solely from an NIOSH listing or DEA schedule. The hazardous-waste sewer ban includes VSQG healthcare facilities, while 40 CFR 266.506 provides only a conditional RCRA exemption.

Official authorities: EPA pharmaceutical waste FAQs · 40 CFR 266.505 · EPA 2026 state adoption map · USP <800> overview

10 original MPJE-style practice questions

Try each question before revealing its correct answer. Each explanation addresses every choice. These questions are original teaching exercises, not recalled actual exam items.

Question 1. A DEA-registered pharmacy discovers a theft of oxycodone. Which federal reporting sequence is correct?
  1. Complete Form 106 only within 7 calendar days
  2. Notify the DEA Field Division in writing within 1 business day; file complete electronic Form 106 within 45 days of discovery
  3. Wait until the investigation is complete before notifying anyone
  4. Notify DEA only after the biennial inventory

Correct answer: B.

A: The 7-day Board policy is not the federal initial notice rule.

B: Correct. The DEA regulation has two distinct clocks.

C: The initial notice should not wait for the full investigation.

D: Discovery of a theft triggers reporting outside routine inventory cycles.

Question 2. Which event must a resident MA Board-licensed pharmacy report under Policy 2026-01 regardless of amount or schedule?
  1. Any minor discrepancy still unconfirmed after one count
  2. Every confirmed dispensing error as stock theft
  3. Confirmed employee pilferage/diversion or theft
  4. A return of an unused patient medication to an authorized collector

Correct answer: C.

A: Unconfirmed loss is not submitted as confirmed RLCS; investigate.

B: Confirmed dispensing errors fall under other CQI duties, not automatically RLCS.

C: Correct. Theft, pilferage and diversion are reportable regardless of schedule/quantity.

D: Authorized patient take-back is a different activity.

Question 3. Which Massachusetts rule specifically addresses a covered DCP registrant's theft, loss, or suspected tampering report within 24 hours?
  1. 105 CMR 700.008
  2. 247 CMR 9.21's 10-day CII reconciliation
  3. DEA Form 222
  4. 40 CFR 266.505

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. It applies to covered DCP registrants and includes suspected tampering.

B: That is a separate inventory reconciliation rule.

C: Form 222 is for Schedule I–II stock orders.

D: That federal section prohibits sewering hazardous waste pharmaceuticals.

Question 4. When does a DEA reverse distributor's 30-calendar-day destruction deadline begin for stock received for destruction?
  1. On the manufacturer's expiration date
  2. When the original pharmacy ordered the drug
  3. When the pharmacy first discovers damage
  4. When the reverse distributor receives the controlled substance for destruction

Correct answer: D.

A: The manufacturer's expiry is not the start of this rule.

B: Original order is not the clock.

C: Discovery of damage does not begin the reverse-distributor clock.

D: Correct. Section 1317.15(d) ties it to receipt.

Question 5. Which statement about DEA Form 41 for controlled stock destruction is correct?
  1. Every syringe residual after immediate administration requires Form 41
  2. For specified registrant destruction, it records the process and two employee witnesses; a narrow immediate-administration residual exception exists
  3. Two licensed pharmacists must sign for every lawful destruction regardless of situation
  4. Removing a drug label makes it non-retrievable

Correct answer: B.

A: There is a specific exception for an immediate-administration unusable remainder.

B: Correct. The scope and two-employee witness rule are specified in §§1304.21(e) and 1317.95.

C: The federal rule refers to employees, not always two pharmacists.

D: Label removal does not chemically/physically make the drug non-retrievable.

Question 6. A retail pharmacy wants to operate a DEA controlled-substance take-back receptacle. What is needed?
  1. Its ordinary DEA retail registration alone makes it an authorized collector
  2. The pharmacy must receive a new federal manufacturing license
  3. The pharmacy must modify its DEA registration to become an authorized collector and follow collection rules
  4. It can open liners and count returned pills to keep proper records

Correct answer: C.

A: Collector authorization is not automatic.

B: Manufacturing registration is not the relevant collector requirement.

C: Correct. Collector registration modification and compliant equipment/handling are necessary.

D: Sealed liners may not be opened or penetrated.

Question 7. Under 21 CFR 1317.80, which two events have separate three-business-day rules in an LTCF?
  1. Dispensing a prescription and contacting the prescriber
  2. Permanent discontinuation of resident use and removal of a sealed collection liner
  3. Receiving an initial DEA registration and renewing annually
  4. Reporting a theft to DEA and filing Form 106

Correct answer: B.

A: These are not the paired Part 1317.80 deadlines.

B: Correct. One applies to resident discontinuation, the other to sealed liner storage after removal.

C: These do not define the two LTCF clocks.

D: Federal theft reporting uses different timelines.

Question 8. Under applicable Massachusetts 247 CMR 9.21, how long must ordinary pharmacy surveillance videos and videos related to known/suspected diversion be retained?
  1. 14 days ordinarily; 2 years when theft/diversion is known or suspected
  2. 24 hours ordinarily; 7 days after a theft
  3. 2 years ordinarily; 10 years if theft suspected
  4. No video retention needed if the pharmacy has an alarm

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Section 9.21(4) specifies both periods.

B: Those are not MA surveillance requirements.

C: This reverses/misstates the numbers.

D: The alarm does not replace video duties.

Question 9. Which background fact creates a DEA employee controlled-substance-access restriction under 21 CFR 1301.76(a)?
  1. Any speeding ticket
  2. Any pharmacy employee who is not a pharmacist
  3. A person with a felony conviction relating to controlled substances assigned access to controlled substances
  4. A generic expired CPR certificate

Correct answer: C.

A: A traffic citation does not equal the defined restriction.

B: Licensed and qualifying nonpharmacist workers may have authorized duties.

C: Correct. The rule addresses specified felony/DEA-registration history for controlled-access workers.

D: CPR certification is not the DEA controlled access condition.

Question 10. Under 40 CFR 266.505, which statement about a pharmacy's RCRA hazardous waste pharmaceuticals is correct?
  1. Sewering is allowed for very small quantity generators
  2. DEA stock records automatically override EPA disposal requirements
  3. Every NIOSH hazardous drug is always RCRA hazardous waste
  4. Sewering hazardous waste pharmaceuticals is prohibited for covered healthcare facilities, including VSQGs

Correct answer: D.

A: EPA expressly includes VSQGs.

B: DEA compliance does not erase the national sewer ban.

C: NIOSH hazards and RCRA classifications are distinct.

D: Correct. The prohibition applies to covered healthcare facilities of all generator sizes.
