Determine the legal pathway first

Pharmacist-initiated prescriptions under M.G.L. c.94C §19F, emergency contraception via §19A statewide order, naloxone via §19B statewide order, special MassHealth/HSN Opill dispensing, licensed prescriber EPT and ordinary OTC sales have different conditions. They are not interchangeable. Check source date, training, patient eligibility, product and reporting before applying any numerical limit.

Current-source guard: DCP 26-08-125 (Aug 25, 2026) is the latest general pharmacist-prescribing circular; older public ACCESS pages link to prior circulars. The four separate 2026 statewide HIV/STI clinical protocol DOCX files were not fully auditable for this guide—do not invent dosing, laboratory schedules or pharmacist supply limits.

1. DCP 26-08-125: common pharmacist-prescribing requirements

Topic: 2026 Pharmacist Prescribing · Original Korean note: w3-d-12 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Under M.G.L. c.94C §19F and DCP Circular 26-08-125 (August 25, 2026), Massachusetts-licensed pharmacists may choose to prescribe and dispense specified oral hormonal contraceptives, contraceptive patches, HIV prevention drugs, and STI prevention/treatment medications. The 2026 circular rescinded and replaced DCP 25-04-124. It does not mean that every medication or every clinical diagnosis is within the pharmacist's independent scope.

Every eligible pharmacist must obtain an NPI, prescribe only eligible FDA-approved drugs according to product labeling or applicable statewide guidance, counsel about use/risks/adverse effects, provide the patient a written record, dispense as soon as practicable, document the encounter, maintain competence, and refer/advice clinical follow-up or—with consent—notify the PCP.

Narrow MCSR exception: a pharmacist does not need to obtain a separate Massachusetts Controlled Substances Registration under this route when both prescribing and dispensing occur at a single pharmacy location. The rule does not waive unrelated facility registration, pharmacy licensure, or requirements in other prescribing paths. MassHealth limited ordering/referring enrollment is encouraged, not stated here as mandatory.

StepVerify
Authority§19F + current DCP 26-08-125 + product-specific protocol
ProfessionalMA licensed pharmacist, NPI and appropriate training
DrugListed FDA-approved drug, labeled use or current statewide protocol
PatientScreening, counseling, written record and clinical referral or consented PCP notice
RegistrationSame-pharmacy-location prescribing/dispensing MCSR exception only

Real-world example: A pharmacist assesses a patient for oral contraception, writes a prescription in the same pharmacy, and dispenses it there. The circular provides a narrow MCSR exception; it is not a license to prescribe controlled Schedule II medication or automatically send any pharmacist prescription elsewhere.

MPJE exam trap: The newer DCP 26-08-125 governs 2026; older Mass.gov ACCESS pages still reference historical circulars. Do not import CDTM's MCSR/MassHealth language here.

Official references: dcp · law19f

2. Oral and patch contraception: all ages, screening, 12-month supply

Topic: 2026 Pharmacist Prescribing · Original Korean note: w3-d-13 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Eligible pharmacists may prescribe oral hormonal contraceptives and hormonal patches to patients of any age under the current DCP circular, regardless of whether the product is classified OTC or Rx-only. Prior to prescribing, the patient must complete the pharmacy-provided self-screening risk assessment based on current U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria (US MEC). The pharmacist then evaluates risk factors, contraindications and product appropriateness.

The prescription may provide up to 12 months' supply. The pharmacist must not require the patient to schedule an appointment simply for these prescribing/dispensing services. Lack of a recent primary care or reproductive visit does not automatically block valid refills under DCP 26-08-125; nevertheless, the pharmacist still provides appropriate care guidance and documentation.

This special authorization does not cover every contraceptive form. Vaginal rings, implants, injections and IUD placement are not automatically prescribable because another statute requires insurance coverage for those products. Coverage, prescribing, dispensing and physical administration are separate legal acts.

SituationLegal significance
Patient age 16Not automatically excluded by retail CDTM's age-18 rule
Pharmacy risk questionnaireRequired before prescribing, with appropriate clinical evaluation
QuantityPrescription up to 12-month supply
AppointmentCannot demand scheduled appointment as condition of service
Vaginal ring/IUD/implantNot within the circular's oral/patch-only pharmacist prescribing language

Real-world example: A 17-year-old asks for a contraceptive patch without an existing prescription. A qualified pharmacist may use the §19F pathway after risk screening, training and counseling; an 18+ retail CDTM rule is not the governing age limit.

MPJE exam trap: The 12-month prescribing maximum is not a guarantee of 12 months immediate insurance reimbursement, nor blanket authority for all contraception devices.

Official references: dcp · law19f · access

3. Contraceptive training and documentation: do not copy HIV training hours

Topic: 2026 Pharmacist Prescribing · Original Korean note: w3-d-14 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Before prescribing oral/patch contraception, DCP 26-08-125 requires training on contraceptive mechanisms/products, patient eligibility, counseling on proper use/risks/benefits, prevention of STIs, and documentation of patient-care visits. ACPE/IPCE accreditation is recommended in this contraception subsection; completion documentation must be produced upon request.

The contraceptive subsection does not impose a fixed two-hour minimum. The two-hour accredited training requirement in the same circular applies to HIV prevention pharmacist prescribing and cannot simply be copied to contraception.

Record the patient risk-assessment answers and clinical review, product and supply prescribed/dispensed, counseling, written record given to patient and referral/advice or consent-based PCP notification. A routine checkout receipt alone does not document a complete prescribing encounter.

Prescribing areaTraining distinction
Oral/patch contraceptionContent-based required course; ACPE/IPCE recommended
HIV PrEP or PEPAt least 2 hours qualifying accredited training with specified subject matter
STI prevention/treatmentRelevant qualifying accredited training; circular does not assign the HIV two-hour minimum verbatim

Real-world example: A pharmacist has only completed a two-hour medication pharmacology lecture. That alone does not demonstrate completion of the full contraception curriculum specified by DCP.

MPJE exam trap: HIV's two-hour rule ≠ automatic contraception course duration. Distinguish 'required curriculum' and 'recommended accreditation' from a made-up minimum.

Official references: dcp

4. Statewide emergency contraception standing order: LNG and UPA

Topic: Emergency Contraception & ACCESS · Original Korean note: w5-b-2 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Under M.G.L. c.94C §19A, a Massachusetts statewide standing order permits licensed pharmacists to dispense levonorgestrel (Plan B/generics) or ulipristal acetate (ella) emergency contraception (EC) within that order's procedures. EC prevents pregnancy after intercourse; it is not medication abortion. The standing order is a specific prescribing/dispensing instrument, not a free-standing right to prescribe any medication.

The Mass.gov pharmacy FAQ states no additional training is required for a pharmacist to use the statewide EC standing order, though optional education may be useful. Participation in this standing-order service is voluntary for individual pharmacists; that does not eliminate separately applicable pharmacy duties to facilitate access to legally prescribed reproductive-care medications.

A pharmacist must follow the current standing order, evaluate the patient, create a patient-specific prescription, label and record the dispensing, and provide appropriate counseling and referral. An unsigned generic model found online is not a blanket authority for any product or dose.

Emergency contraceptiveStatus
Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg (Plan B and generics)OTC generally; also eligible via MA standing order for Rx/insurance route
Ulipristal acetate (ella)Prescription-only; standing order may support pharmacy dispensing
Medication-abortion drugs (e.g. mifepristone)Different medications/REMS path, not EC

Real-world example: A person requests ella at a participating pharmacy without a physician's individual prescription. The pharmacist may use the actual MA EC standing-order workflow after assessing the person and generating the required prescription.

MPJE exam trap: EC standing-order training is not a mandatory two-hour class. OTC levonorgestrel and prescription ella have different federal marketing statuses.

Official references: ecLaw · ec · access

5. EC assessment: minors, third-party requests, advance supply

Topic: Emergency Contraception & ACCESS · Original Korean note: w5-b-3 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Mass.gov explains that parental consent is not required under the EC statewide standing order for a minor. This is a specific protected-access rule; do not assume every other minor health intervention has identical consent laws.

For EC dispensed under the standing order, the pharmacist must assess the actual patient in person or by phone. A friend cannot request a patient-specific standing-order prescription while the intended patient is never evaluated. That is different from purchasing available OTC levonorgestrel for another person.

The official FAQ permits specified antiemetics, repeating a dose following certain vomiting episodes, and advance EC supply as described in the signed standing order. These are protocol-specific, not unrestricted authority for any antiemetic, repeat schedule or patient. If sexual assault is disclosed, provide EC without unnecessary delay and then arrange appropriate support/referral.

Someone buys OTC levonorgestrel for friendOTC purchase is different from standing-order prescription

Real-world example: A patient's partner asks for an ella prescription but the pharmacist cannot speak to the patient. Under the statewide order, obtain patient assessment, potentially by phone. OTC Plan B purchasing follows its different retail pathway.

MPJE exam trap: Third-party OTC purchase is not the same as issuing a patient-specific EC standing-order prescription without assessing the patient.

Official references: ec · ecLaw

6. EC reporting and product-confusion table

Topic: Emergency Contraception & ACCESS · Original Korean note: w5-b-4 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Massachusetts requires annual reporting of the number of EC dispensing events under the standing order for the January 1–December 31 reporting year. Obtain the current Annual Emergency Contraception Standing Order Report submission link from the DPH pharmacy contact listed on Mass.gov. Do not copy the naloxone January 15 deadline to EC; the official EC FAQ specifies annual reporting but not that identical deadline.

For standing-order dispensing, maintain the patient prescription, required label and counseling/assessment records. Insurer coverage may differ by plan; using a standing order for billing does not convert the drug's FDA marketing category.

7. ACCESS law: twelve-month insurance coverage versus pharmacist scope

Topic: Emergency Contraception & ACCESS · Original Korean note: w5-b-5 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Massachusetts's ACCESS law gives eligible insured patients access to a 12-month supply of covered prescription birth control at once under the applicable three-month trial and other plan-specific rules. Methods covered by the insurance-access framework may include pills, patches, rings or injections; this does not confer independent pharmacist prescribing of every method.

Check whether the plan is subject to Massachusetts coverage rules (e.g., many fully insured MA plans, MassHealth, GIC). Self-funded employer plans may not be subject to the same state mandate. Insurance benefit rules, retail supply availability and legal prescriptive authority are independent questions.

A 2025 MassHealth/HSN standing order separately permits eligible patients to receive up to a 365-day supply of OTC norgestrel 0.075 mg (Opill) with defined screening and counseling. OTC purchase without an insurer claim is lawful independently; the standing order is a coverage/dispensing path.

PathWhat it controls
ACCESS insurance coverageEligibility and covered method/quantity under plan
DCP 26-08-125 pharmacist prescriptionOral/patch prescription, risk screening, ≤12 months
MassHealth/HSN Opill standing orderUp to 365 days eligible OTC norgestrel supply through stated process

Real-world example: A patient has a ring prescription from a physician and ACCESS-eligible insurance. The pharmacist may dispense covered medication; that insurance right does not automatically allow the pharmacist to initiate the ring prescription under DCP 26-08-125.

MPJE exam trap: Birth-control insurance coverage does not expand the pharmacist's oral/patch prescribing list. Three-month trial and twelve-month supply are insurance concepts.

8. Opill versus Plan B versus ella: OTC and prescription status

Topic: Emergency Contraception & ACCESS · Original Korean note: w3-d-15 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Opill (norgestrel 0.075 mg) is a daily oral contraceptive approved for OTC use. It is not emergency contraception. Plan B One-Step (levonorgestrel 1.5 mg) is OTC emergency contraception, available without a current federal minimum-age purchasing restriction. ella (ulipristal acetate) remains a prescription-only EC product.

Pharmacist-generated prescriptions may be used for certain insurance pathways even where the exact product is FDA-authorized OTC. Such administrative/billing arrangements do not mean a prescription is legally required for every cash OTC purchase.

Emergency contraception is not medication abortion. Product-specific restrictions and medical recommendations should be checked before counseling; do not use the brand name alone to infer dose or FDA approval for every form.

DrugTypical purpose / access
Opill / norgestrelOTC daily contraception
Plan B / levonorgestrelOTC emergency contraception
ella / ulipristalPrescription-only EC; MA statewide-order route
Mifepristone/misoprostolMedication abortion uses; separate federal/state requirements

Real-world example: A customer asks for Opill to take as a one-time after-sex rescue pill. Explain that Opill is not the intended emergency-contraceptive regimen and identify the appropriate authorized EC access path.

MPJE exam trap: Opill ≠ Plan B ≠ ella. Prescription-only status of ella does not mean a physician appointment is always needed when the lawful statewide-order pathway applies.

Official references: fdaSwitch · ec · birthcontrol

9. HIV PrEP, PEP and STI pharmacist authority: training and boundaries

Topic: HIV Prevention & STI Prescribing · Original Korean note: w3-d-16 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

PrEP prevents HIV infection in a person at future risk before exposure; PEP is post-exposure prevention. Treating already-established HIV infection is a distinct medical purpose. Under DCP 26-08-125, pharmacist PrEP and PEP prescribing must comply with the specific Massachusetts statewide guidance and medication eligibility.

Prior to HIV prevention prescribing, pharmacists need at least two hours of qualifying accredited training covering HIV risk evaluation, acute HIV symptoms, indications/limits of PrEP/PEP, and other prevention strategies—not a pharmacology-only course. Appropriate ACPE/IPCE (the circular prints ICPE) or AMA PRA Category 1 recognition must be checked.

STI prevention and treatment (including specified chlamydia/gonorrhea treatments and doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis, DoxyPEP) require the corresponding separate statewide protocol and relevant accredited training. Do not assume the HIV two-hour minimum is a universal STI training requirement, or that all suspected infections qualify for pharmacist treatment.

ServiceDistinguishing feature
Oral PrEPBefore exposure; specific statewide PrEP guidance and lab/screening criteria
HIV PEPAfter exposure; separate urgency, testing, referral and protocol
Chlamydia/gonorrhea treatmentSeparate clinical eligibility and statewide STI treatment guidance
DoxyPEPSTI prevention after potential exposure; separate eligible population/protocol

Real-world example: A patient reports an exposure yesterday and asks for 'PrEP'. The pharmacist must distinguish the urgency and criteria for PEP rather than apply a routine PrEP refill algorithm.

MPJE exam trap: Two-hour accredited HIV training must include patient assessment, not just drug facts. Separate statewide guidelines matter.

Official references: dcp · protocol

10. Protocol verification boundary: do not invent tests, quantities or refill limits

Topic: HIV Prevention & STI Prescribing · Original Korean note: hiv-protocol-open · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Massachusetts publishes a 2026 protocol index listing oral PrEP (August 2026), HIV PEP (September 2026), STI treatment (August 2026), and DoxyPEP (August 2026). These are separate protocol documents; the general pharmacist-prescribing circular is not a complete clinical algorithm.

Verification boundary: The entire standalone DOCX appendices on precise initial/repeat HIV testing, pending laboratory results, eligibility exclusions, dispensing quantities, refill limits, urgent referral and follow-up were not reliably available in full for this guide. Consequently the guide does not fabricate a 30/60/90-day supply cap or substitute a different state's protocol or generic CDC recommendation for Massachusetts's actual pharmacist permissions.

Verified general safeguards remain: NPI, required training, product-label/statewide-protocol adherence, documentation, patient written record, counseling and a narrow same-location MCSR exception. For actual clinical service, consult each current official protocol in full before prescribing.

Confirmed sourceStill requires full protocol for execution
DCP 26-08-125Common pharmacist legal authority, NPI, counseling and training
Statewide PrEP/PEP documentsExact HIV testing/supply/referral decision tree
Statewide STI treatment/DoxyPEP documentsSpecific qualifying infection/exposure, dosing, follow-up and exclusions

Real-world example: An exam question asks for the maximum Massachusetts pharmacist PrEP initial days' supply. Unless the exact statewide protocol is obtained and cited, do not confidently borrow another jurisdiction's supply limit.

MPJE exam trap: The four statewide guidance documents are not interchangeable. An explicitly unresolved point is safer than a memorized invented number.

Official references: protocol · dcp

11. HIV product and brand distinctions: prevention is not treatment

Topic: HIV Prevention & STI Prescribing · Original Korean note: w5-b-8 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Truvada (emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate) and Descovy (emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide) have different formulations, labeling, populations and restrictions. Do not substitute the products or assume every patient and use are the same.

Apretude (cabotegravir) is an HIV prevention product while Cabenuva (cabotegravir/rilpivirine) is a treatment combination. Yeztugo is a lenacapavir HIV PrEP product, whereas Sunlenca has a different HIV treatment context; the same active ingredient does not make their regimens interchangeable.

Each product's approved indication, test requirements, route and administration pathway must be checked. An FDA-approved injectable does not mean every pharmacist is automatically authorized to administer it merely because that pharmacist can prescribe under §19F.

PairDifferent uses
Truvada / DescovyTwo different oral drug combinations; prevention labeling differs
Apretude / CabenuvaCabotegravir PrEP versus cabotegravir/rilpivirine treatment
Yeztugo / SunlencaLenacapavir PrEP versus treatment regimen/indication

Real-world example: A prescriber specifies Cabenuva for a patient receiving HIV treatment. The pharmacist cannot treat it as an Apretude-style PrEP order merely because both contain cabotegravir.

MPJE exam trap: Shared ingredients, similar brand names and FDA approval do not make all HIV medicines clinically or legally interchangeable.

12. Naloxone: OTC product versus statewide standing order

Topic: Naloxone & Overdose Prevention · Original Korean note: w3-d-18 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Some naloxone nasal spray products are OTC; the generic name alone does not make every formulation or strength OTC. Massachusetts's statewide standing order under M.G.L. c.94C §19B remains a pathway for prescription insurance billing when a buyer does not have an individual physician prescription.

A person may obtain naloxone not only for themselves but also to rescue another person at risk for opioid overdose. The purchaser need not be the individual with an opioid prescription. State law recognizes good-faith naloxone administration for apparent opioid-related overdose under defined protections; this is not unlimited immunity for any clinical conduct.

FDA approved Rextovy 4 mg nasal naloxone OTC on June 16, 2026, adding another product option; availability and coverage can differ. Distinguish legal OTC status, MA standing-order Rx dispensing, and insurance claims.

Access routeKey fact
Ordinary OTC naloxone productDirect retail purchase of OTC-labeled form
Statewide standing orderPharmacist may issue/dispense for rescue, including third-party helper, and bill eligible insurance
Patient-specific prescriptionStandard lawful prescription path
Rescue administrationGood-faith state protection has conditions

Real-world example: A parent requests a rescue kit for their adult child's possible overdose. The pharmacist may provide naloxone under the standing order even though the parent is not the intended patient.

MPJE exam trap: Naloxone is not universally OTC in every formulation; the statewide standing order remains useful for insurance billing.

Official references: naloxone · nalOrder · nalPublic · fdaNal

13. Naloxone stock, opioid offer, counseling, insurance and label

Topic: Naloxone & Overdose Prevention · Original Korean note: w3-d-19 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Massachusetts Board Policy 2024-01 (revised February 6, 2025) requires pharmacies to keep a continuous sufficient supply of naloxone responsive to community need and offer naloxone whenever dispensing a Schedule II opioid. Offering is required; the patient is not required to purchase it. The opioid trigger should not be applied automatically to every Schedule II stimulant.

When dispensing through the standing order, label the rescue kit with the expiration of its naloxone units, counsel the buyer, supply an approved opioid-antagonist information pamphlet, and make reasonable effort to determine prescription insurance coverage.

For profile/label under the special insured standing-order route, the policy states to use 'Naloxone Rescue Kit' in place of the purchaser name/address. The Board policy states no regulatory quantity limit for the individual, although the insurer may impose coverage or claim limitations.

SituationBoard 2024-01 rule

Buyer requesting kit for friendMay dispense via standing order
Rescue kit packagingMark unit expiration date and provide counseling/pamphlet
Insurer covers fewer kitsCoverage cap does not mean legal maximum number dispensed

Real-world example: A patient picks up oxycodone and declines naloxone. The pharmacy must offer it, but does not force the patient to accept. Document according to policy and workflow.

MPJE exam trap: Offer on Schedule II opioid, not all CII drugs; 'no regulatory limit' is different from an insurer's payment limit.

Official references: naloxone · nalOrder

14. Two naloxone reporting options: daily PMP or January 15 aggregate

Topic: Naloxone & Overdose Prevention · Original Korean note: w3-d-20 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Preferred route under Policy 2024-01: include naloxone dispensing records in daily PMP submissions. This data is for collection and does not appear on the patient's usual PMP profile.

If the pharmacy does not use PMP submissions for naloxone data, send the annual report by January 15 for the preceding calendar year to the policy-specified DPH channel. Required fields are pharmacy name + ZIP, total naloxone doses dispensed, and total doses paid for by insurance.

The policy encourages, but does not require, extra event-level details such as dispensing date, buyer ZIP, per-event dose count and insurance payment flag. Count doses, not prescriptions, patient names or retail boxes.

Real-world example: A pharmacy dispensed 100 rescue kits with two doses per kit and 60 kits were covered by insurance. Its aggregate report requires 200 doses, 120 insured doses (assuming all kits have two doses), and pharmacy name/ZIP—not '100 prescriptions'.

MPJE exam trap: January 15 applies to naloxone alternative reporting, not automatically to EC's separate annual standing-order report.

Official references: naloxone

15. Naloxone dispensing outside pharmacy premises: pharmacist must be present

Topic: Naloxone & Overdose Prevention · Original Korean note: w3-d-21 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Policy 2024-01 permits naloxone standing-order dispensing at a qualifying remote location outside the licensed pharmacy space if a MA-licensed pharmacist linked to a MA pharmacy is physically present whenever naloxone is stored or dispensed there.

Naloxone must be stored securely under the pharmacist's constant observation. Access and handling must be restricted to licensed pharmacy personnel, with HIPAA privacy and secure encrypted technology that allows confidential records.

This is a narrow naloxone service model, not a general permission to send unsupervised technicians with arbitrary Schedule II–VI stock to an off-site event.

Real-world example: A naloxone community event sends a technician to distribute standing-order kits, while pharmacist answers questions by video from the home store. This does not meet the policy's remote-location pharmacist presence rule.

MPJE exam trap: Remote telephonic consultation cannot replace on-site pharmacist presence in the narrow standing-order remote-location framework.

16. Expedited Partner Therapy (EPT): chlamydia-only partner prescriptions

Topic: EPT & Protected Care · Original Korean note: w3-d-22 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Under MA Joint Policy 2020-08, Expedited Partner Therapy (EPT) allows treatment of partners of a diagnosed person with chlamydia without individual examination or testing of each partner. This specific pharmacy policy says it applies only to chlamydia, not automatically to all STIs or to the newer pharmacist STI-prescribing program.

An EPT partner receives a separate prescription. The prescriber may name the partner, or legally use 'Expedited Partner Therapy', 'E.P.T.' or 'EPT' in place of the partner's name/address. The pharmacy uses that designated identifier in the profile and prescription label, not fabricated patient demographic information or the index patient's name.

If the pharmacist cannot or will not fill a proper EPT prescription, the MA policy requires referral to an alternate pharmacy. Appropriate drug, directions and safety information should still be provided.

EPT caseCorrect handling
Named partnerSeparate partner-specific Rx
Unnamed partnerUse EPT authorized identifier as name/address
Unwilling/unable to fillRefer to alternate pharmacy
Other STIDo not assume chlamydia-only policy authorizes EPT for every disease

Real-world example: A prescriber sends a chlamydia partner prescription labeled 'EPT' without partner DOB/address. The pharmacist should apply the authorized EPT pathway rather than invent a birthday to force a standard profile.

MPJE exam trap: EPT is a partner treatment exception for chlamydia; it is not the same as pharmacist-initiated STI therapy for an assessed patient.

17. Protected reproductive and gender-affirming care: access and Schedule VI labels

Topic: EPT & Protected Care · Original Korean note: w3-d-23 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Massachusetts Board Policy 2023-05, effective March 22, 2023, treats family-planning medications (including contraception, EC, mifepristone and misoprostol) as medicines needed for community care. MA licensed pharmacies are expected to maintain continuous sufficient supply and, when a valid order cannot be filled immediately, arrange timely access through replenishment, prescriber alternative, lawful transfer or another appropriate pathway.

The December 18, 2025 labeling advisory summarizes Shield Law 2.0 (Acts of 2025 c.16): where a prescriber requests it for Schedule VI reproductive/gender-affirming-care medication, pharmacy label rules use the healthcare practice name instead of the individual prescriber's name. This does not extend automatically to federally controlled testosterone products or erase the internal original prescription and records.

Protected care, access policy, FDA REMS, pharmacy licensure and appropriate documentation must operate together. A Shield Law protection is not a universal waiver of any federal/state product requirement.

Legal issueWhat applies
Reproductive medication accessPolicy 2023-05 sufficient stock and timely alternatives
Valid prescription and product unavailableReplenish, contact prescriber, transfer or appropriate other access
MA Schedule VI protected-care prescriptionPractice-name label upon prescriber request

Real-world example: A pharmacy lacks a validly prescribed family-planning medicine. The pharmacy must not obstruct access; it should make a timely lawful supply or referral/transfer arrangement while meeting any product REMS.

MPJE exam trap: The 2025 practice-name label protection applies to the specified Schedule VI category and upon prescriber request, not all controlled medications.

18. Documentation comparison: prescribing, dispensing, administration, and notification

Topic: EPT & Protected Care · Original Korean note: w3-d-24 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Different public-health pharmacy services share an accountability concept but do not use one universal form or one universal deadline. Document the exact legal authority, professional's qualifications, patient eligibility and consent, relevant current protocol/order, medicine and dose, actual prescription versus dispense versus administration, required counseling and the appropriate communications.

Different verbs and clocks matter: CDTM has a mandatory 24-hour Rx copy; DCP 26-08-125 includes an advice/referral or consent-based PCP notification option; vaccination has MIIS reporting; a serious injection adverse reaction requires immediate prescriber notification under DCP 26-03-124; naloxone has its own PMP/January-15 reporting.

A pharmacy should not make up data (name, address, DOB) when an authorized EPT label intentionally uses a substitute identifier. Nor should a mere over-the-counter sale be automatically treated as a documented clinician prescription.

PathPrimary event/record
§19F pharmacist prescribingNPI, risk/eligibility, record to patient, counseling and referral
EC standing orderActual patient assessment, Rx and annual count
Naloxone standing orderSpecific kit/profile/label/counseling and PMP or annual report
EPT partner prescriptionSeparate Rx with EPT identifier if unnamed
Nonvaccine administrationDate, drug, lot, injection route/site, administrator and outcome

Real-world example: A pharmacist prescribes EC under the statewide standing order and also dispenses naloxone at another encounter. The annual EC count and naloxone daily-PMP/Jan-15 reporting are separate workflows, not combined into a single form.

MPJE exam trap: A reporting clock is meaningful only with its correct actor, event and source; never copy a number across different programs.

Official references: dcp · ec · naloxone · ept · dcpAdmin

19. Naloxone, nalmefene and naltrexone are different

Topic: Drug-name Exam Traps · Original Korean note: w5-b-14 · Reviewed 2026-11-09

Naloxone reverses an opioid overdose; some specific nasal naloxone products are OTC (including Narcan and, since June 16, 2026, Rextovy). Not every naloxone dosage form is automatically OTC.

Nalmefene is a different opioid antagonist with product-specific uses and FDA status; do not assume the MA naloxone standing order automatically covers every similarly named reversal product. Naltrexone is used to treat opioid/alcohol use disorders, not an interchangeable immediate opioid-overdose kit.

A product containing buprenorphine/naloxone is not an OTC rescue kit merely because its combination includes naloxone; apply the complete product approval, schedule and prescribing rules.

DrugDistinction
NaloxoneOverdose rescue; select formulations OTC; standing-order path
NalmefeneDifferent opioid antagonist and product-specific authorization
NaltrexoneSubstance-use disorder maintenance/relapse prevention
Buprenorphine/naloxoneCombination addiction treatment; not OTC naloxone rescue

Real-world example: A patient asks for naloxone to prevent recurrence of alcohol use disorder. Naloxone is not the same as oral/injectable naltrexone therapy.

MPJE exam trap: Look-alike names do not establish same use, schedule, insurance or standing-order eligibility.

Official references: naloxone · fdaNal

Question 1. A pharmacist wants to prescribe an oral hormonal contraceptive under DCP 26-08-125. Which general condition is required?
  1. The patient must already have a physician-written contraception prescription
  2. The pharmacist must have an NPI and complete appropriate screening/training under §19F, documenting and providing a written record
  3. The pharmacy must use a CDTM CPA for every such encounter
  4. Contraception pharmacist prescribing only applies to patients age 18+

Correct answer: B.

A: Prior physician prescription is not universally required.

B: Correct. Current DCP circular sets NPI, risk assessment, training, counseling and written record.

C: This statutory path is distinct from retail CDTM.

D: The circular allows patients of any age for oral/patch contraception.

Question 2. Which contraceptive option is within the DCP 26-08-125 independent oral/patch pharmacist prescribing description?
  1. Vaginal ring insertion
  2. Intrauterine device placement
  3. Eligible oral hormonal contraception for a screened 17-year-old
  4. All injectable contraceptives without additional authorizations

Correct answer: C.

A: Vaginal rings are not named as pharmacist-prescribable under this subsection.

B: IUD insertion is not authorized through this oral/patch prescribing route.

C: Correct. Any age plus assessment and other requirements.

D: Insurance coverage of injections does not expand prescribing authority.

Question 3. A minor requests emergency contraception from a pharmacist using the statewide standing order. What is accurate?
  1. MA's EC order does not mandate parental consent; pharmacist assesses actual patient and generates appropriate patient-specific Rx
  2. All EC requires a physician appointment
  3. Any third party may obtain an ella standing-order prescription without patient assessment
  4. EC is the same as medication abortion

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. The patient can be evaluated by phone or in person.

B: The statewide order permits pharmacist dispensing without a physician appointment.

C: Third-party request still requires actual intended patient assessment for standing-order Rx.

D: Emergency contraception and medication abortion are different.

Question 4. What is the deadline for the annual emergency-contraception standing-order report?
  1. Always January 15, exactly like naloxone
  2. No report is required if levonorgestrel is OTC
  3. Every 72 hours through MIIS
  4. The MA EC law requires annual reporting of standing-order dispensing events; the official FAQ does not state naloxone's January 15 deadline

Correct answer: D.

A: That date belongs to naloxone alternative aggregate reporting, not assumed EC.

B: The EC order carries annual reporting even for eligible OTC products dispensed through it.

C: MIIS is immunization reporting.

D: Correct. Check current DPH report instructions.

Question 5. Which statement distinguishes ACCESS insurance coverage from pharmacist prescribing authority?
  1. A benefit covering rings and injections authorizes every pharmacist to prescribe both
  2. Eligible plans may cover a 12-month supply after a trial; DCP 26-08-125 independently restricts pharmacist prescribing to oral/patch options
  3. All self-funded employer plans must follow every MA ACCESS rule
  4. The 12-month supply eliminates product expiration checks

Correct answer: B.

A: Insurance benefits do not grant prescriptive scope.

B: Correct. Coverage and prescribing are independent.

C: Self-funded plan rules differ.

D: Insurance does not alter FDA product shelf life.

Question 6. Before prescribing HIV PrEP or PEP under DCP 26-08-125, what training requirement applies?
  1. An online pharmacology-only module of any duration is sufficient
  2. Only vaccination training is required
  3. At least two hours of appropriate accredited training covering HIV risk/assessment and PrEP/PEP clinical limits as well as pharmacology
  4. Only a CDTM CPA is required

Correct answer: C.

A: The circular specifically rejects pharmacology-only course as sufficient.

B: Vaccination training does not substitute.

C: Correct.

D: This is a separate statutory prescribing path.

Question 7. When dispensing naloxone in Massachusetts, which trigger requires the pharmacy to offer it?
  1. Whenever a Schedule II opioid is dispensed
  2. Whenever any Schedule II stimulant is dispensed
  3. Only when the buyer is the opioid patient
  4. Only when a physician specifically writes naloxone

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Board policy requires an offer with Schedule II opioid dispensing.

B: Do not extend the opioid-specific trigger to all CII stimulants.

C: Third-party rescuers may purchase naloxone.

D: Standing-order dispensing is allowed.

Question 8. A pharmacy does not report naloxone doses in daily PMP submissions. What alternative report is required?
  1. Nothing, naloxone is OTC

  3. An MIIS entry within 72 hours

A: OTC status does not remove the policy's reporting scheme.

B: The annual report is aggregate, not a patient-name publication.

C: MIIS is for vaccine reporting.

Question 9. An anonymous chlamydia partner receives an EPT prescription. What may appear on MA pharmacy profile/label?
