Start with three questions

(1) What information? Is it identifiable health information protected by HIPAA, a specially protected Part 2 SUD-program record, Massachusetts minor-confidentiality information, or the personal information covered by 201 CMR 17.00? More than one may apply.

(2) Who is asking, and why? Treatment, payment, operations, patient/authorized representative, marketing, and police/legal requests have different rules. Working in the pharmacy never creates a right to browse unrelated charts.

(3) What deadline or safeguard? Separate the HIPAA 30-day patient-access response from the 60-day individual breach notification, the HHS 500-person reporting split, HIPAA six-year documentation retention, and Massachusetts-specific requirements.

2026 legal-status warning: The February 16, 2026 Part 2 compliance date has passed. A June 18, 2025 court ruling vacated most new reproductive-health HIPAA provisions, but certain NPP modifications survived. Proposed Security Rule changes are not automatically effective law. Specific restrictions and record-retention obligations may be longer than this summary.

Massachusetts pharmacy licensees must protect confidentiality under 247 CMR 9.01(16); federal HIPAA governs independently.

1. What HIPAA protects: PHI, covered entities and business associates

Original Korean source: w3-b-12 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Protected health information (PHI) is identifiable health information protected under HIPAA. It can appear on paper, on a computer screen, or in spoken conversation. A labeled prescription bag, a spoken diagnosis, and an electronic medication history can all contain PHI. HIPAA is not only about computerized patient records.

The Privacy Rule governs permitted and prohibited uses and disclosures by HIPAA covered entities (including qualifying healthcare providers) and their business associates. The Security Rule adds protective requirements for electronic PHI (ePHI). The Breach Notification Rule addresses notifications after certain impermissible disclosures of unsecured PHI.

A business associate is a vendor performing services involving PHI for a covered entity, such as certain claims-processing or health-data hosting services. An appropriate business associate agreement (BAA) is generally needed for that relationship. Sending needed medical information to another treating provider is different: provider-to-provider treatment communications do not automatically create a business-associate relationship. A BAA never overrides substantive privacy limitations.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A pharmacy sends prescription data to an outside billing company as part of contracted claims processing. Evaluate whether a BAA is required. By contrast, discussing a prescription safety issue with the patient's treating physician is ordinarily a treatment communication.

MPJE exam trap: Paper and oral PHI are protected by HIPAA Privacy Rule even though the HIPAA Security Rule specifically addresses ePHI.

Official references: HHS HIPAA Privacy Rule · HHS HIPAA Security Rule

2. TPO, minimum necessary and unauthorized curiosity access

Original Korean source: w3-b-13 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

TPO means Treatment, Payment, and Health Care Operations. HIPAA generally permits uses/disclosures for these purposes without a separate written authorization, subject to applicable rules. Examples: clarifying a dose with the treating prescriber (treatment), billing an insurer (payment), and a legitimate internal compliance review (operations).

Minimum necessary means limiting PHI use, disclosure and requests to what is reasonably needed for the purpose. Important exception: the standard generally does not apply to disclosures to or requests by a healthcare provider for treatment. It also does not apply to the individual's own access, uses/disclosures under a patient authorization, or disclosures required by law, among other specified exceptions. Avoid simplifying this to “the minimum-necessary rule never applies during treatment”: internal use and workforce access still require job-related justification and appropriate controls.

A technician who looks up a neighbor's medication profile out of curiosity is not providing treatment. Likewise, giving an outside marketing company a patient list is not automatically a permitted TPO use; authorization is ordinarily necessary for marketing/sale-of-PHI activities unless a specific exception applies. The phrase “healthcare” alone never supplies authority.

Extra patient protection: if a patient pays a service in full out of pocket and requests that related PHI not be disclosed to the health plan for payment/operations, the covered provider generally must agree, unless disclosure is otherwise required by law (45 CFR 164.522(a)(1)(vi)).

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A physician telephones the pharmacist to resolve a suspected duplicate anticoagulant prescription. Treatment disclosure is generally permitted without an extra authorization. A coworker searching the same patient's unrelated family member's prescriptions for gossip has no treatment purpose.

MPJE exam trap: Provider-to-provider treatment exception ≠ every employee has unlimited permission to browse records; TPO ≠ marketing.

Official references: HHS HIPAA Privacy Rule · HHS minimum necessary guidance · HHS TPO guidance · HHS restricted health-plan disclosures

3. Prescription pickup, family members, personal representatives and minors

Original Korean source: w3-b-14 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

HIPAA does not require a universal signed form before a friend or family member may pick up a prescription. A pharmacist may use professional judgment and reasonable inferences about the patient's best interests to permit a pickup. Only information relevant to that person's involvement in care or payment should be shared. If the patient objects or the situation raises confidentiality concerns, do not assume the family connection resolves them.

Under HIPAA, a parent ordinarily acts as a minor child's personal representative, but important exceptions apply when a minor can consent to particular care under state law, when a court/other authorized person consents, or when parents agree to a confidential relationship. Where state law expressly governs access, the HIPAA rule defers to it.

Massachusetts M.G.L. c.112 §12F permits minors to consent in certain circumstances, including marriage, parenting, armed-forces status, pregnancy, financial independence away from parents, and specified public-health-disease care. Sexually active minors may also consent to HIV prevention care. This is not unrestricted minor consent for every medical procedure; §12F includes limits for abortion/sterilization under several listed categories. The statute separately protects records connected with qualifying minor-consented care, with specific consent/judicial and serious-risk provisions.

Before disclosing a minor's sensitive prescription or PrEP record, check the specific service, consent authority, parental-access law, and lawful disclosure pathway. A parent paying insurance premiums does not by itself establish a right to all protected minor-consented records. Insurance explanations of benefits can create separate privacy concerns; confidential billing is not automatically guaranteed without appropriate arrangements.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A 16-year-old lawfully consents to HIV prevention services under §12F. A parent phones to ask whether the teen is taking PrEP. Do not release the protected treatment details merely because the caller is the parent; verify service-specific confidentiality and any relevant legal exception.

MPJE exam trap: A minor's permission to consent, a pharmacist's authority to prescribe, a drug's FDA age/weight eligibility, and parental access to records are four different questions.

Official references: HHS pharmacist/family FAQ · HHS parental access FAQ · M.G.L. c.112 §12F · MA DPH billing privacy · MA DPH HIV guidance

4. Notice of Privacy Practices, patient access and record retention

Original Korean source: w3-b-15 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

A covered provider with a direct treatment relationship generally provides the Notice of Privacy Practices (NPP) no later than first service delivery and makes a good-faith effort to obtain acknowledgment of receipt (with emergency exceptions). The patient does not have to sign it for treatment to continue. An NPP acknowledgment is not blanket authorization for future disclosures.

Under 45 CFR 164.524, a covered entity generally must act on a qualifying patient's request for records in the designated record set within 30 calendar days after receiving it. One extension of up to 30 more calendar days is possible only with timely written explanation and a completion date during the initial period. If a business associate holds the relevant PHI, that does not pause the covered entity's clock. Other rights include requesting amendments, certain confidential communications and restrictions, and permitted disclosure accounting, each with specific limits.

Never confuse retention clocks: HIPAA generally requires retention of specified compliance documentation for six years (45 CFR 164.530(j)); HIPAA does not impose a universal six-year medical-record retention rule. Massachusetts 247 CMR 9.16(1) requires pharmacy prescription records, purchasing/return records, and disposal/destruction records to be readily retrievable for at least two years. Other state, federal, contract, litigation-hold or specialized record obligations may require longer retention.

Massachusetts 247 CMR 9.01(16) requires licensees to preserve patient confidentiality; 247 CMR 9.16(7) requires confidential patient profiles; 247 CMR 9.18(6) requires a consultation area providing adequate auditory and visual privacy.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A patient requests prescription history on November 1. The pharmacy cannot tell the patient the request will start processing only when its data-storage vendor responds. It must track the HIPAA 30-day response window from receipt.

MPJE exam trap: NPP acknowledgment ≠ authorization. Patient-access 30 + possible 30 ≠ HIPAA six-year documentation ≠ Massachusetts two-year pharmacy record minimum.

Official references: HHS Notice of Privacy Practices · HHS access deadlines · HHS medical-record retention · 247 CMR 9.00 (Massachusetts Board)

5. HIPAA breaches: risk assessment and the 60-day/500-person rules

Original Korean source: w3-b-16 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

An impermissible use or disclosure of PHI is generally presumed to be a breach unless a specific exception applies or a documented assessment establishes a low probability of compromise. The four risk factors include the nature/sensitivity of PHI, who received it, whether it was viewed/acquired, and mitigation. A wrong-patient bag or misdirected fax is not automatically harmless just because it was accidental.

For a reportable breach of unsecured PHI, affected individuals must receive notice without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery. The HHS Secretary must be notified within that same maximum window if 500 or more individuals are affected. For fewer than 500, HHS reports are due within 60 calendar days after the end of the discovery calendar year. That later HHS deadline does not delay notices to affected patients.

Media notification is triggered by a breach affecting more than 500 residents of a state or jurisdiction, not simply by any nationwide count of 500. The “500 or more for HHS” and “more than 500 residents for media” wording differs. A business associate has its own covered-entity notification obligations, without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days.

Massachusetts is another layer: M.G.L. c.93H §3 requires notice as soon as practicable and without unreasonable delay for qualifying Massachusetts personal-information incidents, including to the Attorney General and OCABR for owners/licensors, with rules that depend on the entity's role. Massachusetts does not universally borrow HIPAA's 60-day maximum as its own fixed deadline. M.G.L. c.93H §5 addresses interaction with federal breach procedures; do not assume one notice ends all responsibilities.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A pharmacy accidentally sends 12 patients' readable PHI to a wrong external recipient. Escalate immediately, contain and document, evaluate breach exceptions/risk, and if reportable notify patients within the HIPAA window; any HHS submission for fewer than 500 follows the annual deadline. Also assess whether a distinct Massachusetts c.93H personal-information event occurred.

MPJE exam trap: 500 or more → HHS within 60 days; more than 500 residents of one state/jurisdiction → media; under 500 → later HHS report, but patient notices are still prompt.

Official references: HHS Breach Notification Rule · M.G.L. c.93H §3 · M.G.L. c.93H §5

6. 42 CFR Part 2: sensitive SUD-program records and 2026 changes

Original Korean source: w3-b-17 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

42 CFR Part 2 protects records from qualifying federally assisted substance use disorder (SUD) programs that provide diagnosis, treatment or referral. Some requirements also bind recipients of Part 2 records. A pharmacy holding a buprenorphine prescription is not automatically itself a Part 2 program merely because buprenorphine can treat opioid use disorder. Identify the record's origin and the pharmacy's legal status.

HHS's 2024 Part 2 final rule became effective April 16, 2024, with compliance required by February 16, 2026. It permits a single patient consent for future TPO uses and disclosures of Part 2 records. A HIPAA covered entity or business associate receiving Part 2 records under this consent may redisclose them as HIPAA permits, but not use them against the patient in legal proceedings without Part 2's separate consent/court-order safeguards.

Part 2 is not simply HIPAA TPO: the originating Part 2 program normally needs compliant consent or a specific Part 2 exception to disclose identifiable SUD records. Specially defined SUD counseling notes require a separate consent and cannot ride on a broad TPO consent. New privacy-notice and breach-reporting requirements also apply; do not mistake these for an open license to publish SUD information.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A covered pharmacy receives a Part 2 program's patient record with a valid single TPO consent. Permitted later treatment use may be possible, but the record cannot simply be handed to investigators to support proceedings against the patient.

MPJE exam trap: Not all SUD-related prescriptions are Part 2 records; 2026 HIPAA alignment did not abolish Part 2 consent and legal-proceeding protections.

Official references: HHS Part 2 guidance · HHS Part 2 final-rule fact sheet

7. 2025 reproductive-health HIPAA court ruling: what remains in 2026

Original Korean source: w3-b-18 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

On June 18, 2025, a federal district court vacated most of HHS's 2024 reproductive-health HIPAA rule. The court specifically vacated certain NPP changes at 45 CFR 164.520(b)(1)(ii)(F), (G), and (H). Other NPP modifications remain in effect, with a February 16, 2026 compliance date. Do not teach the vacated 2024 reproductive-health attestation scheme as a generally operative 2026 requirement.

Existing HIPAA still protects reproductive-health PHI. A law-enforcement officer requesting information does not automatically make disclosure mandatory. A subpoena, court order, warrant and ordinary request are different legal instruments; determine whether and exactly what the applicable law permits or compels, and comply with applicable Massachusetts law and narrow legal-process requirements. Escalate nonroutine legal requests to the privacy officer/counsel.

Massachusetts may provide separate protections for certain care and records; those should be evaluated independently rather than assumed to be superseded by a withdrawn federal rule. This is a date-sensitive topic—always recheck official HHS updates if your exam date is later.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A police officer asks for every patient's mifepristone history with no clear legal process. The pharmacist does not automatically hand over records just because the request involves law enforcement; the legal authority, applicable exception and permitted scope require review.

MPJE exam trap: Vacatur of most new 2024 reproductive-health provisions ≠ repeal of HIPAA privacy protections or all NPP changes.

Official references: HHS reproductive-privacy vacatur notice · HHS HIPAA Privacy Rule

8. HIPAA Security Rule versus Massachusetts WISP

Original Korean source: w6-a-11 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

The HIPAA Security Rule covers electronic PHI and seeks confidentiality (prevent unauthorized disclosure), integrity (prevent improper changes), and availability (ensure authorized access when needed). Requirements include administrative safeguards (risk analysis, policies, training), physical safeguards (workspace/device controls), and technical safeguards (access controls and audit mechanisms).

Massachusetts 201 CMR 17.00 separately requires businesses holding covered Massachusetts residents' personal information to maintain a comprehensive written information security program (WISP) proportionate to the organization. Its statutory definition of personal information is not the same as HIPAA PHI: it generally requires a resident's name/initial plus Social Security, driver-license/state ID, or qualifying financial-account/payment-card information. It covers qualifying paper and electronic records and includes employee supervision, service-provider oversight, physical protections, and technical controls where applicable.

A 2025 HHS proposal to strengthen cybersecurity must not be treated as the finalized current HIPAA Security Rule as of November 9, 2026. Using multifactor authentication may be sound security design, but do not automatically ascribe every specific proposed technical requirement to the effective rule without checking its actual status.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: An employee stores a spreadsheet of pharmacy patients' names plus payment-card information on an unencrypted laptop. The pharmacy must assess HIPAA if the file includes ePHI and Massachusetts 201 CMR 17.00 if the covered personal-information definition is met; a signed HIPAA training sheet alone does not satisfy an entire WISP.

MPJE exam trap: HIPAA PHI ≠ Massachusetts statutory PI. Security Rule is ePHI-focused; HIPAA Privacy Rule reaches spoken/paper PHI; WISP can protect qualifying paper records.

Official references: HHS HIPAA Security Rule · 201 CMR 17.00 (Massachusetts)

9. Computer downtime, incident response and safe workflows

Original Korean source: w6-a-12 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

A pharmacy computer outage does not create an automatic waiver of prescription validity, patient identification, controlled-substance restrictions, drug-interaction review, or avoidance of duplicate dispensing. Follow the pharmacy's lawful downtime procedures and perform reconciliation after restoration. Security-related downtime may create both patient-safety and privacy risks.

If you discover suspicious access or a misdirected PHI disclosure: contain the exposure, preserve evidence and logs, notify the responsible privacy/security personnel, investigate what happened, mitigate further harm, evaluate the breach criteria, and follow any legal reporting duties. The pharmacy should not alter or delete records to hide the incident. An alert or attempted intrusion does not necessarily prove a legally reportable breach, but an accidental disclosure also does not automatically fall outside the rules.

Keep pharmacy quality-improvement/error reporting separate from HIPAA breach notification and separate again from Massachusetts c.93H obligations. A single incident can trigger multiple independent workflows and clocks.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: During a ransomware incident, staff revert to approved paper records. They still verify prescription legitimacy and patient identity, document fills, prevent duplicate supply, and later reconcile transactions. The security team investigates whether PHI was actually compromised.

MPJE exam trap: Downtime ≠ permission to skip safety checks. Security incident ≠ proven reportable breach, but immediate escalation and assessment are appropriate.

Official references: HHS HIPAA Security Rule · HHS Breach Notification Rule · 247 CMR 9.00 (Massachusetts Board) · M.G.L. c.93H §3

10. Minor HIV prevention: consent, confidentiality and dispensing boundaries

Original Korean source: w5-b-6 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Massachusetts §12F includes a specific pathway for sexually active minors to consent to HIV prevention. For pharmacy scenarios this may involve PrEP or PEP-related care, but the exact FDA product labeling, prescriber/pharmacist authority, clinical protocol, and record-disclosure rules must still be satisfied. The statute's absence of a universal stated age minimum for this consent pathway is not permission to ignore medication-specific eligibility requirements.

When the minor validly consents under the law, §12F includes strong confidentiality language for connected records and specified written-consent, judicial-order and serious-risk exceptions. Its special parental notification provision identifies a treating physician or dentist in a serious life/limb-endangerment situation: do not rewrite that sentence as an automatic duty for every pharmacy employee to phone the parents. Check the exact record and other applicable laws.

The patient's care can still generate an insurance explanation of benefits, which is a separate confidentiality issue. Massachusetts DPH describes options for confidential billing, but those steps are not always automatic. Explain available privacy procedures without promising that billing will be invisible to the insurance policyholder.

Real-world pharmacy scenario: A sexually active 17-year-old obtains lawful HIV prevention care and asks that their parent not learn about it through pharmacy calls. The pharmacist should verify confidentiality flags, patient communication preferences, the applicable minor-consent pathway and potential insurance-billing disclosures.

MPJE exam trap: Minor confidentiality is service-specific; legal consent does not independently authorize a pharmacist to prescribe an ineligible product.

Official references: M.G.L. c.112 §12F · HHS parental access FAQ · MA DPH billing privacy · MA DPH HIV guidance

Memory tricks

PHI ≠ PI: health information under HIPAA is not the same definition as Massachusetts financial/ID-linked personal information. 30 / 60 / 6 / 2: 30 days to act on access; 60-day maximum for individual breach notification; 6 years for certain HIPAA documentation; 2-year Massachusetts pharmacy-record minimum. TPO is a purpose, not a permission slip. Part 2 adds a second lock for qualifying SUD-program records.

10 original MPJE-style multiple-choice questions

Question 1. Which item can be protected health information (PHI) under the HIPAA Privacy Rule?
  1. Only information stored electronically
  2. A spoken patient diagnosis, a labeled prescription bag, or an identifiable electronic medication history
  3. Only documents containing a Social Security number
  4. Only records sent to a government agency

Correct answer: B.

A: HIPAA Privacy Rule also covers oral and paper information.

B: Correct. Identifiable health information can be oral, paper or electronic.

C: An SSN is not required for PHI status.

D: HIPAA is not limited to government disclosures.

Question 2. A pharmacist calls the patient's treating physician about a potential drug interaction. Which statement is correct?
  1. A written patient authorization is always required for provider-to-provider treatment discussions
  2. The pharmacist may disclose the entire pharmacy database without restriction
  3. Treatment disclosures/requests by providers are generally outside the minimum-necessary requirement, while other privacy controls still apply
  4. HIPAA prohibits all disclosure outside the pharmacy

Correct answer: C.

A: HIPAA usually permits appropriate TPO disclosures without separate written authorization.

B: A treatment purpose does not authorize releasing unrelated databases.

C: Correct. The provider-treatment exception does not authorize curiosity access.

D: The Privacy Rule expressly permits appropriate disclosures.

Question 3. A patient's spouse comes to collect a routine prescription. The patient has not objected and nothing suggests elevated privacy risk. What may the pharmacist do under HIPAA?
  1. Use professional judgment and reasonable inferences to permit the pickup, sharing only relevant information
  2. Require a notarized HIPAA authorization in every pickup case
  3. Release the patient's entire medical record to the spouse
  4. Treat the spouse as a business associate and require a BAA

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. HIPAA permits reasonable inference and professional judgment in ordinary family pickup situations.

B: A universally notarized authorization is not the rule.

C: Being a spouse does not automatically confer unlimited access.

D: Family prescription pickup does not create a BAA relationship.

Question 4. A sexually active minor independently consents to qualifying HIV-prevention care under M.G.L. c.112 §12F. A parent asks for all related records. What is the best approach?
  1. Always release everything because the patient is under 18
  2. Always refuse all information about every minor's care regardless of service
  3. Treat the minor's prescription as proof that a pharmacist may prescribe any product at any age
  4. Apply service-specific minor-consent/confidentiality rules and any lawful disclosure exception before responding

Correct answer: D.

A: State law and HIPAA may protect minor-consented treatment information.

B: The rule is not a universal ban on all parental access.

C: Prescriptive authority and FDA product eligibility are different issues.

D: Correct. Examine §12F, HIPAA personal-representative rules and any relevant exception.

Question 5. A patient requests access to their designated-record-set records. What is HIPAA's standard response time?
  1. 60 days automatically, with no explanation
  2. Within 30 calendar days, with one possible up-to-30-day extension if timely written conditions are met
  3. Two years, matching Massachusetts pharmacy record retention
  4. Only after the business associate retrieves the data

Correct answer: B.

A: An automatic 60-day allowance is incorrect.

B: Correct. The extension requires a written reason and completion date within the initial 30 days.

C: Retention requirements do not set HIPAA access-response deadlines.

D: The business associate does not pause the covered entity's clock.

Question 6. Which statement about retention is correct?
  1. HIPAA's six-year period generally applies to specified compliance documentation, while 247 CMR 9.16(1) sets an at-least-two-year minimum for specified Massachusetts pharmacy records
  2. HIPAA requires every prescription to be retained for exactly six years
  3. All Massachusetts prescription records may be destroyed immediately after dispensing
  4. The NPP signature expires two years after dispensing

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. HIPAA documentation and pharmacy records are separate types of requirements; other rules may require longer retention.

B: HHS explicitly says HIPAA does not set universal medical-record retention.

C: 247 CMR 9.16 requires records to be maintained.

D: This is not the rule on NPP acknowledgment.

Question 7. A covered pharmacy discovers a reportable unsecured-PHI breach affecting 12 people. Which statement is correct?
  1. No individual notices are required because fewer than 500 people are affected
  2. The pharmacy must wait until year-end to notify the individuals
  3. Individual notice is due without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days; HHS notice can follow the under-500 annual reporting deadline
  4. Media notice is mandatory whenever 12 people are affected

Correct answer: C.

A: The under-500 rule concerns HHS reporting, not the need to notify affected people.

B: The individual 60-day maximum runs from breach discovery.

C: Correct. Under-500 HHS notification is within 60 days after the discovery year ends.

D: HIPAA media notice has a separate more-than-500-residents threshold.

Question 8. Which statement best describes 42 CFR Part 2 as of November 2026?
  1. Any pharmacy dispensing buprenorphine automatically becomes a federally assisted Part 2 treatment program
  2. The 2026 compliance date eliminated all SUD patient-consent requirements
  3. A Part 2 record can always be used against the patient in criminal proceedings after a normal TPO disclosure
  4. A qualifying Part 2 record may be disclosed with a compliant single TPO consent, but separate safeguards for proceedings against the patient remain

Correct answer: D.

A: The existence of a buprenorphine prescription alone does not establish program status.

B: Part 2 still imposes specialized requirements on covered records.

C: Special legal-proceeding safeguards remain.

D: Correct. The 2024 final-rule compliance date was February 16, 2026.

Question 9. Which statement correctly characterizes the 2024 federal reproductive-health HIPAA rule in November 2026?
  1. A June 18, 2025 court decision vacated most new provisions, while certain remaining NPP modifications became due February 16, 2026
  2. All HIPAA protections for reproductive-health PHI were repealed
  3. Every police request now requires immediate disclosure of patient records
  4. The entire 2024 special attestation scheme is still universally required

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. HHS distinguishes vacated provisions from surviving NPP changes.

B: Existing HIPAA privacy protections continue to apply.

C: Legal-process authority and disclosure limits must be evaluated.

D: Most of those 2024 additions were vacated, so blanket enforcement is inaccurate.

Question 10. Which statement best distinguishes HIPAA Security Rule from Massachusetts 201 CMR 17.00?
  1. The HIPAA Security Rule covers only paper PHI, while 201 CMR 17.00 covers only spoken diagnoses
  2. HIPAA Security Rule covers electronic PHI; the Massachusetts WISP rule covers specified state-defined personal information in paper and electronic records
  3. A written HIPAA privacy notice alone satisfies every Massachusetts WISP duty
  4. The 2025 HHS proposed cybersecurity changes were automatically fully effective when proposed

Correct answer: B.

A: HIPAA Security Rule is specifically about ePHI.

B: Correct. The statutory definitions and regulatory scopes differ.

C: The WISP involves operational administrative, physical and technical safeguards.

D: An NPRM is not automatically a final rule.
