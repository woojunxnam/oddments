How to answer sterile-compounding MPJE questions

Ask in this order: (1) Is it actually compounding, or manufacturer-directed preparation/administration? (2) Is it immediate-use, Category 1, Category 2, or Category 3? (3) What is the PEC/SEC and testing environment? (4) Are personnel qualified and records complete? (5) Which BUD is allowed by process, storage, sterility testing, stability and state policy? (6) Is there a reporting, recall, or special MA licensing issue?

Legal-status guard: 247 CMR 17.00 continues to appear on Massachusetts's draft-regulation list, so it must not be presented as enacted. Distinguish M.G.L. c.112 §§39F–39I, enacted 247 CMR 20, USP <797> and adopted Board policies (including 2026-03). A public USP FAQ is educational, not a substitute for full current USP–NF text.

Massachusetts sterile CE: 5 specialized contact hours each calendar year for qualifying sterile-compounding duties, potentially within the annual 20. Complex nonsterile uses a different 3-hour requirement.

1. Sterile compounding and Category 1–3

Source note: w4-b-12 · Checked November 9, 2026

A compounded sterile preparation (CSP) is a preparation made by combining, diluting, pooling, reconstituting, repackaging, or otherwise altering a product that must be sterile. Examples include intravenous preparations, some ophthalmic and irrigation solutions, and sterile implantable-pump medications. Even a formulation made from nonsterile ingredients can become a CSP after validated sterilization.

Contamination can cause severe harm because injections may place microorganisms or endotoxins directly into sensitive tissues. USP <797> sets minimum requirements for environmental controls, personnel, preparation, testing, and beyond-use dates (BUDs).

PathwayWhat it means
Immediate-useLimited pathway for qualifying CSPs to be administered promptly
Category 1Short BUD; qualifying ISO 5 primary engineering control may be in a segregated compounding area
Category 2Appropriate classified cleanroom suite; different BUDs depend on ingredients, processing, testing
Category 3Enhanced training, air/surface monitoring, sterility testing and stability requirements for extended BUD

Example: A pharmacist makes a sterile antibiotic in a qualifying buffer-room cleanroom. Its category does not depend solely on the name of the antibiotic; assess the process, tests, facility, and assigned BUD.

Exam trap: Category 3 does not mean “toxicity level 3,” and the old low/medium/high-risk labels are not simple aliases. USP <825> must also be considered for radiopharmaceuticals.

Official and supporting sources: USP <797> · USP FAQ, categories

2. Immediate-use: four hours from start

Source note: w4-b-13 · Checked November 9, 2026

The immediate-use provision allows certain CSPs to be prepared without meeting all Category 1–3 requirements, but only if every qualifying condition is met. Personnel must be trained, the preparation area must be clean and uncluttered, evidence-based compatibility used, starting components inspected, and aseptic technique observed.

A preparation must use no more than three different sterile products (not necessarily just three physical vials). Single-dose leftovers must be discarded after preparation for the one patient. Administration must begin no later than four hours from the start of preparation; otherwise discard. Label with ingredients, amounts, preparer and exact deadline unless the qualifying direct-administration/direct-observation exception applies.

Example: Preparation starts at 8 AM and finishes at 8:20 AM. Administration must begin by noon, not 12:20 PM. The four-hour limit does not mean every infusion must finish within four hours.

Exam trap: A manufacturer-directed preparation excluded by USP <797> is not automatically subject to immediate-use BUDs. “Urgent” alone does not waive training, compatibility or cleanliness.

Official and supporting sources: USP FAQ, immediate-use

3. Manufacturer labeling and proprietary bag/vial systems

Source note: w4-b-14 · Checked November 9, 2026

Preparing a single patient dose exactly according to FDA-approved manufacturer labeling can be outside the scope of USP <797> when the labeling specifies the diluent, resultant strength, container–closure system and storage time. Follow the actual label; merely using the right diluent while independently changing concentration or storage is not enough.

Proprietary bag/vial systems have two distinct cases. Docking and activating for immediate administration in accordance with labeling is not compounding. Docking in advance for future activation is compounding and must be performed under ISO Class 5 conditions following the applicable chapter; its BUD cannot exceed the manufacturer’s labeled allowance.

Example: A nurse activates a properly labeled dual-chamber system at bedside for a patient. Compare that with a pharmacy docking several systems today for tomorrow’s patients. Those are not equivalent activities.

Exam trap: Spiking an IV bag without further manipulation for administration is different from adding another product to create a new compounded medication.

Official and supporting sources: USP FAQ, approved labeling and proprietary systems

4. PEC, SEC, SCA, and cleanroom suites

Source note: w4-b-15 · Checked November 9, 2026

PEC (primary engineering control) protects critical sites with ISO Class 5 air, for example a qualified hood or isolator. SEC (secondary engineering control) is the environment surrounding the PEC. A segregated compounding area (SCA) is an unclassified but designated space with a properly installed ISO 5 PEC, used only for Category 1 CSPs under the applicable conditions.

AreaTypical required environment / purpose
ISO 5 PECDirect critical-site protection / first air
ISO 7 buffer roomClassified compounding room for typical nonhazardous Category 2/3 cleanroom suites
ISO 8 ante-roomTypical ante-room for a positive-pressure ISO 7 buffer room
ISO 7 ante-roomApplies with a negative-pressure hazardous-drug buffer-room arrangement
SCADefined, uncluttered, unclassified area with an ISO 5 PEC; Category 1 only

In an SCA, a sink may be inside the defined area but must be at least one meter from the PEC. A hood placed next to an ordinary sink or on a cluttered bench is not automatically a compliant SCA.

Some appropriately designed pharmaceutical isolators have narrower placement allowances; do not apply those exceptions to all devices called CAI or CACI. Hazardous sterile compounding also requires USP <800> and suitable containment.

Exam trap: ISO 5 air inside the hood is necessary but does not by itself establish the required surrounding room or facility approval.

Official and supporting sources: USP FAQ, facilities and sinks · M.G.L. c.112 §39G

5. First air, room pressure, certification

Source note: w4-b-16 · Checked November 9, 2026

First air means unobstructed HEPA-filtered air reaching a critical site. Do not block it by placing hands or supplies between the filter and a vial stopper, needle or exposed sterile component. Dynamic smoke studies visualize airflow under relevant working conditions; visible clean airflow on an empty hood is not enough by itself.

MeasurementCommon USP <797> minimum or rule
ISO 8 ante-roomAt least 20 total air changes per hour (ACPH)
ISO 7 roomAt least 30 total ACPH
HVAC contributionAt least 15 ACPH through ceiling HEPA filtration for the described classified spaces
Positive room-pressure differentialAt least 0.020 inch water column relative to applicable adjacent areas
PEC and classified SEC certificationInitially and at least every 6 months, and after changes that may affect performance

Pressure is monitored continuously; results must be reviewed and documented at least daily on compounding days. Temperature and humidity targets for facility comfort or microbial control are not blanket replacements for manufacturer storage requirements.

Example: A hood still passes an ISO particle count, but smoke testing shows that the worker’s supplies obstruct first air. The process needs correction despite the ISO number.

Exam trap: ACPH and room differential are not requirements imposed identically on an SCA. Certification every six months is not product sterility testing.

Official and supporting sources: USP FAQ, room certification and pressure · USP <797> reference

6. Personnel qualification and media-fill

Source note: w4-b-17 · Checked November 9, 2026

Every person compounding sterile preparations needs training and demonstrated competence. USP <797> distinguishes core principles, garbing/hand hygiene, and aseptic manipulation (including media-fill, fingertip/thumb sampling and a direct compounding area surface check).

Personnel / competencyTypical minimum
Core principles and skillsInitially and at least every 12 months
Initial garbing with fingertip/thumb samplingThree separate successful complete garbing evaluations in succession
Initial aseptic manipulation evaluationOne successful evaluation including media-fill and associated sampling
Category 1/2 active compoundersGarbing and aseptic manipulation at least every 6 months
Category 3 active compoundersAt least every 3 months
Direct oversight but not personally compoundingCorresponding competency at least every 12 months

Important distinction: Initial garbing requires three successive full performances, not putting on garb once and touching three plates. Initial media-fill under revised USP <797> is not automatically a separate three-test requirement; older Massachusetts media-fill advisory recommendations must not be mistaken for the newer USP minimum.

Media-fill simulates the most difficult real aseptic process using microbiological growth media. Failed evaluations require investigation, corrective action and documentation; consult additional facility/state requirements rather than assuming a uniform automatic stop-work rule for every individual failure. Personnel making immediate-use only preparations follow the immediate-use task-based competency provisions.

Exam trap: “3 times initially” belongs to garbing qualification in current USP; Category 3’s “every 3 months” belongs to ongoing compounder assessments.

Official and supporting sources: USP FAQ, personnel qualifications

7. Garbing, sterile gloves, cleaning and sporicides

Source note: w4-b-18 · Checked November 9, 2026

Good aseptic technique protects the product from contamination introduced by personnel, supplies and surfaces. Wash hands and forearms to the elbows with soap and water for at least 30 seconds, dry appropriately, and don garb according to the facility’s documented contamination-reducing sequence. Artificial nails, exposed jewelry and cosmetics can pose contamination problems.

Gloves must be sterile, and appropriate sterile 70% isopropyl alcohol (IPA) is used as required, allowing it to dry. Category 3 requires enhanced sterile garb and skin coverage; do not apply Category 1/2 gown-reuse allowances to Category 3.

Cleaning removes soil; disinfection destroys many microorganisms; sporicidal agents address resistant spores. IPA alone is not a complete sporicidal cleaning program. Use agents compatible with surfaces and follow manufacturer contact times.

Minimum cleaning/disinfection frequencies vary by PEC, floor, wall and other surface, with some tasks performed on each day of compounding and others monthly. Category 3 requires more frequent sporicidal disinfection for certain surfaces than Category 1/2, so a single “every surface weekly” rule is inaccurate.

Exam trap: Never touch sterile critical sites with uncovered skin or assume spraying IPA directly onto visible dirt replaces cleaning.

Official and supporting sources: USP FAQ, cleaning and hygiene

8. Environmental monitoring and MA Policy 2026-03

Source note: w4-b-19 · Checked November 9, 2026

Environmental monitoring (EM) asks whether the air and work surfaces remain suitably controlled. Nonviable particle counting is different from sampling living microorganisms. Volumetric viable-air sampling needs an appropriate sampler and typically 1 m³ / 1,000 L per monitoring location; a settle plate is not an equivalent substitute.

ActivityCategory 1/2Category 3
Viable active air samplingAt least every 6 monthsAt least monthly; additional startup conditions
Surface samplingAt least monthlyAt least weekly; additional after-batch PEC sampling where required
Engineering certificationInitially / at least every 6 months / after significant changesSame baseline certification; not a replacement for product tests

Major Massachusetts update: Board Policy 2026-03, adopted September 3, 2026, supersedes Policy 2023-09. It applies to sterile compounding pharmacies, including institutional and nonresident pharmacies dispensing into, within or from Massachusetts. The MOR/PIC or pharmacist designee must notify the Board of an action-level EM result within one business day after receiving the microbiology report and begin the required risk assessment/remediation response.

ClassViable air action levelSurface action level
ISO 5> 1 CFU / m³> 3 CFU / sample device
ISO 7> 10 CFU / m³> 5 CFU / sample device
ISO 8> 100 CFU / m³> 50 CFU / sample device

The policy also sets nonviable particle thresholds and consequences. A significant loss of control (>15 CFU in an ISO 5 area) triggers recall of CSPs within BUD, adverse-event surveillance, prescriber contact and microbiologist engagement. Nonviable particle action levels generally require suspension until full remediation and recertification; viable excursions have tightly restricted continuation pathways depending on the affected room and risk assessment.

Exam trap: Reporting an environmental excursion is not the same as correcting it. Do not use superseded Policy 2023-09's counting/reporting assumptions.

Official and supporting sources: MA Policy 2026-03 · 247 CMR 20.06 · USP FAQ, air/surface monitoring

9. Sterilization, sterility testing and endotoxins

Source note: w4-b-20 · Checked November 9, 2026

Sterilization is a validated process to render a preparation sterile. Sterility testing checks samples for microbial growth; a passing sample does not guarantee every unit is free from contamination. Endotoxin testing measures bacterial pyrogens, an independent risk especially important for injectable drugs.

Terminal sterilization (after filling and closing, e.g., validated steam/dry heat/irradiation) is preferred when compatible with the drug and container. Sterilizing filtration is an aseptic-processing technique, not terminal sterilization. When nonsterile starting ingredients/devices are used, applicable limits include sterilization within the chapter's specified six-hour window after completion of preparation; verify the exact triggering situation rather than borrowing the immediate-use four-hour clock.

A suitable filter requires validated compatibility and integrity testing. Merely passing through a nominal 0.2 or 0.22 μm filter does not automatically remove endotoxins or justify an extended BUD. Category 2 injectable CSPs from nonsterile components assigned BUDs that require sterility testing have specified endotoxin-testing requirements; not all ophthalmic or noninjectable CSPs are subject to the same endotoxin rule.

Example: A sterile-filtered injectable may pass sterility testing but fail endotoxin specifications. Both are independent release risks.

Exam trap: The four-hour immediate-use start deadline and six-hour nonsterile-component sterilization requirement are completely different clocks.

Official and supporting sources: USP <797> · USP FAQ, endotoxin and release testing

10. USP <797> full BUD matrix

Source note: w4-b-21 · Checked November 9, 2026

These are maximum BUDs only when the preparation meets the listed category/process/testing conditions and appropriate stability limits. More restrictive product-specific data, component expiration, container integrity, or approved labeling may shorten them. Storage temperatures: controlled room temperature 20–25°C, refrigerated 2–8°C, frozen −25 to −10°C.

Category / processRoom tempRefrigeratedFrozen
Category 112 hours24 hoursNo separate frozen extension in the Category 1 table
Category 2 aseptic; ≥1 nonsterile component; no passed sterility test1 day4 days45 days
Category 2 aseptic; only sterile components; no passed sterility test4 days10 days45 days
Category 2 aseptic; required sterility test passed30 days45 days60 days
Category 2 terminal sterilization; no passed sterility test14 days28 days45 days
Category 2 terminal sterilization; required sterility test passed45 days60 days90 days
Category 3 aseptic; all enhanced requirements/tests met60 days90 days120 days
Category 3 terminal sterilization; all enhanced requirements/tests met90 days120 days180 days

Examples: A Category 1 CSP compounded at 10 AM cannot simply be held refrigerated for 10 days. A Category 2 CSP made aseptically from only sterile ingredients with no passed sterility test may have a maximum of 4 days at room temperature or 10 days refrigerated, subject to stability and all other limits.

BUDs are not additive: 45 days frozen plus 10 days refrigerated does not automatically create a permissible 55-day period. Category 3 extended dates require formulation-specific stability-indicating information, matching container materials, enhanced environmental and personnel controls, and applicable sterility/endotoxin/particulate testing.

Exam trap: “Sterility tested” does not equal “stable for months.” Category 2 and Category 3 long BUDs need more than simply passing a test.

Official and supporting sources: USP <797> Tables 12–14 · USP FAQ, BUD limits · Independent table explanation

11. Punctured vials and compounded multidose CSPs

Source note: w4-b-22 · Checked November 9, 2026

The time remaining for an opened ingredient container is different from the final CSP’s BUD. A manufacturer-produced single-dose container first punctured under ISO Class 5 air may generally be used for up to 12 hours after entry while labeled storage conditions are maintained, unless the applicable manufacturer or stability limit is shorter. An opened single-dose ampoule with closure removed must not be stored.

A conventionally manufactured multi-dose parenteral container is generally assigned 28 days after first puncture unless its labeling specifies otherwise. Never apply “28 days” automatically to every eye drop, implant, or single-dose product.

Compounded multidose CSPs have additional preservative-effectiveness, container, labeling, category and BUD limitations. A specific limited pathway exists for single-patient nonpreserved topical or ophthalmic CSPs, but it is not general permission to reuse every nonpreserved injectable.

Example: A manufacturer's single-dose vial was first punctured in an ISO 5 PEC at 10 AM; under qualifying conditions its remaining material cannot be used beyond 10 PM merely because its printed manufacturer expiration is next year.

Exam trap: Do not confuse time after vial puncture, CSP BUD, and administration/hang time.

Official and supporting sources: USP FAQ, single-/multi-dose containers

12. Release checks and MA rapid sterility policy

Source note: w4-b-23 · Checked November 9, 2026

Before a CSP is released, qualified personnel must check the product against the order and label, verify calculations/process checks, examine its appearance for visible particles, haze, discoloration, leakage or container damage, and apply any required sterility/endotoxin results. A CSP not released on the day prepared needs appropriate visual reinspection before release.

Critical Massachusetts difference: The USP <797> FAQ describes certain conditional release before sterility testing results when safeguards exist. But Massachusetts Board Policy 2025-01 states the Board’s position that a CSP requiring sterility testing may not be dispensed before negative test results have been received. The policy cites proposed 247 CMR 17.17(2) but is itself an adopted Board policy. Do not treat the draft as enacted regulation.

Rapid microbial methods may be an alternative to USP <71> only if appropriately validated to be noninferior/equivalent, with method suitability verified for the specific formulation, strength and matrix. A vendor’s assertion that a test is “rapid” is not enough. The MOR/PIC must review relevant validation/suitability reports, and changed formulations may need repeat suitability evaluations.

Example: An injectable batch requires sterility testing. The pharmacist wants to dispense before test results because the patient has a scheduled appointment. MA’s 2025-01 policy does not permit that shortcut for CSPs requiring the test.

Exam trap: National USP minimum and Massachusetts Board policy differ at the early-release boundary. Apply both; do not use USP's conditional allowance to ignore Massachusetts's position.

Official and supporting sources: MA Policy 2025-01 · USP FAQ, conditional release · Draft vs. enacted regulations

13. Labeling, traceability, reporting and recalls

Source note: w4-b-24 · Checked November 9, 2026

CSP labeling must accurately communicate product identity, ingredients/concentrations, strength, route, volume, assigned BUD, storage and applicable warnings, internal tracking and pharmacy information. Massachusetts M.G.L. c.94C §21 requires a label notifying users and practitioners that the medicine is a sterile compounded drug preparation; certain licensed sterile-compounding pharmacies must provide a pharmacist-access phone line staffed at least 56 hours per week, subject to the inpatient institutional exception.

Accountability documentation under M.G.L. c.112 §39D connects the prescribed patient, preparation, lot/expiry and compounder. Keep separate records for normal production, incident/CQI review, failed environmental monitoring, defective preparations and other reporting. A single record does not automatically satisfy all these duties.

MA eventReporting clock / source
Action-level environmental resultWithin 1 business day of receiving microbiology report (Policy 2026-03)
Defective sterile compounded preparation dispensed into/within/from MAGenerally within 7 calendar days of discovery (247 CMR 20.06 and Board reporting guide)
Failed PEC/SEC certificationWithin 7 business days under Board reporting overview
Defective preparation recallImmediate recall when known/suspected; defective log available to Board within 7 days, retain at least 10 years (M.G.L. c.112 §39D(e))

Example: One CSP lot fails sterility. Quarantine remaining stock, investigate other affected lots and recipients, recall where indicated, notify prescribers and regulators under the applicable requirements, document the defect, and implement CAPA. One passing repeat test does not erase an earlier confirmed failure.

Exam trap: 7 calendar days, 7 business days, 1 business day and the 10-year defective-log retention rule belong to different obligations.

Official and supporting sources: M.G.L. c.94C §21 · M.G.L. c.112 §39D · 247 CMR 20.06 · 2026 Board reporting overview

14. Implantable infusion pumps: stability during infusion

Source note: w5-a-12 · Checked November 9, 2026

Massachusetts Board Policy 2024-02 addresses compounded sterile drugs placed into implantable infusion pumps for administration over long periods. The compounding BUD is not automatically proof that a drug remains stable and compatible throughout an extended infusion.

The pharmacy must ensure that the drug combination maintains potency, stability and compatibility for the full intended infusion duration and temperature exposure, supported by FDA-approved use, credible published evidence, or appropriate direct testing. Consider the drugs and excipients, pump materials, device compatibility, body temperature and reasonable fever-related variations.

Example: A formulation has stability information at 20–25°C but will be infused from an implanted pump at approximately body temperature for several weeks. Do not assume the room-temperature data prove extended in-pump stability. Evaluate the actual pump, medication, thermal conditions and infusion period.

Exam trap: USP <797> administration is outside the compounding chapter’s main scope; Massachusetts nonetheless expressly expects the pharmacy to evaluate extended infusion stability for implantable pumps.

Official and supporting sources: MA Policy 2024-02

15. Source hierarchy: USP, policies, enacted law, drafts

Source note: w5-a-11 · Checked November 9, 2026

Always identify the legal authority before memorizing a rule. Enacted laws include M.G.L. c.112 §§39F–39I and M.G.L. c.94C §21. Enacted regulations include 247 CMR 6.00 and 20.00. Adopted Board policies include 2026-03 (environmental monitoring), 2025-01 (rapid sterility methods), 2024-02 (implantable pumps), and 2021-04 (CE).

USP <797> is a technical standard made officially effective on November 1, 2023. Massachusetts law explicitly directs licensed sterile-compounding pharmacies to follow current applicable USP standards.

Proposed 247 CMR 17.00 still appears in the Board's draft regulations listing. Its appearance on an inspection checklist is not proof that it has been promulgated. Likewise, older Board advisories or inspection tools can remain online after newer guidance is adopted.

Massachusetts CE distinction: Policy 2021-04 (revised Sept. 4, 2025) requires 5 hours of sterile-compounding CE each calendar year for pharmacists who perform, oversee, supervise or finally verify CSPs, even temporarily. It may count toward the generally required 20 hours; do not mix it up with the 3 hours for complex nonsterile compounding.

10 Original MPJE-Style Practice Questions

Choose one of four answers, then select Check answer to reveal explanations and update your score. Use Try again to restart. These are original educational questions, not recalled exam items.

Question 1. When does administration of a qualifying immediate-use CSP need to begin?
  1. Within 4 hours after preparation is finished
  2. Within 4 hours after preparation starts
  3. Within 12 hours of receiving the prescription
  4. Within 24 hours if refrigerated

Correct answer: B.

A: No. The clock does not start after the compounder finishes.

B: Correct. The limit runs from the beginning of preparation.

C: That is not the immediate-use clock.

D: Immediate-use preparations do not gain a 24-hour refrigerated exemption.

Question 2. What is the difference between docking a proprietary bag/vial system for immediate use and docking it in advance?
  1. Neither activity can be compounding
  2. Both always require a Category 3 facility
  3. Immediate labeled docking/activation may be outside <797>, but advance docking for future activation counts as compounding
  4. Advance docking is always exempt if the products are sterile

Correct answer: C.

A: False. Advance docking is regulated compounding.

B: False. Not all docking requires Category 3 conditions.

C: Correct. Timing and the manufacturer-label conditions matter.

D: False. Advance docking requires appropriate ISO 5 conditions.

Question 3. A Category 1 CSP has no shorter applicable stability restriction. What is the USP default maximum BUD at controlled room temperature?
  1. 4 hours
  2. 12 hours
  3. 4 days
  4. 10 days

Correct answer: B.

A: Four hours is the immediate-use administration-start window.

B: Correct. Category 1 has a 12-hour room-temperature maximum.

C: Four days is one Category 2 aseptic sterile-component scenario.

D: Ten days is the refrigerated Category 2 aseptic sterile-component limit.

Question 4. An aseptically compounded Category 2 CSP is prepared only from sterile components without a passed sterility test. Its usual maximum refrigerated BUD is:
  1. 24 hours
  2. 4 days
  3. 10 days
  4. 45 days

Correct answer: C.

A: 24 hours is Category 1 refrigerated.

B: Four days is the Category 2 room-temperature figure for only sterile components.

C: Correct. 10 days is the refrigerated maximum for this Category 2 pathway, assuming other conditions.

D: 45 days is the frozen limit for the same processing pathway.

Question 5. How many initial successful complete garbing evaluations with fingertip/thumb testing are required by current USP <797>?
  1. One
  2. Two
  3. Three separate successful evaluations in succession
  4. Three fingertip samples after one handwashing/garbing event

Correct answer: C.

A: Initial aseptic manipulation is not identical to initial garbing.

B: Not the required initial count.

C: Correct. Each of the three is a separate complete garbing session.

D: Incorrect. One garbing event and three samples do not meet the initial requirement.

Question 6. A pharmacist temporarily performs final verification of CSPs in Massachusetts. Which annual specialized CE requirement applies?
  1. 3 complex nonsterile hours only
  2. 5 sterile compounding hours; they may count toward general annual CE
  3. No specialized CE because the duty was temporary
  4. 8 extra hours in addition to 20

Correct answer: B.

A: Three hours relates to complex nonsterile duties.

B: Correct. The 2025-revised Board CE policy includes temporary activities.

C: Incorrect. Temporary service is expressly included.

D: Incorrect. No universal eight-extra-hour requirement is established.

Question 7. On September 2026 MA policy terms, when must a sterile pharmacy notify the Board of an action-level microbiology report?
  1. Within one business day after receipt of the report
  2. Within seven calendar days of sampling
  3. Only when the next routine inspection occurs
  4. Only after the repeat sample is also abnormal

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Policy 2026-03 explicitly states one business day from report receipt.

B: This confuses other reporting clocks.

C: Incorrect. Notification is not postponed until inspection.

D: Incorrect. First action-level results trigger the required response.

Question 8. A CSP requires sterility testing. May the Massachusetts pharmacy dispense before receiving negative results just because national USP guidance describes conditional early release?
  1. Yes, with no restrictions
  2. Yes, always if the patient agrees
  3. No. MA Board Policy 2025-01 states it may not be dispensed until negative results are received
  4. Yes, if the technician initials the label

Correct answer: C.

A: Incorrect; the Massachusetts policy is more restrictive.

B: Patient consent does not waive the policy.

C: Correct. Distinguish MA Board position from USP’s national conditional release discussion.

D: A technician’s signature does not replace release requirements.

Question 9. A manufactured single-dose vial first punctured under ISO 5 conditions has no shorter labeled restriction. The usual maximum time for retaining its contents is:
  1. Four hours
  2. 12 hours
  3. 28 days
  4. 90 days

Correct answer: B.

A: Four hours is not the normal ISO 5 single-dose-container in-use limit.

B: Correct. Up to 12 hours after initial puncture under qualifying conditions.

C: 28 days usually relates to manufactured multidose parenteral containers.

D: 90 days is not a general punctured-vial allowance.

Question 10. Which statement about Massachusetts 247 CMR 17.00 is accurate in November 2026?
  1. It replaced USP <797> as an enacted regulation
  2. It is listed as a draft; enacted statutes, regulations, USP requirements and current policies must be distinguished
  3. It eliminated sterile pharmacy licensure
  4. It abolished environmental monitoring reports

Correct answer: B.

A: Incorrect. It is not confirmed as enacted and USP still matters.

B: Correct. Draft status is explicit on Mass.gov.

C: False; M.G.L. c.112 §39F/39G require licensure.

D: False; enacted 247 CMR 20 and 2026-03 policy govern reporting.
