How to solve a compounding question

A compounded nonsterile preparation (CNSP) is a patient-oriented drug preparation made by combining or changing ingredients or drug products without making a sterile product. Examples include some individualized creams, suspensions, and capsules. It is not the same as simply counting tablets into a vial.

Work through this sequence: activity → patient need → legal route → MA license → ingredients → records/label → BUD.

  1. Is this ordinary manufacturer-directed reconstitution, or does it count as compounding?
  2. Is there a valid patient-specific prescription (or allowed limited advance preparation before such a prescription)?
  3. If a comparable FDA-approved product exists, what prescriber-documented change is medically important?
  4. Is the preparation complex or hazardous, requiring a separate Massachusetts specialty license?
  5. Are ingredients, environment, formulation, record, BUD, and labeling acceptable?
Do not memorize a draft as law: “247 CMR 18.00: Non-Sterile Compounding” is currently listed by Mass.gov among draft Board regulations. For current exam study, use enacted statutes such as M.G.L. c.112 §§39D, 39F, 39H, the enacted regulations that apply, and current Board Policy 2023-07. A Board policy and a statute are different types of authority.

Authority: Board Policy 2023-07 · Board draft-regulation list.

1. Compounding vs. reconstitution vs. repackaging

Compounding means changing ingredients or a drug product to create a preparation. Reconstitution means adding diluent to make a medicine ready to administer or dispense. Repackaging means moving a drug into a different container without necessarily changing its formulation.

ActivityCompounding?Why
Add water to an antibiotic exactly as its FDA-approved label directsNoManufacturer-directed preparation
Change the diluent or concentration beyond label instructionsGenerally yesThe manufacturer-directed exception no longer applies
Add a flavoring agent to a manufactured drugYesMA Policy 2023-07 explicitly considers it compounding
Split a nonhazardous tabletNoOrdinary splitting is not compounding
Use a premeasured compounding kitYesThe kit does not waive USP compounding requirements

Example: A pharmacy mixes a commercially made antibiotic powder with the specified volume of water according to its label. This is not compounding. Adding flavor afterward is a separate compounding activity.

Exam trap: Do not assume any preparation performed behind a pharmacy counter is compounding, or that a manufacturer kit automatically falls outside USP standards.

2. Federal 503A is a conditional exemption

503A is a federal pathway for eligible human drug compounding by licensed pharmacists in state-licensed pharmacies or federal facilities, or licensed physicians. If all relevant conditions are met, the drug is exempt from certain federal requirements: premarket new-drug approval, cGMP under FD&C Act §501(a)(2)(B), and labeling with adequate directions for use.

That does not mean the FDA has approved the compounded product, that insanitary conditions are acceptable, or that state pharmacy law stops applying. A valid individual-patient prescription is central; limited anticipatory compounding is permitted based on an established history of prescriptions, but advance preparation is not blanket authorization to dispense anonymous office stock.

Individual patient prescriptionGenerally required, with limited anticipatory preparationMay or may not be required under federal law
cGMP exemptionYes, if all 503A conditions are metNo

Example: Preparing a small amount of a formula based on routinely received patient prescriptions may qualify as anticipatory work, but a Massachusetts pharmacy needs a patient-specific prescription before dispensing it to the patient.

3. Bulk APIs, FDA registration, and certificates of analysis

An API (active pharmaceutical ingredient) is the ingredient that provides the intended drug effect. If a compounder uses bulk API under 503A, eligibility depends on an applicable USP/NF monograph, whether the ingredient is in an FDA-approved drug, or an applicable FDA 503A bulk-substances route. Interim FDA enforcement policies can have additional conditions.

The bulk substance must come from an FDA-registered manufacturer and have a valid certificate of analysis (COA). A COA provides information about testing and specifications; it cannot make an otherwise prohibited ingredient permissible.

Example: A pharmacy receives “pure powder 100%” from a website but has no valid COA or evidence of FDA-registered manufacture. It should not treat that material as an acceptable 503A API merely because the label sounds pharmaceutical. Distinguish pure API from a diluted trituration when calculating dose.

Exam trap: FDA-registered manufacturer ≠ FDA-approved compounded preparation; a valid COA is necessary but not independently sufficient.

Original W4-B-3 · FDA 503A Bulk Drug Substances.

4. Copies of commercially available drugs

Massachusetts generally prohibits making a compounded copy of a comparable commercially available FDA-approved drug merely for convenience or lower cost. A patient-specific significant difference must meet an individualized medical need determined and documented by the prescribing practitioner. Examples may include removing an allergenic dye or a clinically needed change in dosage form, strength, or delivery mechanism.

Reasoning example: “Patient needs a dye-free formulation due to documented allergy” may support compounding when properly prescribed and justified. “This would be cheaper” is not a sufficient significant difference.

FDA's analysis of whether a product is commercially available may be affected by an FDA-listed shortage, but a temporary shortage, local stock-out, and a prescriber-documented individualized medical need are not interchangeable. Check the current product-specific federal and state rules rather than assuming that shortage authorizes all copies. FDA reiterated copy restrictions in April 2026.

Original W4-B-4 · MA Policy 2020-02 · FDA April 2026 clarification.

5. Do not confuse the two “5% rules”

503A interstate distribution 5% concerns the relationship between a compounding state's FDA MOU status and out-of-state prescription-order distribution. FDA currently describes the 2020 standard MOU as suspended and defers enforcement of the statutory limit until the effective date of a final rule and a revised MOU. The underlying statute still exists; a postponed enforcement policy is not statutory repeal.

DEA practitioner distribution 5% is a different federal controlled-substance distribution provision, generally involving dosage units distributed by a practitioner to another practitioner under specified conditions. It uses another denominator and addresses a different activity.

Exam trap: Never substitute the DEA denominator (controlled-substance dosage units) for the FDA 503A denominator (prescription orders). Do not assume Massachusetts has signed a revised MOU without checking.

Original W4-B-5 · FDA interstate MOU status.

6. Massachusetts ordinary vs. complex nonsterile compounding

Most Massachusetts pharmacies can perform noncomplex compounding while complying with applicable requirements. Complex nonsterile compounding requires a separate pharmacy specialty license under M.G.L. c.112 §39F(b), with licensing requirements in §39H.

MA Board Policy 2023-07 includes complex calculations (such as multiple aliquots, loss on drying, salt conversion), hazardous drug compounding, and forms such as capsules, suppositories, troches, lollipops, transdermal dosage forms, tablets, modified-release forms, and other specified systemic-delivery preparations.

Example: A community drugstore proposes custom capsules. It cannot decide this is ordinary compounding merely because the powder ingredients are nonhazardous; the Massachusetts Board lists capsules among complex dosage forms. The appropriate specialty license and other requirements must be satisfied.

Two classifications, two purposes: USP <795> uses water activity and dosage-form properties for default BUDs. Massachusetts uses the activity/formulation risk to determine complex compounding licensure. A 180-day USP BUD category does not mean the pharmacy can prepare that dosage form without a specialty license.

Original W4-B-6 · M.G.L. c.112 §39F · §39H · MA Policy 2023-07 §VII.

7. Flavoring, manufacturer kits, and tablet splitting

MA Policy 2023-07 lets a pharmacist add flavoring to a Schedule VI medication at the request of the patient or agent, even without a prescriber request, if the change is documented appropriately. The pharmacist still needs relevant stability/compatibility information and must not change the dose or concentration beyond permissible parameters. Do not generalize that permission to Schedules II–V or to arbitrary formulation changes.

A premeasured compounding kit is still compounding. Ordinary nonhazardous tablet splitting is not; manipulation of hazardous drugs must meet applicable USP <800> requirements. Never assume a modified-release tablet can safely be split or crushed just because nonhazardous splitting may be outside the compounding definition.

Example: A parent asks for strawberry flavor in a Schedule VI oral antibiotic after it has been reconstituted as labeled. The label-directed reconstitution itself is not compounding, but the flavor addition is. Record and label the change as applicable.

Original W4-B-7 · MA Policy 2023-07 §§II(B), III.

8. Facility, quality control, designated person, CE

For noncomplex compounding, MA expects a designated compounding area; Policy 2023-07 uses “should” for at least 10 square feet of counter space (not an unconditional mandatory minimum). Work surfaces must be cleanable and kept sanitary; separate the area from water sources, control cross-contamination, and maintain scales and equipment. Scales/balances must be sealed at least once per calendar year.

The USP <795> designated person responsibility may be fulfilled by the Massachusetts pharmacy's Manager of Record (MOR) or an on-site licensed pharmacist. Board enforcement discretion on certain cleaning schedules and room-temperature monitoring for noncomplex operations is conditional: keep the pharmacy clean, sanitary, and within proper storage conditions.

For hazardous-drug compounding, the Board requires the complex specialty license and appropriate USP <800> containment. HD and non-HD nonsterile compounding cannot share the same room or primary engineering control. The Board's policy says a dedicated complex-nonsterile room will be required generally, while a dedicated HD compounding room is currently required; do not convert future wording into an already-effective universal room rule.

Specialized CE: A pharmacist who performs, oversees, supervises, or finally verifies complex nonsterile compounding must complete 3 hours of complex-nonsterile CE each calendar year. These count toward, rather than add to, the usual 20 annual CE hours, subject to the Board's specific CE requirements. Training and competency assessments are still necessary even if CE is complete.

Original W4-B-8 · MA Policy 2023-07 §§IV, VII · MA Policy 2021-04 (revised Sep 2025) · M.G.L. c.112 §24A.

9. Master Formulation Record (MFR) vs. Compounding Record (CR)

Think of MFR = recipe/instructions and CR = what actually happened this time. The MFR includes components, intended amounts, calculations, preparation sequence, equipment, container, checks, storage, and justified BUD. The CR identifies the specific compounding event, actual ingredients/quantities, component lot and expiration, preparation date, personnel, assigned BUD, and quality checks.

Master Formulation RecordCompounding Record
“Mix 2 g API into this vehicle, following these steps.”“On Nov 9, Technician A used lots X/Y, measured actual quantities and Pharmacist B verified.”
Standard formula and expected final appearanceActual batch, prescription, observations, checks, and assigned BUD
Reusable across preparationsRequired record of each preparation

MA allows a properly detailed combined log to serve as both MFR and CR for some premeasured compounding kits or flavor additions. Routine manufacturer-directed reconstitution has a recommended reconstitution log, which is different from the mandatory CR for compounded preparations. A pharmacy must also maintain a defective drug log for potentially defective compounds; additional reporting requirements apply to defective complex preparations.

Exam trap: Having a stored MFR does not prove a particular batch was made and checked correctly.

Original W4-B-9 · MA Policy 2023-07 §§III, VI · 247 CMR 20.06 reporting.

10. How to assign a BUD safely

BUD (beyond-use date) is the last time a compounded preparation is recommended for use under its assigned conditions. It is not the manufacturer's product expiration date and not a prescription's legal validity period.

  1. Identify the actual formulation: dosage form, active and other ingredients, water activity, preservation, container, and storage.
  2. Look for an applicable USP–NF compounded-preparation monograph and valid preparation-specific stability information.
  3. Determine the appropriate USP <795> default limit if those sources do not establish a different supported period.
  4. Choose a shorter BUD if chemical/physical stability, microbial risk, component expiration, or packaging demands it.
  5. Record the BUD and appropriate storage on the compounding record and label.

Example: A nonpreserved water-based pediatric suspension with no valid formulation-specific stability data generally has a maximum default BUD of 14 days refrigerated, not “one year from dispensing.” It may need less than 14 days if another stability limit applies.

Exam trap: Making a new label, transferring to a new bottle, or changing storage locations does not restart the BUD clock.

Original W4-B-10 · USP <795> official educational open-forum presentation, Table 4 · MA Policy 2023-07 §V.

Exceptions: Do not assign 35 days just because the formula has a preservative on its ingredient list; the relevant criteria must be met. High-yield Massachusetts MPJE traps

  • Draft regulation ≠ enacted law: 247 CMR 18.00 is listed as draft; do not quote it as current binding regulation.
  • Complex license ≠ every compounding act: The license applies to complex preparations, including Board-identified capsules and HD compounding.
  • Cost ≠ unique medical need: Significant difference must be patient-specific and prescriber-documented.
  • 503A exemption ≠ FDA approval or permission to ignore safety standards.
  • Patient-specific Rx before dispensing: limited advance preparation is different from stock for unnamed patients.
  • Flavoring counts as compounding: Schedule VI patient-request exception has boundaries and documentation requirements.
  • 10 square feet: Board policy says should, whereas sanitary practice is required.
  • MFR ≠ CR: one is the formula; the other is the actual event.
  • 3 hours CE: complex-nonsterile CE is included within the ordinary annual 20 when applicable.
  • BUD is not expiration: remember 14 / 35 / 90 / 180, but assign shorter if required.
  • Compounded labeling: M.G.L. c.94C §21 requires labeling that identifies the preparation as a sterile or nonsterile compound; MA policy also addresses flavored-drug notation. For sterile/complex pharmacies, §21 includes additional contact-telephone conditions.

10 original MPJE-style practice questions

Try choosing an answer before opening the explanation. These are original teaching exercises, not recalled exam questions or a verified official exam bank.

Q1. A pharmacist adds water to an antibiotic exactly as the approved package insert directs. Is this nonsterile compounding?
  1. Yes, all reconstitution is compounding.
  2. No, manufacturer-directed reconstitution is excluded.
  3. Yes, but only if refrigerated.
  4. No, because antibiotics are exempt from all USP requirements.

Correct: B. A is too broad. C invents a refrigeration condition for the classification. D is false: reconstitution is not a universal exemption from relevant professional standards.

Q2. A commercially available tablet contains a dye the patient cannot tolerate. The prescriber documents a need for a dye-free formulation. Which is most accurate?
  1. Compounding is never permitted if any approved product exists.
  2. The pharmacist may substitute any compounded formulation without documentation.
  3. A documented, clinically significant patient-specific difference may justify compounding subject to all other rules.
  4. Price is the only permitted reason to compound a copy.

Correct: C. A ignores the exception. B lacks the needed prescriber-documented determination. D reverses Massachusetts Policy 2020-02: lower cost alone does not justify a copy.

Q3. A standard retail pharmacy wants to prepare nonhazardous individualized capsules. What is the main Massachusetts licensing issue?
  1. Capsules are categorically excluded from compounding.
  2. USP <795> replaces state specialty licenses.
  3. Only the individual pharmacist needs a personal renewal.
  4. Custom capsules are a Board-listed complex nonsterile dosage form; separate specialty pharmacy licensure is needed.

Correct: D. A is false; B confuses USP standards with state facility licensing; C omits the special pharmacy license required by M.G.L. c.112 §39F(b).

Q4. A pharmacist adds flavor to a manufactured Schedule VI suspension at a patient's request. What is the best next step?
  1. Treat it as a compounding activity, evaluate stability/concentration, and document/label appropriately.
  2. Treat it as legally identical to untouched manufacturer product.
  3. Discard the prescription and dispense without a record.
  4. Assume every Schedule II–V drug can be modified the same way.

Correct: A. B ignores the explicit compounding classification. C violates recordkeeping. D overextends the narrow Schedule VI policy permission.

Q5. With no applicable monograph or preparation-specific stability evidence, what is the general USP <795> maximum BUD for a qualifying preserved aqueous CNSP?
  1. 14 days only
  2. 35 days
  3. 90 days
  4. 180 days

Correct: B. A is the nonpreserved aqueous category; C is nonaqueous oral liquid; D is other nonaqueous preparations. Stability may require less than the listed maximum.

Q6. With no applicable monograph or preparation-specific stability evidence, what is the usual maximum BUD for a qualifying nonaqueous oral liquid?
  1. 14 days
  2. 35 days
  3. 90 days
  4. 180 days

Correct: C. A and B are aqueous categories; D applies to other nonaqueous dosage forms, not oral liquids. Confirm other stability and component limits.

Q7. Which document proves the ingredients and lot numbers actually used for one patient's compound?
  1. Only a generic MFR recipe
  2. Only the drug's original package insert
  3. A manufacturer's advertising brochure
  4. The compounding record for the particular preparation

Correct: D. The MFR in A sets out standard instructions, not what happened this time. B and C do not document the specific preparation.

Q8. A Massachusetts pharmacist directly performs complex nonsterile compounding. Which CE statement is correct?
  1. 3 complex-nonsterile CE hours per calendar year, which may count within the general 20 hours.
  2. 3 hours every two years, replacing other CE.
  3. 5 sterile CE hours always replace the complex nonsterile requirement.
  4. Passing the MPJE permanently replaces compounding CE.

Correct: A. B uses the wrong period and role. C confuses sterile with complex nonsterile CE. D is unsupported. See Board Policy 2021-04, last revised September 2025.

Q9. Which statement about FDA 503A is correct?
  1. Every 503A drug receives FDA premarket approval.
  2. If conditions are satisfied, specified federal cGMP, labeling, and approval requirements may not apply to the compounded drug.
  3. 503A allows unsanitary compounding if the patient signs consent.
  4. 503A permanently overrides Massachusetts specialty licensure.

Correct: B. A confuses exemption with approval. C is unsafe and contrary to remaining requirements. D ignores independent state law.

Q10. A student says the FDA 503A interstate 5% rule and DEA practitioner's 5% exception are interchangeable. What is correct?
  1. They both use total annual U.S. prescriptions.
  2. They both use nonsterile compound mass in grams.
  3. They apply to different activities and denominators; FDA also has a separate current enforcement policy for its interstate limit.
  4. Both provisions were eliminated when USP <795> was updated.

Correct: C. A and B invent a shared denominator. D is false. The FDA program concerns interstate compounding distribution and prescription orders; the distinct DEA provision relates to controlled-substance distribution and dosage units.
