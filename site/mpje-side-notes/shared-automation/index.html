Begin with the right workflow

Identify who physically handles the medication and who receives it: central fill prepares another pharmacy's prescriptions; remote processing handles data; telepharmacy lets a pharmacist provide specified remote functions while the dispensing store retains an on-site pharmacist; APS gives medicine to patients; PPA fills containers inside a licensed pharmacy; ADD supplies licensed care professionals at a facility.

Legal-source check: Massachusetts Policy 2025-02 now expressly identifies pickup lockers as APS devices. Enacted 247 CMR 9.10(1)(g) requires pharmacist consultation access at all APS dispensing hours, despite older policy language using “usual hours.” The LTCF/hospice source tables contain boundary display discrepancies, so exact 50-, 10- and 21-bed cases are flagged rather than guessed.

1. Shared pharmacy services: ownership, contracts and responsibility

Group: Shared Services & Central Filling · Original Korean unit: w4-a-13 · Legal review November 9, 2026

Shared pharmacy services means one participating pharmacist or pharmacy processes part of another pharmacy's patient-specific prescription workflow—for example, performing data processing, clinical review or central filling. The Massachusetts Board's Policy 2021-02 (revised February 6, 2025) establishes minimum safeguards for central fill, remote processing and telepharmacy.

Shared services must occur within the United States. Participating pharmacies may have the same owner OR a written contract clearly identifying the services and shared responsibilities. They must use an authorized secure common electronic file/technology and maintain privacy and security safeguards, including protecting PHI and not duplicating/downloading/removing the source database as described by the policy.

ConditionWhat it means in practice
Who is responsible?All participants remain jointly responsible for properly processed and filled prescriptions
Proof of each stepAudit trail must identify date/time and individual involved in every relevant step
Patient protectionValid patient-specific prescription/standing order, safe continuation of therapy, pharmacist responsibility as applicable
Patient choiceRetail pharmacy must notify patients and offer an opt-out mechanism
Written SOPsJointly develop/implement procedures; each site retains the parts applying to its work
Review and trainingDocumented staff training and joint SOP review at least annually
System outageContinuity-of-care plan if a participant cannot process or fill prescriptions

Example: Pharmacy A contracts with a separately owned licensed pharmacy to review data remotely. Different ownership does not categorically prohibit shared services; the written agreement, license compliance, secure data exchange, duties and safeguards must meet the policy.

MPJE Exam Trap: Same owner is not the only pathway. A contract does not transfer all responsibility to the other pharmacy, and shared computer access does not permit unapproved copies of the patient database.

Official sources: MA Board Policy 2021-02, §§III–VI

2. Central fill versus remote processing versus telepharmacy

Group: Shared Services & Central Filling · Original Korean unit: w4-a-14 · Legal review November 9, 2026

These three terms refer to different operations. Central fill handles the physical medication; remote processing handles permitted prescription information; telepharmacy allows defined remote pharmacist services with on-site safeguards.

ModelMain activityImportant Massachusetts boundary
Central fillOriginating pharmacy sends prescription information; second pharmacy physically prepares and returns filled RxFederally controlled II–V must return to originating pharmacy for final dispensing; no central-fill sterile/complex-nonsterile compounding under this MA policy
Remote processingInformation processing outside licensed premisesDoes not itself include final product verification or dispensing; §9.04(11) must be satisfied
TelepharmacyRemote pharmacist product verification, DUR, counseling and drug therapy monitoring via secure technologyDispensing pharmacy must have at least one licensed pharmacist on its premises

Central fill licensing: A central-fill pharmacy dispensing into, within, or from MA to participating pharmacies must hold appropriate MA resident or nonresident retail pharmacy licensure. Federal Schedules II–V must follow the DEA central-fill requirements; MA Schedule VI generally follows the same central-fill structure except where the policy specifies otherwise.

Shipping difference: Under Policy 2021-02, centrally filled Schedule II–V medicine returns to the original pharmacy for final dispensing. Eligible Schedule VI may ship directly to the patient—but PMP-reportable Schedule VI drugs (e.g. gabapentin) are excluded from that direct-shipping exception.

For remote work, 247 CMR 9.04(11) says a prescription processed outside licensed pharmacy premises cannot be dispensed unless the work was verified by a Massachusetts-licensed pharmacist or performed in a Board-licensed pharmacy. An appropriately licensed MA pharmacy technician may perform permitted remote-processing tasks within professional scope without a pharmacist physically at that technician's remote workstation; final product verification and dispensing still follow their own rules.

Example: A telepharmacist verifies final images while a community store is open. Massachusetts Policy 2021-02 does not permit a technician-only final dispensing store solely because a remote pharmacist is watching.

MPJE Exam Trap: Don't equate central-fill preparation with permission for direct-to-patient CII–V shipments, or remote data processing with final pharmacist verification. Central-fill sterile and complex nonsterile compounds are prohibited under the MA policy.

Official sources: MA Policy 2021-02 §§IV–VI · 247 CMR 9.04(11) · Policy 2020-15, 2025 scope revision · 21 CFR 1306.15 · 21 CFR 1306.27

3. APS / pickup lockers: patient-facing automation

Group: Automated Systems — APS, PPA and ADD · Original Korean unit: w4-a-15 · Legal review November 9, 2026

Automated Pharmacy System (APS) means a patient-facing automated system that can release medication to the patient or agent after correct identification and enable pharmacist consultation. Board Policy 2025-02 (Definitions), adopted August 7, 2025, expressly includes a “pickup locker” as an APS.

APS requirementWhat applies
MedicationsMay dispense Schedule IV–VI only—not Schedule II or III
LocationSame building as licensed pharmacy under enacted 247 CMR 9.10; Board's APS policy additionally specifies same physical address
Mounting and surveillanceSecured against/within a wall or floor and continuously recordable video surveillance
Patient identity and choiceTechnological verification of right patient/drug; patient can choose whether to use APS
Excluded productsNo drugs requiring refrigeration or reconstitution
Data recordElectronic audit trail of transactions and person to whom product was released
CounselingOffer opportunity for pharmacist consultation during ALL hours APS is operating for dispensing
ApprovalsPolicy 2022-07 (rev. Jan 2023) calls for Board written approval before use and relevant pharmacy modification materials

Schedule IV–V and additional PMP-reportable Schedule VI drugs such as gabapentin still require appropriate release-recipient documentation and PMP reporting; the policy also addresses DEA consultation where applicable. A location in a hospital or licensed health-care facility needs the relevant facility authority/approval.

Important change: Older Policy 2022-07 refers to consultation during the pharmacy's usual hours. But enacted 247 CMR 9.10(1)(g) (effective December 6, 2024) requires pharmacist consultation opportunity throughout all APS dispensing hours, including after-hours operation.

Example: A pharmacy wants to fill a refrigerated insulin prescription in a pickup locker that patients access at 1 AM. Even with adequate identity verification, refrigerated medicines may not be stocked in an APS under the present MA regulation. If any APS operates at 1 AM, pharmacist consultation access must be available then.

MPJE Exam Trap: A patient-facing locker is an APS, not an ADD. No CII/CIII or refrigerated/reconstitution products. The enacted regulation's consultation requirement for all operating hours controls over the older “usual hours” policy phrase.

Official sources: MA Policy 2025-02 definitions · Enacted 247 CMR 9.10(1)–(2) · MA Policy 2022-07, revised 2023

4. PPA: internal pharmacy counting, filling and packaging automation

Group: Automated Systems — APS, PPA and ADD · Original Korean unit: w4-a-16 · Legal review November 9, 2026

Pharmacy Processing Automation (PPA) is equipment used inside licensed pharmacy space to count/measure medication, place it in final patient containers or compliance packaging, and label it. It is not a patient-release locker.

247 CMR 9.11 requires technological safeguards such as barcodes, electronic verification, RFID, weight verification or similar tools to support drug accuracy. Pharmacy written procedures must address operation/maintenance, security, controlled-substance accountability, quality assurance, and stocking/return activities.

Automation problemRequired or appropriate response
PPA machine fills the wrong drug despite count accuracyStop affected workflow, investigate and correct the verification failure
Two manufacturer lots are commingled in one PPA cellMaintain an SOP able to quarantine all commingled lots if one is recalled
Barcode check passedDoes not automatically replace the pharmacist's legal final validation
Machine jams or software goes downUse documented maintenance and continuity/QA procedures

Example: A pharmacy combines lots A and B of the same tablet in one robot cell. Later, lot A is recalled. Because the individual dispensed tablets cannot easily be separated by lot, the pharmacy must follow its SOP to quarantine affected commingled stock appropriately rather than assume lot B is safely distinguishable.

MPJE Exam Trap: PPA prepares doses inside a licensed pharmacy. APS releases medication to patients. ADD dispenses to qualified healthcare staff at point of care. Automation does not by itself remove the pharmacist's professional duties.

Official sources: 247 CMR 9.11 · MA Policy 2025-02

5. ADD/ADC/ADM: healthcare-facility point-of-care devices

Group: Automated Systems — APS, PPA and ADD · Original Korean unit: w4-a-17 · Legal review November 9, 2026

Automated Dispensing Device (ADD)—also called an ADC or ADM—is a healthcare-professional-facing cabinet, typically in a licensed hospital or other healthcare facility. Under 247 CMR 9.12, its controlled-substance use must occur in a licensed healthcare facility, follow a valid patient-specific prescription/order, and satisfy all applicable laws and P&Ps.

The Board's Joint Policy 2019-02 (revised Jan 9, 2025) adds safeguards. Until dispensed for administration, medication in the ADD remains property of the pharmacy. The facility must restrict access to authorized licensed personnel, regularly update access lists, and implement QA, security, recordkeeping and drug accountability.

ADD record or controlMinimum condition
On-site records at pharmacyKeep relevant ADD records at least 2 years; make readily available
Machine and pharmacy traceabilityLocations, facility approval where required, cleaning/maintenance, delivery manifests
Transaction auditUser identity, location, action date/time, product/strength/form/quantity, patient ID
InventoryRegular reconciliation and investigation of losses
Dose packagingManufacturer-sealed or professionally compliant single-dose/individual packaging
Facility approvalsCheck licensing authority if needed; special machine-specific CSR/DEA registration for some remote/hospice/retail use

Example: A registered nurse retrieves an ordered drug from a hospital ADC for patient administration. The machine is not a patient pickup locker and the drug was not owned by the nurse: pharmacy-controlled inventory must be reconciled with patient-specific orders and transactions.

MPJE Exam Trap: An ADD is professional-facing in a licensed healthcare facility. A hospital's existence does not make all cabinet access lawful. Do not assume the pharmacy's ordinary registration automatically covers every separate device.

Official sources: 247 CMR 9.12 · Joint Policy 2019-02, revised 2025 · Policy 2025-02 definitions

6. Who can stock an ADD? Narrow Policy 2023-08 technician rules

Group: Automated Systems — APS, PPA and ADD · Original Korean unit: w4-a-18 · Legal review November 9, 2026

Policy 2023-08 applies only in a Massachusetts-licensed healthcare facility with an on-site pharmacy, where the ADD is at the same physical address as the facility's MCSR. It does not authorize every technician to stock outside nursing-home cabinets or patient-facing APS lockers.

Staff classificationWhat they may load / removeRequired limits
Pharmacy technician trainee (PTT)Pharmacist-verified Schedules III–VI; may transport/load and remove expired III–VIBarcode/electronic validation; pharmacist direction/supervision remains, although direct presence not always required
Licensed pharmacy technician (not PTT)Pharmacist-verified Schedules II–VIUnder pharmacist supervision, with electronic verification
Nationally certified licensed pharmacy technicianNon-patient-specific Schedule VI (excluding PMP-reportable drugs such as gabapentin) without prior pharmacist verification in the narrow caseManufacturer-sealed packaging, no further manipulation, electronic validation on removal and loading
Healthcare professional removing/administeringVerifies product on removal/administrationBarcode or comparable electronic check and patient-order compliance

For QA, a designated Board-licensed individual must monitor all ADD inventory transactions, user access, accuracy/trends and random physical audits. These permissions do not establish a general “tech-check-tech” rule allowing all technicians to perform independent final prescription verification.

Example: A PTT may load pharmacist-verified Schedule IV stock into a hospital ADD using barcode validation under the policy, but cannot load unverified Schedule II under this trainee pathway. A nationally certified technician's narrower Schedule VI exception cannot be extended to PMP-reportable gabapentin.

MPJE Exam Trap: PTT: verified III–VI. Licensed PT (nontrainee): verified II–VI. Nationally certified tech's unverified stock exception: qualifying non-PMP Schedule VI only. The facility, location and supervision conditions matter.

Official sources: Policy 2023-08, §§II–V · Policy 2020-15 scope of practice, revised 2025 · Joint ADD Policy 2019-02

7. Long-term care facility emergency kits and bed-based maximums

Group: Institutional Emergency and Hospice Supply · Original Korean unit: w4-a-19 · Legal review November 9, 2026

A Massachusetts long-term care facility (LTCF) emergency kit contains a limited number of medications for urgent patient needs until the pharmacy can supply a normal patient-specific prescription. Under DHCQ 18-6-679 (June 15, 2018), Schedule II–V analgesics and sedatives/anticonvulsants are limited by the facility's licensed bed capacity. The kit may be a secure locked kit or qualifying ADD; the facility should develop its formulary with a single pharmacy provider and use tamper-evident individually packaged single doses.

First band: the separate official table PDF says ≤50; web/archived rendering says <504515
51–1006020
101–1507525
>1509030

Exact boundary caution: Official Mass.gov pages show the first band as <50, but an additional attached Mass.gov table PDF extraction shows ≤50. The conflicting representation affects exactly 50 licensed beds. Do not invent a universal legal assignment for that boundary; consult the Board/DCP or verify the authoritative displayed table if an actual facility has exactly 50 beds. The quantities above are maximums, not required minimum stock levels.

Schedule VI emergency drugs require their own procurement, storage, administration, records, disposal and LTCF quality-assurance controls; the analgesic/sedative II–V table does not authorize unrestricted Schedule VI inventory.

Example: An 80-bed LTCF may select within 60 Schedule II–V analgesic units and 20 sedative/anticonvulsant units maximum. It does not have to stock all 60 and 20, and each medication still needs an appropriate prescriber order for administration.

MPJE Exam Trap: The bed-based quantity is a maximum per group and facility, not a minimum, not a per-patient entitlement, and not a waiver of legitimate ordering/administration. The exactly-50-bed boundary has inconsistent official presentations and is flagged, not guessed.

Official sources: DHCQ 18-6-679 circular · Official separate LTCF table PDF · Official circular PDF

8. Hospice inpatient acute-use ADD stock: bridge medication

Group: Institutional Emergency and Hospice Supply · Original Korean unit: w4-a-20 · Legal review November 9, 2026

Inpatient hospice acute-use medication allows symptom-relief medication while the provider obtains/fills/delivers the complete prescription. Under DHCQ 20-3-700 (March 6, 2020), the condition need not be immediately life-threatening; the aim is prompt palliative care. The pharmacy must use an ADD and have a machine-specific CSR from the Board, determine whether machine-specific DEA registration is required, and provide continuous video surveillance.

The hospice and pharmacy develop an appropriate formulary within the listed maximum units per licensed-bed capacity. The pharmacy retains ownership until the medication is dispensed for administration under a patient-specific prescription/order; reconcile ADD transactions to orders. Packaging must be tamper-evident and individually single-dose.

Published hospice bandAnalgesics II–V maxSedatives/anticonvulsants II–V maxAncillary VI max
<10 beds503025
11–20 beds1006050
>21 beds1509075

Boundary caution: The public circular lists <10, 11–20, >21, leaving exactly 10 or 21 beds unspecified in the published displayed ranges. The guide does not silently convert these into inclusive values. Verify the applicable facility limit with the Board/BHCSQ before applying those exact boundary cases.

Example: A 15-bed inpatient hospice can formulate its acute-use ADD stock within the second listed band—not more than 100 analgesic, 60 sedative/anticonvulsant, and 50 ancillary VI units. That is a facility stock ceiling, not a medication order for any specific patient.

MPJE Exam Trap: Hospice acute-use ADD is a bridge for palliative symptoms, not restricted to life-threatening emergencies, and not the same as a patient-facing APS. Machine-specific registration and the prescription-to-withdrawal reconciliation still apply.

Official sources: DHCQ 20-3-700 official circular · Official hospice PDF · ADD Policy 2019-02

9. Federal central-fill controlled-substance records and in-transit loss

Group: Shared Services & Central Filling · Original Korean unit: w2-d-12 · Legal review November 9, 2026

Central filling is not a transfer of a prescription to a new dispensing pharmacy for the patient's own choice. It is a regulated fulfillment arrangement between the original retail pharmacy and an appropriately registered central-fill pharmacy.

For Schedule II, 21 CFR 1306.15 requires recording the central-fill pharmacy's name/address/DEA number, transmitting retail pharmacist and date, maintaining the original prescription for two years after it was filled, and documenting the returned fill's receipt (date, carrier type, employee accepting it). The central-fill pharmacy separately retains the information received, pharmacist fill date and delivery method/date. Schedules III–V have comparable but not identical records under §1306.27, including the number of refills already and still available.

ActivityRetail/originating pharmacyCentral-fill pharmacy
Send prescription informationIdentify central-fill pharmacy, responsible pharmacist and transmission date; preserve originalMaintain copy/electronic transmitted data and originating retail identification
Fill and labelMaintain patient prescription record and final dispensing roleFill/label with original retail pharmacy details and central-fill DEA identifier
Return shipmentRecord receipt date, carrier and employee who accepted deliveryRecord filled date, delivery date and carrier
In-transit loss: central-fill pharmacy contracted carrierCoordinate, documentCentral-fill pharmacy responsible for DEA report
In-transit loss: retail pharmacy contracted pickup carrierRetail pharmacy responsible for DEA reportCoordinate, document

Under 21 CFR 1301.76(d), the registrant selecting the carrier for this central-fill transport arrangement is the responsible reporting party for relevant in-transit loss. Federal §1301.74(e) requires appropriate transport security measures. Apply the DEA theft/significant-loss rules and additional MA reporting as appropriate.

Example: A central-fill facility uses its own courier to deliver CII packages to the originating pharmacy. The courier loses a tote. Under the express federal central-fill rule, the central-fill facility is responsible for relevant in-transit loss reporting. Reverse the contracting relationship and the reporting party can change.

MPJE Exam Trap: Form 222 is for stock ordering, while §1306.15/27 govern central-fill patient prescriptions. An emergency oral Schedule II prescription cannot simply be received directly by the central filler as though it were the originating retail pharmacist's emergency oral authorization.

Official sources: 21 CFR 1306.15 · 21 CFR 1306.27 · 21 CFR 1306.14 · 21 CFR 1306.24 · 21 CFR 1301.76(d) · 21 CFR 1306.11(d)

10 original MPJE-style practice questions

Question 1. Two Massachusetts-licensed pharmacies have different owners but want to share prescription processing. Under Board Policy 2021-02, which is correct?
  1. Common ownership is mandatory; contracts are never permitted
  2. They can participate under a valid written contract addressing services and shared responsibilities, with other safeguards
  3. Sharing a database alone permits unlimited outsourcing
  4. Only a national wholesaler can own shared services

Correct answer: B.

A: Policy 2021-02 allows same ownership OR written contract.

B: Correct. Legal licensing, secure information, joint responsibility, patient notice and SOP requirements remain.

C: A database connection alone does not satisfy the policy.

D: A wholesaler is not the required shared-services owner.

Question 2. A central-fill MA pharmacy fills an originating pharmacy's Schedule II prescription. Where must it generally send the completed medication for final dispensing?
  1. Directly to the patient as ordinary parcel delivery
  2. To an independent pickup locker anywhere in the state
  3. Back to the originating pharmacy
  4. To the prescriber's office for general stock

Correct answer: C.

A: MA Policy 2021-02 requires the II–V return to the originating retail pharmacy.

B: An APS/locker is a different process and can dispense only IV–VI.

C: Correct. The originating pharmacy completes final dispensing.

D: A patient prescription cannot be converted into prescriber office stock.

Question 3. Under MA Policy 2021-02, which can an appropriately authorized central-fill pharmacy directly ship to a patient under the special Schedule VI pathway?
  1. Qualifying Schedule VI medication not additionally reportable to the PMP
  2. All Schedule II medications
  3. Schedule VI gabapentin without considering PMP reporting
  4. Sterile compounded preparations from central fill

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. The special exception excludes PMP-reportable Schedule VI drugs.

B: Federally controlled II–V must return to the originating pharmacy.

C: Gabapentin is a specific example of the exclusion.

D: MA prohibits central filling sterile/complex nonsterile compounds.

Question 4. An after-hours patient pickup locker dispenses Schedule V prescriptions. What pharmacist consultation availability does 247 CMR 9.10 require?
  1. Only during ordinary pharmacy counter hours
  2. Only for Schedule II drugs
  3. No consultation because a locker has no pharmacist
  4. Opportunity for pharmacist consultation during all hours the APS operates for dispensing

Correct answer: D.

A: Older policy language is overridden by current enacted 247 CMR 9.10(1)(g).

B: Schedule II is not a permitted APS category.

C: A pharmacist opportunity is required even outside counter hours.

D: Correct. Apply this condition whenever APS is available for dispensing.

Question 5. Which system may operate as a patient-facing pickup locker in MA under APS rules?
  1. ADD dispensing Schedule II directly to patient
  2. APS dispensing Schedule IV–VI within same building and other safeguards
  3. PPA located outside pharmacy to repackage dispensed CII for unsupervised patient pickup
  4. Any store vending machine capable of barcode scanning

Correct answer: B.

A: ADD is professional-facing and CII is not allowed through APS.

B: Correct. Policy 2025-02 defines pickup locker as APS; 247 CMR 9.10 imposes further conditions.

C: PPA is pharmacy processing automation, not an APS.

D: Technology alone doesn't establish licensed APS compliance.

Question 6. A pharmacy robot mixes two manufacturer lot numbers in one PPA cell. Then one lot is recalled. What is required by 247 CMR 9.11?
  1. Quarantine all commingled lots according to written PPA procedure
  2. Only quarantine the recalled lot if individual tablets cannot be distinguished
  3. The pharmacy is exempt because barcode verification passed
  4. Transfer responsibility entirely to the robot manufacturer

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. A written plan must quarantine all commingled lots when one is recalled.

B: The rule accounts for the inability to distinguish mixed lots.

C: Barcode accuracy does not remove recall responsibilities.

D: Pharmacy quality accountability remains.

Question 7. Under MA Policy 2023-08, what may a pharmacy technician trainee load into a qualifying hospital ADD?
  1. Any unverified Schedule II
  2. Verified Schedule II–VI without pharmacist oversight
  3. Pharmacist-verified Schedule III–VI stock using required electronic validation and pharmacist direction/supervision
  4. Only noncontrolled drugs and never any Schedule VI

Correct answer: C.

A: PTTs do not receive general Schedule II loading authority under this policy.

B: PTTs may not load those categories without verification or supervision.

C: Correct. The rule allows III–VI in the specific qualifying facility, not unchecked CII.

D: Schedule VI pharmacist-verified stock is permitted in this pathway.

Question 8. A licensed technician with national certification wants to load unverified non-patient-specific gabapentin Schedule VI into a qualifying hospital ADD. Does the special exception permit this?
  1. Yes, all Schedule VI including PMP-reportable products
  2. Yes, if the pharmacy is closed
  3. Yes, for any patient-specific repackaged product
  4. No; the narrow no-prior-pharmacist-verification pathway excludes PMP-reportable Schedule VI medications

Correct answer: D.

A: The policy expressly excludes PMP-reportable VI.

B: Being closed does not waive prior-verification conditions.

C: The exception is restricted to qualifying non-patient-specific stock/packaging.

D: Correct. Gabapentin is excluded from this particular certified-technician exception.

Question 9. An 80-bed Massachusetts LTCF chooses an emergency kit under DHCQ 18-6-679. Which maximum is listed for Schedule II–V analgesic units?
  1. 45 units
  2. 60 units
  3. 90 units
  4. Unlimited if the nurse signs the log

Correct answer: B.

A: Forty-five belongs to the first smaller-bed group.

B: Correct. An 80-bed facility is in the 51–100 band and max is 60 analgesic units.

C: Ninety is the higher >150-bed group.

D: Nurse verification is not a waiver of the facility maximum.

Question 10. A central-fill pharmacy contracted the carrier that lost completed controlled prescriptions en route to the originating pharmacy. Who has federal reporting responsibility for the in-transit loss?
  1. The central-fill pharmacy that contracted that carrier
  2. The patient because the medicine was prescribed to them
  3. The prescriber in all cases
  4. No entity because the pharmacy never delivered to patient

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. 21 CFR 1301.76(d) identifies the contracting central-fill registrant in this situation.

B: The patient does not automatically take ownership/reporting before receipt.

C: The prescriber is not automatically responsible for this shipping contract.

D: In-transit losses can trigger DEA reporting despite not reaching the patient.
