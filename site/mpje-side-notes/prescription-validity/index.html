Prescription Validity, Prescriber Authority, EPCS & Corrections

Understand who may prescribe, how a valid prescription is created, why electronic orders differ from paper, fax and phone, when emergency CII orders are possible, and exactly which changes a pharmacist may make.

Four things a pharmacist must keep separate

1. Prescriber authority: who ordered the medication and may legally prescribe it for this patient? 2. Medical purpose: is the order legitimate rather than a document containing all the right fields but no valid treatment purpose? 3. Original prescription channel: is it paper, oral, fax or a compliant original EPCS—and does an exception permit that format? 4. Corrections and follow-up: which details may be corrected with whom, what documentation is required, and which clock applies?

Important current-law caution: M.G.L. c.94C §20(c) and 105 CMR 721.070(B) use materially different language concerning follow-up to some oral prescriptions. This guide labels the sources and does not silently replace the statutory two-day clock with a general seven-business-day rule. Emergency oral CII remains separately bound by the federal seven-day maximum. Older electronic-prescribing manuals may contain outdated LTCF dates.

1. Who may issue a prescription? Professional scope, license and registration

Korean original: w2-a-4 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

A legally valid prescription must come from an appropriately authorized practitioner. Check the practitioner's identity, active professional license, legal prescribing scope for the specific medicine and patient, and DEA/Massachusetts Controlled Substances Registration (MCSR) when required. The label “doctor,” “NP,” or “PA” alone does not authorize every prescription. Dental, veterinary, podiatric and other prescribers must remain within the relevant profession's legal scope.

Massachusetts APRNs are not interchangeable. The rules for certified nurse practitioners (CNPs), psychiatric nurse mental health clinical specialists, CRNAs, nurse-midwives and physician assistants derive from their respective state statutes/regulations. Under 105 CMR 721.020(E), a CNP, psychiatric nurse mental health clinical specialist or CRNA prescription lists a supervising qualified healthcare professional unless the nurse has authorized independent practice under 244 CMR. Do not reflexively demand a supervising name for every independent-practice nurse.

A pharmacist's dispensing license does not confer unlimited independent prescribing authority. Massachusetts CDTM and specific public-health prescribing pathways are separate, with their own setting, drug-class, training and registration requirements. Similarly, identifying a valid-looking DEA number is not enough: you still must check actual authority, schedule, patient and clinical context.

Pharmacy example: An MA CNP with recognized independent prescriptive practice sends a compliant Schedule VI prescription without naming a supervising physician. The pharmacist does not automatically reject it for that omission; first verify the CNP's actual independent-practice status and other prescription requirements.

MPJE exam trap: Prescriber profession ≠ unlimited scope. An APRN with approved independent practice is not treated as a perpetually supervised prescriber.

Official sources: 105 CMR 721.000 — official DPH prescription-format regulation (PDF) · 244 CMR 4 — advanced practice registered nursing · M.G.L. c.94C §23 — prescription restrictions and electronic-prescribing authority · 21 CFR 1306.05 — federal controlled-substance prescription contents

2. Prescriber agents, hospital DEA numbers and signing restrictions

Korean original: w2-a-4 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

A prescriber's authorized agent may prepare or communicate certain prescription information within permitted channels. Under 21 CFR 1306.05(f), a secretary or agent may prepare a controlled-substance prescription for the practitioner's signature, but does not get to make the clinical prescribing decision or forge the practitioner's signature. A prescriber agent can convey a permissible oral Schedule III–V order where authorized; this is not permission to create a new therapy, change a patient or supply an electronic signature.

Emergency Schedule II is different: 21 CFR 1306.11(d) provides emergency oral authorization from the prescribing individual practitioner to the pharmacist. Do not convert that into a general allowance for assistants to telephone a new CII order and select the drug or dose themselves.

Under 21 CFR 1301.22(c) and 1306.05(g), an authorized institutional practitioner may issue prescriptions using a hospital/institution's DEA registration number plus an assigned special internal code when the specified exemption, employment, authorization, state scope and institution recordkeeping conditions are met. It does not authorize every person wearing a hospital badge to prescribe.

Some federal-service practitioners have a different DEA registration exemption under 21 CFR 1306.05(h); an apparently missing personal DEA number is not automatically an invalid prescription when an actual statutory exemption and qualifying identifiers apply.

Pharmacy example: A hospital resident has a prescription bearing the hospital DEA number and the resident's authorized individual institutional code. The pharmacy checks the institutional exemption and resident status rather than rejecting it solely for lacking a separate individual DEA number.

MPJE exam trap: An agent's delivery of instructions is not the prescriber's clinical decision or legal signing. CII emergency oral authorization has narrower federal rules.

Official sources: 21 CFR 1306.05 — federal controlled-substance prescription contents · 21 CFR 1301.22 — institutional practitioner DEA number/internal code · 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII prescriptions and emergency oral orders · 21 CFR 1311.140 — secure prescriber EPCS signing

3. Legitimate medical purpose and corresponding responsibility

Korean original: w2-a-5 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Under 21 CFR 1306.04(a), a controlled-substance prescription is effective only if issued for a legitimate medical purpose by a practitioner acting in the usual course of professional practice. The practitioner has primary responsibility, but a corresponding responsibility rests on the dispensing pharmacist. Massachusetts M.G.L. c.94C §19 reinforces the core state authorization and responsibility requirements.

A fully completed form, familiar name or apparently valid DEA number is not a substitute for examining material red flags. Consider patient-treatment relationship, suspicious patterns, prescription authenticity, other drugs and PMP data as relevant to the actual concern. When needed, contact the practitioner, investigate, document reasonable resolution and decide using professional judgment. One isolated red flag does not prove every patient is diverting medication; nor does a nominal confirmation excuse unresolved serious concerns. This is a reasoning approach, not a fabricated mandatory checklist for every prescription.

Office-stock prohibition: under 21 CFR 1306.04(b), a practitioner cannot issue ordinary patient prescriptions merely to obtain general office stock for treating other patients. Drug supply between registrants requires the correct ordering/distribution rules. The limited §1306.04(d)/§1306.07(f) route for certain Schedule III–V maintenance/detoxification medicines to a treating practitioner is not broad permission to replenish any practice's stock.

Pharmacy example: A clinic sends five 'patient prescriptions' to replenish controlled medications for an office supply cabinet rather than to dispense to identified patients. These are not valid ordinary patient prescriptions, regardless of the prescriber's DEA number.

MPJE exam trap: Technically complete ≠ clinically and legally valid. Corresponding responsibility and office-stock restrictions apply even when the paperwork looks correct.

Official sources: 21 CFR 1306.04 — legitimate medical purpose and corresponding responsibility · M.G.L. c.94C §19 — legitimacy and pharmacist responsibility · 21 CFR 1306.05 — federal controlled-substance prescription contents

4. Prescription elements: date, signature, drug, patient, DEA/MCSR and partial-fill language

Korean original: w2-a-6 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Under 21 CFR 1306.05(a), a controlled-substance prescription must be dated as of and signed on the actual issue day and state the patient's full name/address; drug name, strength, dosage form, quantity, and directions; and the practitioner's name, address and registration number. Proper exemptions and special prescription categories may modify the identifier required; do not invent a universal federal Schedule VI prescription requirement.

Massachusetts 105 CMR 721.020(C)–(D) additionally specifies the practitioner or hospital/clinic identifying information, registration, issuance date, drug quantity, patient identification (subject to defined exceptions, including veterinarian, approved naloxone/opioid antagonist, certain EPT and public-health orders), directions/cautions and statement of the number of refills. For a prescriber limited to Schedule VI, the appropriate MCSR can appear in place of a DEA number under 105 CMR 721.010.

Schedule II opioid: M.G.L. c.94C §22(c) and 105 CMR 721.020(D)(7) require a notation telling the patient of the option to request less than the full prescribed quantity. This is a separate form requirement; it does not automatically modify the prescription to whatever quantity the pharmacist prefers.

No substitution must be a separate, nondefault prescription indication under 105 CMR 721.020(B). It is not supplied by the normal signature, and generic substitution law still applies. Under 105 CMR 721.050, more than one drug product generally requires separate prescription forms/records, with a defined hospital-setting exception and clear directions.

Actual signature matters: printing a computer-generated paper prescription with the prescriber's typed name does not replace the manual signature when a qualifying paper prescription requires one. An EPCS instead must satisfy the actual secure signing system rules.

Pharmacy example: A CII oxycodone prescription has the prescribed strength and quantity but omits the patient partial-fill notation. Follow the current Board/DCP correction policy and notify/confirm as directed rather than silently assume the entire form requirement never applies.

MPJE exam trap: Issue date ≠ earliest-fill date. No-substitution indication ≠ prescriber signature. Opioid CII partial-fill notation is distinct.

Official sources: 21 CFR 1306.05 — federal controlled-substance prescription contents · 105 CMR 721.000 — official DPH prescription-format regulation (PDF) · M.G.L. c.94C §22 — prescription contents and CII opioid partial-fill notation · Massachusetts DPH — elements of a written prescription · Board/DCP Policy 2018-01 — permitted changes/additions (revised January 11, 2024) · DCP Schedule II opioid partial-fill guidance

5. Paper, telephone, fax and EPCS are not interchangeable

Korean original: w2-a-7 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Four ways a prescription may arrive can create different legal consequences: paper (a signed written prescription); oral (a permissible verbal order properly documented); fax (an image transmitted by facsimile); and EPCS (a natively created, signed and transmitted electronic controlled-substance prescription using a compliant system). Scanning a paper form or typing a telephone order into pharmacy software does not turn it into an original EPCS. Under 105 CMR 721.010, fax is explicitly excluded from Electronic Transmission.

Under 21 CFR 1311.140, the prescribing individual practitioner reviews and approves the EPCS and completes the prescribed two-factor authentication signing process using compliant software. Another employee may enter information but cannot use the prescriber's credentials to sign. If required electronic prescription content is altered during transmission, 21 CFR 1306.08(d) deems it invalid.

For paper/faxed prescriptions, determine whether that form is allowed for the schedule and circumstance. A faxed CII ordinarily requires the manually signed original before dispensing unless a narrow federal exception applies (for example, certain compounded direct-administration therapies, LTCF patients or qualifying hospice patients); ordinary fax ≠ generic original CII permission. See 21 CFR 1306.11.

Unfilled original transfers: DEA 21 CFR 1306.08(e)–(h) permits one-time transfer for initial dispensing of qualifying original unfilled EPCS II–V between retail pharmacies at patient request, in original electronic form with direct licensed-pharmacist communication and prescribed documentation. Massachusetts Board Policy 2023-10 adopts the distinction and says unfilled paper/fax/oral II–V cannot simply be transferred for initial filling; conversion into the computer system does not change their origin. Original unfilled Schedule VI orders have a distinct Massachusetts transfer path.

Pharmacy example: A patient asks Pharmacy A to transfer a never-filled oxycodone prescription. If the original was genuine EPCS and all conditions are met, a one-time initial transfer may be possible. If the original was a handwritten paper CII that the pharmacy scanned, the DEA EPCS initial-transfer exception does not apply.

MPJE exam trap: Fax ≠ EPCS. Scanned paper ≠ EPCS. Never-filled CII EPCS transfer ≠ transfer of a paper CII or a CII refill.

Official sources: 105 CMR 721.000 — official DPH prescription-format regulation (PDF) · 21 CFR 1306.05 — federal controlled-substance prescription contents · 21 CFR 1306.08 — electronic prescriptions and one-time initial transfers · 21 CFR 1311.140 — secure prescriber EPCS signing · 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII prescriptions and emergency oral orders · Board Policy 2023-10 — unfilled prescriptions

6. Massachusetts e-prescribing rule and the important exceptions

Korean original: w2-a-8 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Massachusetts generally requires an electronic prescription with electronic signature for prescriptions generated in the Commonwealth (105 CMR 721.020(A)), unless a 105 CMR 721.070(A) exception applies. Listed pathways include veterinary prescriptions, temporary technology or power failure, granted waivers, emergencies, legally non-electronic orders, prescriptions issued outside Massachusetts, chlamydia EPT to a partner, qualifying compounded drug preparations, Schedule VI, DME and specified urgent public-health situations. An exception allows an otherwise lawful alternative format; it does not repeal the separate schedule, authenticity or federal controlled-drug requirements.

Long-term care date trap: 105 CMR 721.070(A)(11) says certain LTCF prescriptions issued before January 1, 2025, or such later date as DPH determines, have an exception. It is not a universal, indefinite 2026 LTCF waiver. A later extension must be established before relying on it; do not use an older manual's obsolete 2023 date without comparing the effective regulation.

Pharmacist exception-verification rule: 105 CMR 721.070(C) says a pharmacist receiving an otherwise valid written or oral prescription is not required to verify whether it properly falls within the e-prescribing exception, including a prescriber waiver. This is not a waiver of normal checks on prescriber authority, prescription authenticity, federal CII emergency rules or other dispensing requirements.

Pharmacy example: A valid Massachusetts Schedule VI antibiotic prescription arrives on a permitted signed paper form. The pharmacist does not reject it merely because it was not issued electronically; Schedule VI is expressly in the regulation's list of exceptions.

MPJE exam trap: eRx exception ≠ oral CII refill permission. Pharmacist need not check the waiver category ≠ pharmacist may ignore a forged prescription.

Official sources: 105 CMR 721.000 — official DPH prescription-format regulation (PDF) · Massachusetts DPH regulation publication (effective September 27, 2024) · M.G.L. c.94C §23 — prescription restrictions and electronic-prescribing authority · DCP electronic prescribing and dispensing manual (older material; compare with 2024 regulation)

7. Emergency oral Schedule II: all conditions and the federal seven-day clock

Korean original: oral-followup · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Ordinary CII prescriptions cannot simply be called into a pharmacy. Under 21 CFR 1306.11(d) and 105 CMR 721.060(B), a qualifying emergency can support an oral order from the prescribing individual practitioner, with all of these safeguards: (1) immediate treatment is needed and normal signed prescription delivery is impractical or would cause harmful delay; (2) quantity is limited to the amount needed for the emergency period; (3) the pharmacist records the required elements immediately (without the practitioner's contemporaneous signature); (4) reasonable good-faith prescriber verification is performed when not known.

Federal 21 CFR 1306.11(d)(4) requires the practitioner to cause a compliant written emergency follow-up within seven days of authorization, with “Authorization for Emergency Dispensing” and the date of the oral order; if mailed, it must be postmarked within those seven days. The federal rule permits a compliant electronic follow-up with appropriate annotation, but does not generally allow ordinary fax to substitute for every paper original. The pharmacist attaches or cross-references the follow-up to the original oral order and must notify the nearest DEA office if the required follow-up is not delivered under that rule.

Do not extend federal seven days to seven business days: Massachusetts 105 CMR 721.060(B)(4) uses “seven business days,” while also requiring compliance with federal and state law. When a shorter federal period controls, the shorter rule must be honored. M.G.L. c.94C §20(c) contains additional electronic/oral follow-up language; read the statute and current regulation together rather than merging every oral-prescription situation into a single clock (see Topic 8).

Pharmacy example: At night, a patient urgently needs limited opioid treatment and meets the true federal emergency criteria. The prescribing practitioner calls a CII order; the pharmacist verifies if needed, records it promptly, limits quantity to the emergency period and tracks the federal seven-calendar-day follow-up and DEA reporting duty.

MPJE exam trap: Emergency CII oral ≠ routine telephone CII prescription; seven calendar days under federal law ≠ seven business days simply because a state regulation uses that phrase.

Official sources: 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII prescriptions and emergency oral orders · 105 CMR 721.000 — official DPH prescription-format regulation (PDF) · M.G.L. c.94C §20 — oral prescriptions and statutory follow-up

8. Oral Schedule III–V follow-up: a genuine statute/regulation discrepancy

Korean original: oral-followup · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Do not memorize the oversimplification that all Massachusetts oral prescriptions get follow-up in exactly two days or that all get seven business days. The actual legal language differs by source and statutory exception:

ProvisionFollow-up textScope / caution
M.G.L. c.94C §20(c)Ordinarily an electronic prescription within two days after an oral prescriptionPrescriber with commissioner e-prescribing exception: written follow-up within seven days or a shorter federal period
105 CMR 721.070(B)When an oral Schedule II–V order is issued under listed exceptions, written or electronic follow-up within seven business days or shorter federal periodText is not identical to §20(c); do not erase the discrepancy
21 CFR 1306.11(d)Emergency CII follow-up within seven daysFederal emergency-specific maximum; no business-day extension
Schedule VI§20(c) follow-up requirement expressly does not applyDoes not excuse legitimacy, agent authority or documentation

Unresolved regulatory interface: the general two-day statutory rule and the regulation's seven-business-day wording cannot be reduced to an unconditional single clock for every III–V scenario without authority addressing their interaction. The regulation does not, on its face, automatically repeal the statute. For actual pharmacy processing or a contested test scenario, identify which provision and exception the question invokes and follow a current Board/DCP interpretation; err on the more restrictive applicable requirement unless official direction resolves the interaction.

When an appropriate oral follow-up arrives, link it to the original oral documentation. It is not a second independent authorization to dispense the same quantity a second time.

Pharmacy example: An NP calls a Schedule IV order under a permitted non-electronic pathway. The pharmacist should not assume that a universally applicable seven-business-day grace period supersedes §20(c)'s statutory two-day language; the specific exception and applicable federal/state requirements must be reconciled.

MPJE exam trap: CII emergency seven days is federal; MA oral statute two days has an exception; 105 CMR 721.070(B) says seven business days; MA VI is expressly exempt from the §20(c) follow-up.

Official sources: M.G.L. c.94C §20 — oral prescriptions and statutory follow-up · 105 CMR 721.000 — official DPH prescription-format regulation (PDF) · 21 CFR 1306.11 — CII prescriptions and emergency oral orders

9. Prescription corrections: nonchangeable elements versus permitted CII / III–VI edits

Korean original: w2-a-10 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Massachusetts Board/DCP Policy 2018-01, revised January 11, 2024, distinguishes corrections that require a new prescription from permitted changes with appropriate consultation. It covers new Schedule II–VI prescriptions. The pharmacy must never substitute a different patient, different controlled substance (apart from lawful generic substitution), prescriber name or prescriber signature through an informal correction. A Schedule II earliest-fill date cannot be changed or added this way; a new prescription is required.

For CII: with the prescriber, one can make the policy-authorized changes to directions, dosage form, strength, quantity and prescriber address, and one can add a missing issue date (but not replace an already written date). A missing patient address can be added without consultation; changing an existing patient address requires consultation with the prescriber or authorized agent. A missing prescriber DEA number may be added without consultation, but actual identity and number must still be verified. The policy does not let an authorized agent decide CII clinical changes simply because the agent can confirm an address.

For Schedules III–VI: the policy permits certain changes to directions, form, strength, quantity, refill information and related elements after consultation with the prescriber or authorized agent. The issue date, when missing, may be added, not arbitrarily rewritten. The missing-address/DEA exceptions are similar as stated in the policy.

Missing no-substitution and CII opioid patient partial-fill notation: the policy includes narrow add-and-notify provisions; do not treat that as permission to invent the prescriber's intent or alter the drug. For CII partial-fill language, consult applicable DCP circular instructions and document any prescriber/agent confirmation required by the specific circumstances.

Pharmacy example: An oxycodone CII is written for Patient A but actually intended for Patient B. Changing the name after a phone call is not permissible under Policy 2018-01; a new valid prescription is required. In contrast, a missing patient address can be added under the policy's narrower CII rule.

MPJE exam trap: Never change patient, drug, prescriber name, signature, or CII earliest-fill date by pharmacy correction. A missing issue date may be added—not rewritten.

Official sources: Board/DCP Policy 2018-01 — permitted changes/additions (revised January 11, 2024) · 105 CMR 721.000 — official DPH prescription-format regulation (PDF) · DCP Schedule II opioid partial-fill guidance · 21 CFR 1306.05 — federal controlled-substance prescription contents

10. Schedule VI failover, gabapentin exclusion and documentation of changes

Korean original: w2-a-10 · Legal review: November 9, 2026

Massachusetts Schedule VI-only failover: under 105 CMR 721.010 and 721.020(G), a properly generated Schedule VI electronic prescription that experiences an unforeseen electronic transmission defect and becomes a computer-generated fax can be treated as an oral prescription without a follow-up written/electronic prescription if it has all required elements except the signature, is immediately recorded in the pharmacy system or reduced to writing, the pharmacist makes reasonable identity checks when the practitioner is unknown, and the order is not an Additional Drug.

Gabapentin is a critical exception: although it is Massachusetts Schedule VI, gabapentin is treated as an Additional Drug for PMP purposes, so this special failover does not apply. Get an otherwise legally valid prescription rather than dispensing under the excluded shortcut. Likewise, a crashed system does not turn a Schedule II EPCS into a valid emergency oral order: all CII emergency criteria must independently be satisfied.

Schedule VI correction flexibility: Policy 2018-01 allows a missing MCSR number for a qualifying VI-only practitioner to be added without consultation; permitted flavoring on patient/agent request is another exception. A pharmacist may adjust a Schedule VI days' supply without prescriber consultation only for drugs that do not require PMP reporting, and must consider clinical risks, especially narrow-therapeutic-index or behavioral-health drugs. It is not blanket authority to rewrite gabapentin supplies or every medication strength.

Record each authorized change: the date, what was changed/added, authorizing prescriber where applicable, and pharmacist accepting/making it. Preserve actual auditability instead of making an undocumented change to a signed prescription image.

Pharmacy example: A gabapentin e-prescription transmission fails and a computer-generated fax arrives. Do not process it under the Schedule VI failover exemption, because gabapentin is an Additional Drug. If the same occurs with a qualifying non-PMP Schedule VI antibiotic and all 721.020(G) conditions are met, failover may be permissible.

MPJE exam trap: VI failover ≠ any fax. Gabapentin VI Additional Drug is excluded. For Schedule VI supply adjustments, PMP-reportable drugs are excluded from the pharmacist-only change route.

Official sources: 105 CMR 721.000 — official DPH prescription-format regulation (PDF) · Board/DCP Policy 2018-01 — permitted changes/additions (revised January 11, 2024) · M.G.L. c.94C §20 — oral prescriptions and statutory follow-up · 247 CMR 9.00 — Massachusetts pharmacy professional practice standards

Memory tools

Authority → Purpose → Format → Changes. Do not decide a prescription is valid simply because the prescriber has a DEA number. “EPCS is born electronic”: scanning paper does not create an EPCS original. “Agent prepares, practitioner signs”: authorized staff can assist but cannot invent orders or sign as the prescriber. “CII emergency: only the emergency quantity, seven calendar days federally.” “5 never-edit categories” under Policy 2018-01: patient, actual drug, prescriber name, prescriber signature, and CII earliest-fill date.

Separate the clocks: MA §20(c) two-day electronic and specified seven-day written exception, 105 CMR 721.070(B) seven-business-day text, and the federal CII emergency seven-day deadline are different authorities with distinct conditions. A Schedule VI failover is not a CII emergency. Refer to Topic 8 whenever a question lacks enough detail to identify its exact pathway.

Question 1. A properly licensed Massachusetts CNP with authorized independent prescriptive practice sends a compliant Schedule VI prescription without a supervising physician name. What is the best action?
  1. Automatically reject because every CNP order must list a supervisor
  2. Check the CNP's independent-practice authority; a supervisor name is not categorically required for an independently authorized CNP
  3. Supply the medication because professional scope never matters for VI
  4. Replace the CNP name with a physician's name

Correct answer: B.

A: 105 CMR 721.020(E) expressly distinguishes independent practice.

B: Correct. The supervision line is conditional; other validity requirements still apply.

C: MA VI still requires a lawful prescriber.

D: Prescriber identity cannot be rewritten through an informal correction.

Question 2. An office employee telephones a new CII prescription to a pharmacy and says the prescriber authorized her to select the dose. Which is correct?
  1. The agent can choose any CII dose if employed by the prescriber
  2. An agent's role automatically substitutes for the prescriber's signature
  3. Every CII prescription can be filled from an agent's routine phone call
  4. A new emergency oral CII order requires the narrow emergency rules and authorization of the prescribing individual practitioner; the agent cannot independently choose therapy

Correct answer: D.

A: Clinical prescribing is not delegated by office employment.

B: Agent preparation and communication do not replace proper authentication.

C: CII oral prescribing is not a routine pathway.

D: Correct. 21 CFR 1306.11(d) supplies narrower conditions.

Question 3. A prescription has a legitimate DEA number but is intended solely to replenish a physician's general office stock. What is the appropriate interpretation?
  1. It is not a valid ordinary patient prescription to replenish general office stock
  2. Any DEA number authorizes ordinary patient prescriptions for general office stock
  3. It is valid if the medication is Schedule II only
  4. The pharmacy may convert it to a new patient's prescription

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. 21 CFR 1306.04(b) prohibits using patient prescriptions for general office stock.

B: Registration does not override legitimate-purpose rules.

C: The office-stock restriction is not a CII-only matter.

D: Patient identity is not freely changeable by the pharmacist.

Question 4. Under Massachusetts 105 CMR 721.020(D), which additional notation is required for a Schedule II opioid prescription?
  1. An automatic no-substitution choice on every prescription
  2. A fixed 90-day supply instruction
  3. Information that a patient may request a partial fill of less than the full quantity
  4. A statement that the pharmacist may issue unlimited refills

Correct answer: C.

A: No substitution must not be the default.

B: The notation is about patient choice of a smaller amount, not a 90-day supply.

C: Correct; the patient may request less than the prescribed full quantity.

D: CII prescriptions are not refillable.

Question 5. Pharmacy A has an original never-dispensed CII EPCS, and the patient requests a transfer to Pharmacy B. What is accurate?
  1. All unfilled paper, fax and oral CIIs can be converted into EPCS by typing them into a computer
  2. A one-time original electronic transfer may be permitted, with direct licensed-pharmacist communication and required records
  3. Every CII prescription can be transferred an unlimited number of times
  4. A scanned CII paper form becomes EPCS automatically

Correct answer: B.

A: Entering a paper order into software does not create an original EPCS.

B: Correct. See 21 CFR 1306.08 and MA Policy 2023-10.

C: This is a one-time initial transfer rule.

D: The original form of the prescription does not change through scanning.

Question 6. An otherwise valid Schedule VI antibiotic prescription is issued on a lawful signed paper form by an MA prescriber. Which statement about ePrescribing is correct?
  1. The paper order can never be dispensed because every MA prescription is required to be EPCS
  2. The pharmacist must call DPH to confirm the prescriber's waiver in every case
  3. The pharmacy must convert the paper document into a new CII emergency prescription
  4. MA 105 CMR 721.070(A)(9) expressly provides a Schedule VI exception; the pharmacist still checks authenticity and ordinary validity

Correct answer: D.

A: The Massachusetts regulation has enumerated exceptions.

B: 105 CMR 721.070(C) does not universally require verifying the eRx exception.

C: Antibiotics are not transformed into CII emergency orders.

D: Correct. Format exception is not a waiver of other laws.

Question 7. What does federal 21 CFR 1306.11(d) require after a lawful emergency oral Schedule II order?
  1. A qualifying follow-up within seven days, with the required emergency authorization designation; notify DEA if the required prescription is not received
  2. An automatic refill authorization after 90 days
  3. The prescriber may deliver the follow-up whenever convenient without a clock
  4. A seven-business-day federal maximum that overrides any shorter limit

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Federal seven days are not seven business days; the pharmacy tracks and reports failure.

B: CII refills remain prohibited.

C: Emergency written follow-up is time sensitive.

D: The federal text uses seven days, not business days.

Question 8. Which description accurately handles the Massachusetts oral prescription follow-up rules?
  1. All oral Schedule II–VI prescriptions always use a universal seven-business-day period
  2. Massachusetts §20(c) and 105 CMR 721.070(B) contain different time formulations; Schedule VI is expressly excluded from §20(c), and emergency CII has its federal seven-day rule
  3. All oral prescriptions always need electronic follow-up exactly two days later, even Schedule VI
  4. A pharmacy may fill a second full quantity when the follow-up arrives

Correct answer: B.

A: The statute, regulation, exceptions and federal CII rule differ.

B: Correct. Specific pathway and governing requirement matter; an unresolved III–V discrepancy should not be concealed.

C: Section 20(c) expressly excludes VI and contains exceptions.

D: Follow-up documents the original order, not a new duplicate authorization.

Question 9. Under Massachusetts Policy 2018-01, which CII correction requires a NEW prescription?
  1. Adding a missing patient address under the policy
  2. Adding a verified missing DEA number under the policy
  3. Changing the CII earliest-fill date to an earlier date
  4. Changing quantity after actual consultation with the prescriber as allowed by the policy

Correct answer: C.

A: Missing patient address may be added without consultation.

B: Missing DEA number may be added under the policy.

C: Correct; earliest-fill date is on the policy's cannot-change list.

D: CII quantity change may be allowed after consultation with the prescriber.

Question 10. A computer-generated fax is produced when an MA Schedule VI gabapentin eRx transmission fails. Can the pharmacy treat it as an eligible no-follow-up 'failover' under 105 CMR 721.020(G)?
  1. Yes; every Schedule VI fax is automatically authorized
  2. Yes, but only if the patient's insurance authorizes it
  3. Yes; gabapentin is federally Schedule II
  4. No; gabapentin is an Additional Drug and is excluded from that specific failover pathway

Correct answer: D.

A: A true unforeseen failover has multiple conditions and Additional Drug exclusions.

B: Insurance coverage is not the criterion.

C: Gabapentin is MA Schedule VI, not federally Schedule II.

D: Correct. Obtain a lawful prescription by a different applicable route.
