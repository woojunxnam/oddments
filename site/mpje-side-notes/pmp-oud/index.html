Four different layers of pharmacy law

First: MassPAT clinical query and pharmacy reporting are separate; mandatory prescriber lookup is drug- and exception-specific. Second: pickup customer ID, hardship, delivery, naloxone and gabapentin have different reporting/identification rules. Third: opioid quantities, antagonist offers, directives, methadone OTP and buprenorphine prescribing are not one universal opioid rule. Fourth: temporary all-schedules telemedicine and the permanent OUD-specific telemedicine rule have different conditions.

As of November 10, 2026: Broad DEA/HHS telemedicine authorization at 21 CFR 1307.41 ends December 31, 2026 unless further extended. The special OUD-specific rule at 21 CFR 1306.51 is already effective and adds a patient-state PDMP check and a pharmacy identity-verification condition. The old X-waiver is abolished, not general professional registration.

1. MassPAT means THREE distinct duties

Original Korean unit: w2-c-8 · Law review November 10, 2026

MassPAT is Massachusetts's Prescription Monitoring Program. Three activities have different actors and triggers: register/access (authorized credentials to use PMP), patient-specific clinical query (looking up a medication history for a permitted purpose), and dispensing-data submission (sending records to the DPH PMP Clearinghouse).

Pharmacists dispensing reportable drugs must satisfy applicable PMP registration/access duties and reasonable clinical review, but the express prescriber query mandate in 105 CMR 700.012(G) does not mean every dispensing pharmacist must run MassPAT before every Schedule II–V fill. If a pharmacist performs a separately authorized prescribing role, practitioner-specific PMP rules can apply. 21 CFR 1306.04 corresponding responsibility remains for controlled prescriptions and may call for investigating warning signs.

Sending an accepted dispensing report is not proof that a pharmacist looked up the patient's history or resolved a clinical concern. A patient-specific query is not a substitute for the pharmacy's reporting. MassPAT access for curiosity, gossip, relatives or coworkers unrelated to care is not authorized.

Pharmacy scenario: A pharmacist checks a patient's controlled-drug history before resolving a concerning prescription. After dispensing, the pharmacy must still send the required record to the Clearinghouse.

MPJE exam trap: ACCESS, QUERY and SUBMISSION are different acts. Prescriber each-opioid-query ≠ universal pharmacist query.

Official references: 105 CMR 700.012 — Massachusetts Prescription Monitoring Program · M.G.L. c.94C §24A — MassPAT program · DCP pharmacy reporting and data submission · 21 CFR 1306.04 — controlled prescription legitimate purpose

2. Prescriber's mandatory query: drug category and exceptions

Original Korean unit: w2-c-9 · Law review November 10, 2026

Under 105 CMR 700.012(G) and M.G.L. c.94C §24A, a qualified practitioner ordinarily must check the PMP before prescribing each Schedule II or III opioid or a benzodiazepine. DPH can designate other drugs in Schedule IV/V; its current public notice identifies benzodiazepines in that designated category. A Schedule IV benzodiazepine is therefore not exempt merely because it is not CII.

Specific exceptions include hospice care; emergency care where querying first is likely to cause patient harm; a system, electricity or access failure making use not reasonably possible; and applicable Department waivers/conditions. “Too busy” is not a universal exception. Gabapentin is an MA Schedule VI Additional Drug reported to PMP, but being reported does not automatically place every gabapentin prescription in the mandatory opioid/benzodiazepine query provision.

Check the role: a registered prescriber has a pre-prescribing obligation, whereas the dispensing pharmacist conducts professional clinical safety review without a blanket obligation to repeat every prescriber-side query unless a separate law or specific pathway imposes one.

Pharmacy scenario: A prescriber wants to issue lorazepam (Schedule IV). Because it is a benzodiazepine, the general pre-prescribing PMP-query requirement must be considered.

MPJE exam trap: Check WHO and which DRUG: Schedule II/III opioid, benzodiazepine, and any specifically designated IV/V drugs; exceptions are narrow.

Official references: 105 CMR 700.012 — Massachusetts Prescription Monitoring Program · M.G.L. c.94C §24A — MassPAT program · DCP mandatory MassPAT querying for benzodiazepines

3. Delegate confidentiality: when to report within one business day

Original Korean unit: w2-c-9 · Law review November 10, 2026

Appropriately authorized MassPAT delegates can assist a registered primary participant, but the participant remains accountable for permitted use and monitoring. Do not share someone else's personal PMP credentials. Delegate access must be for a legally permitted patient-care or specified official purpose.

105 CMR 700.012(F)(3) calls on the primary account holder to notify the Department within one business day when a delegate has violated the PMP user terms or is no longer authorized, and to take reasonable steps to ensure the delegate understands the system. That one-business-day notice is a delegate account rule, not the pharmacy's dispensing-data submission deadline.

When staff change roles or leave employment, promptly remove access; retained authorization cannot be assumed. Patient records cannot be searched merely because a user has a technically working username/password.

Pharmacy scenario: A clinic's approved delegate leaves. The primary account holder terminates the delegate's access and follows the one-business-day DPH notice requirement.

MPJE exam trap: ONE BUSINESS DAY applies to the delegation/violation notice; do not substitute it for all other PMP deadlines.

Official references: 105 CMR 700.012 — Massachusetts Prescription Monitoring Program · M.G.L. c.94C §24A — MassPAT program

4. What is reported: II–V, gabapentin, and nonresident pharmacies

Original Korean unit: w2-c-10 · Law review November 10, 2026

Current Massachusetts DCP pharmacy reporting guidance requires reporting dispensed Schedule II–V drugs and gabapentin, a Massachusetts Schedule VI Additional Drug. An appropriately registered out-of-state pharmacy that delivers these medications to a patient in Massachusetts is also covered. Ordinary non-PMP Schedule VI drugs are not all reportable simply because gabapentin is.

Pregabalin is federally Schedule V and reportable on that basis; gabapentin is not federally Schedule V, but Massachusetts has added it to PMP reporting. DCP explains that the Veterans Affairs reports federally controlled II–V, not gabapentin, so its gabapentin prescriptions may be absent from a patient's MassPAT history. Missing data in the system do not conclusively establish that the drug was never received.

Distinguish pharmacy dispensing from prescriber clinical queries and nonretail administration/medication orders. Data-reporting categories do not define every professional duty triggered by that drug.

Pharmacy scenario: Gabapentin and pregabalin are dispensed at an MA retail pharmacy. Both are reportable; only pregabalin is federally controlled Schedule V.

MPJE exam trap: II–V + GABAPENTIN report. Do not call every MA VI reportable or assume VA gabapentin is always on MassPAT.

Official references: 105 CMR 700.012 — Massachusetts Prescription Monitoring Program · DCP pharmacy reporting and data submission · M.G.L. c.94C §24A — MassPAT program

5. Reporting cadence, zero files, rejection and corrected transactions

Original Korean unit: w2-c-10 · Law review November 10, 2026

M.G.L. c.94C §24A provides an at-least-daily / 24-hour structure. Current DCP operational instructions under 105 CMR 700.012 require covered pharmacy data through the Clearinghouse within 24 hours or the next business day (with applicable holiday, operation-day and waiver provisions). Weekly or monthly batches do not satisfy the usual cadence.

When no II–V or gabapentin prescriptions were dispensed in a relevant period, reporting pharmacies ordinarily must submit a Zero Report; submitting no data is not the same as attesting to zero activity. An approved days-of-operation or other specific waiver may alter that duty. If a pharmacy otherwise has a non-dispensing exemption but actually dispenses a reportable medication, evaluate that dispensing under current rules rather than assuming permanent immunity.

File accepted ≠ every record accepted. Check rejected transactions; correct and resubmit. Distinguish new record entries, revisions of accepted erroneous reports and void/cancel scenarios. Report actual sale/delivery versus prepared dates, and record original/refill/partial information correctly. Old software-guide fields referring to XDEA do not restore the repealed federal X-waiver.

Pharmacy scenario: A reporting file is accepted, but three controlled-substance records are rejected. The pharmacy must fix and resubmit them; a file upload alone is insufficient.

MPJE exam trap: Within 24 HOURS/NEXT BUSINESS DAY; Zero Reports when applicable; rejected data must be corrected. Upload ≠ complete compliance.

Official references: M.G.L. c.94C §24A — MassPAT program · 105 CMR 700.012 — Massachusetts Prescription Monitoring Program · DCP pharmacy reporting and data submission · DCP PMP Data Submission Dispenser Guide v5.2

6. Customer identification: recipient ID, hardship and delivery exemptions

Original Korean unit: w2-c-11 · Law review November 10, 2026

247 CMR 9.04(14) requires identification by a valid government-issued customer identifier when dispensing Schedule II–V or designated Additional Drugs such as gabapentin to the ultimate user or that user's agent. The person who physically receives the medicine may not be the patient. Follow DCP's acceptable-ID and customer-field reporting definitions; do not substitute a patient's Social Security number.

A pharmacist may use a narrow serious-hardship exception if delay or failure to dispense would result in serious hardship. The pharmacist must document the reason, and the recipient must print name/address and sign the prescription/log as required; use the DCP-specified exception data entry rather than leaving the field blank. This is neither automatic permission without any ID nor a rule that every patient without a card must be denied.

DCP also defines a delivery exception for customer-ID collection when a pharmacy or common carrier delivers to a residence or facility, coupled with internal delivery tracking. Identity, authorized delivery and medication security still matter. The separate federal OUD-specific telemedicine ID requirement under 21 CFR 1306.51 can also apply; satisfying its flexible ID examples does not remove Massachusetts's independent requirements.

Pharmacy scenario: A spouse picks up a patient's controlled medication. Record the collecting agent's qualifying identifier. If serious hardship prevents ID presentation, follow the signed alternative and documentation pathway.

MPJE exam trap: CUSTOMER ≠ PATIENT; federal alternate OUD telemedicine ID does not automatically override MA's customer-ID rule.

Official references: 247 CMR 9.04 — pharmacist and customer identification requirements · DCP PMP Data Submission Dispenser Guide v5.2 · DCP pharmacy reporting and data submission · 21 CFR 1306.51 — special permanent OUD telemedicine prescribing

7. Naloxone reporting: preferred daily PMP versus annual option

Original Korean unit: w2-c-11 · Law review November 10, 2026

According to DCP and Board Policy 2024-01 (revised February 6, 2025), pharmacies may report naloxone dispensation as part of daily PMP submissions, which is the preferred method for meeting separate statutory naloxone reporting. If not using that preferred process, the policy describes an annual report due January 15 for the previous calendar year's totals, including counts of doses and doses covered by insurance.

Naloxone data submitted by this mechanism are for aggregate data collection and do not appear in an individual patient's MassPAT profile. DCP expressly says no customer ID collection is needed for naloxone reporting. Naloxone is not designated a PMP “Additional Drug” in the same way as gabapentin.

Rules governing naloxone access, standing orders and counseling remain separate from the data-submission mechanism. Do not treat obtaining naloxone as a reason to label a patient as misusing opioids.

Pharmacy scenario: A patient obtains naloxone under an eligible pharmacy pathway. The transaction may be reported via preferred daily data submissions, but the patient does not receive an individually visible naloxone MassPAT record.

MPJE exam trap: Naloxone aggregate reporting ≠ gabapentin Additional Drug. Preferred daily PMP or qualified annual Jan 15 reporting; no naloxone customer ID.

Official references: DCP pharmacy reporting and data submission · Board Policy 2024-01 Naloxone Dispensing, revised 2025 · M.G.L. c.94C §24A — MassPAT program

8. Opioid quantity limits, exceptions and required pharmacist naloxone offer

Original Korean unit: w2-c-12 · Law review November 10, 2026

M.G.L. c.94C §19D(a) generally prohibits a practitioner from issuing more than a seven-day supply for an adult's first outpatient opioid prescription. For a minor, it generally limits each opioid prescription to seven days, and the practitioner must discuss risks and reasons with the parent/guardian.

§19D(b) permits specified longer supply where the practitioner determines it is needed for an acute medical condition, chronic pain, cancer-related pain or palliative care and documents both the condition and why a non-opiate alternative was not appropriate. §19D(c) excludes medicines designed for treatment of substance abuse/opioid dependence. The separate §23(d) CII/CIII single-fill limits still apply. These are primarily prescriber issuance duties; pharmacists retain the separate responsibility to assess a suspicious order.

Newer independent pharmacist duty: Effective July 1, 2024, M.G.L. c.94C §18D(b) requires a pharmacist dispensing Schedule II opioid to inform of adverse risks and offer to dispense an opioid antagonist to the patient and, where applicable, their designee or a minor's parent/guardian. The law requires offering, not forcing a purchase or automatically dispensing an antagonist against patient wishes.

Pharmacy scenario: An opioid-naïve adult presents a first outpatient oxycodone prescription for 14 days with no explained exception. That presents a §19D prescribing concern. When filling a lawful CII opioid, the pharmacist must also communicate risks and offer an opioid antagonist.

MPJE exam trap: 7 DAYS is not every opioid Rx for every adult. §18D adds a PHARMACIST risk-information and antagonist-offer duty for CII opioid.

Official references: M.G.L. c.94C §19D — opioid seven-day limits · M.G.L. c.94C §18D — CII opioid risk information/antagonist offer · M.G.L. c.94C §23(d) — quantities of II/III · 21 CFR 1306.04 — controlled prescription legitimate purpose

9. Voluntary non-opiate directive and lawful revocation

Original Korean unit: w2-c-13 · Law review November 10, 2026

M.G.L. c.94C §18B permits a patient to execute a voluntary non-opiate directive indicating that they do not wish to receive opioid prescribing or administration. The statute also permits eligible authorized persons to act and allows the individual to revoke orally or in writing, subject to the statute's process. The directive is not simply a drug allergy entry and should not be treated as irrevocable.

The law offers specified outpatient written/electronic prescription validity presumptions and good-faith protections. Those provisions are not a blanket license to ignore a directive that the pharmacist actually knows remains active. Check the patient's wishes, any proper revocation, applicable exceptions and the prescriber order before acting. A non-opiate directive does not prohibit unrelated non-opioid medication.

Pharmacy scenario: A patient with a documented non-opiate directive presents a new prescription and says they revoked the directive. The pharmacist evaluates the actual revocation and legal/patient-safety circumstances, rather than assuming the old directive is necessarily permanent or meaningless.

MPJE exam trap: Directive ≠ allergy; revocation can be oral or written. Good-faith protections depend on facts, not willful disregard.

Official references: M.G.L. c.94C §18B — voluntary non-opiate directive

10. Methadone for pain is not ordinary retail methadone maintenance for OUD

Original Korean unit: w2-c-14 · Law review November 10, 2026

Methadone is Schedule II. For legitimate pain treatment, a qualified practitioner can issue an appropriately lawful retail prescription. For routine OUD maintenance/detoxification, the federal framework in 21 CFR 1306.07(a) and 42 CFR Part 8 generally requires authorized opioid treatment program (OTP) direct administration/dispensing. The fact that a prescriber possesses a general DEA number does not transform ordinary retail methadone pain prescribing into an OTP prescription pathway.

Buprenorphine used for OUD is generally Schedule III, can be lawfully prescribed by appropriately authorized community practitioners, and can be dispensed by retail pharmacies subject to applicable federal/state and product-specific requirements. Do not assume every methadone prescription is for OUD, or that every methadone OUD patient can receive retail refills.

Pharmacy scenario: A doctor sends methadone for documented chronic pain: evaluate standard CII validity. A clinician asks to supply methadone for OUD maintenance via an ordinary community pharmacy prescription: the OTP treatment pathway raises a different legal issue.

MPJE exam trap: METHADONE PAIN ≠ OUD MAINTENANCE; buprenorphine OUD usually follows lawful Schedule III prescription pathway.

Official references: 21 CFR 1306.07 — methadone/OTP and three-day OUD emergency · SAMHSA — methadone treatment · 21 CFR 1306.04 — controlled prescription legitimate purpose · DEA buprenorphine FAQ: removal of X-waiver

11. Buprenorphine X-waiver elimination and one-time MATE training

Original Korean unit: w2-c-14 · Law review November 10, 2026

The federal DATA/X-waiver, associated X-number, and former federal patient caps for buprenorphine OUD treatment were eliminated by the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023. DEA explains that appropriately licensed/registered practitioners may prescribe buprenorphine with their applicable standard DEA registration, assuming state scope and legitimate purpose. Old dispenser software fields or historical notes referring to XDEA do not restore the former requirement.

Professional/state and federal registrations remain necessary. The separately enacted MATE Act requires a one-time eight-hour SUD training/qualifying education attestation for covered DEA practitioner registrants (except veterinarians) on their first initial/renewal application on or after June 27, 2023. There are several alternative recognized ways to fulfill the requirement. It is not the same as requesting a buprenorphine-specific X-waiver, and an ordinary pharmacy is not universally required to receive a copy of the prescriber's MATE training certificate with every buprenorphine prescription.

Pharmacy scenario: A buprenorphine/naloxone prescription has a valid standard DEA number but no old X-number. The missing X-waiver is not a basis by itself to decline the prescription in 2026.

MPJE exam trap: X-WAIVER GONE ≠ DEA registration gone. MATE 8 HOURS is separate, generally ONE-TIME practitioner attestation.

Official references: DEA buprenorphine FAQ: removal of X-waiver · DEA MATE Act training guidance · DEA MATE Act Q&A · 21 CFR 1306.04 — controlled prescription legitimate purpose

12. Three-day OUD emergency bridge and hospital incidental treatment

Original Korean unit: w2-c-15 · Law review November 10, 2026

21 CFR 1306.07(b) is a limited exception allowing an eligible practitioner not specifically registered as an OTP to administer or directly dispense (NOT prescribe) a narcotic to relieve acute opioid withdrawal while arranging prompt referral. The current text permits up to three days' medication to be dispensed at ONE TIME; that emergency treatment cannot be renewed or extended.

Historical versions described one day's medicine at a time; that is outdated. The exception does not authorize an ordinary retail pharmacist to create a new methadone OUD prescription without legal prescriber authorization. §1306.07(c) separately allows hospital administration/dispensing for opioid withdrawal as an incidental adjunct to treatment of a different medical/surgical condition; it is not an all-purpose community OUD maintenance prescription. §1306.07(f) has a narrow pharmacy-to-practitioner delivery route for specified Schedule III–V OUD injection/implant products meant for direct administration.

Pharmacy scenario: An ED practitioner administers and directly dispenses up to three days of methadone as an emergency bridge while arranging an OTP referral. This is not a routine retail prescription for ongoing maintenance.

MPJE exam trap: 3 days AT ONCE is permitted under the specified emergency direct-dispensing route, but it cannot renew; direct DISPENSING ≠ retail PRESCRIBING.

Official references: 21 CFR 1306.07 — methadone/OTP and three-day OUD emergency · DEA buprenorphine FAQ: removal of X-waiver

13. 2026 general telemedicine versus permanent OUD-specific telemedicine

Original Korean unit: w2-c-17 · Law review November 10, 2026

General temporary authority: the fourth DEA/HHS extension at 21 CFR 1307.41 applies through December 31, 2026. A practitioner with appropriate DEA authority may prescribe Schedule II–V by qualifying interactive telemedicine without a prior in-person evaluation, subject to the rule's conditions, legitimate purpose, registration and applicable state law. Do not treat all text messages, internet questionnaires or out-of-state activities as automatically legal. The temporary authority is not guaranteed to continue into 2027.

Separate permanent OUD-specific pathway: 21 CFR 1306.51 permits eligible FDA-approved Schedule III–V OUD medications such as buprenorphine via audio-only or audio-video telemedicine where an in-person evaluation has not occurred. The prescriber must query and document the PDMP of the patient's state before each covered prescription and may treat for an initial period up to six calendar months beginning with the first prescription—not simply issue one 180-day prescription. If the PDMP is inaccessible, the rule permits only seven-day supplies, with repeat documented attempts. The initial six-month limit requires an in-person evaluation or another lawfully authorized telemedicine pathway for continued treatment.

Special pharmacist identity verification: Under §1306.51(b)(4), the pharmacist must verify identity before filling an order issued under that permanent specific rule. It permits government photo ID or other sufficiently reliable identity documents, including those of qualified ultimate users. This does not invalidate Massachusetts's independent 247 CMR 9.04(14) government-issued customer-ID/hardship/delivery requirements; assess both applicable laws. Do not mistakenly apply the OUD-specific PDMP/six-month conditions to every Schedule II video visit valid under the distinct temporary general route.

Pharmacy scenario: A practitioner relies on §1306.51 for audio-only buprenorphine initiation. The prescriber checks state PDMP and documents it; the pharmacist verifies identity under that rule plus MA law. An ordinary Schedule II video visit may instead rely on the temporary §1307.41 pathway.

MPJE exam trap: 2026 TEMPORARY GENERAL II–V route ≠ PERMANENT OUD-ONLY §1306.51 (6 months, PDMP, 7-day when unavailable, ID).

Official references: 21 CFR 1307.41 — 2026 temporary telemedicine extension · 21 CFR 1306.51 — special permanent OUD telemedicine prescribing · Federal Register — 2025 fourth telemedicine temporary extension · Federal Register — permanent OUD-specific telemedicine rule · SAMHSA — buprenorphine telemedicine guidance · 247 CMR 9.04 — pharmacist and customer identification requirements · M.G.L. c.94C §18 — out-of-state controlled prescriptions

Question 1. A pharmacist reviews a patient's MassPAT history and then dispenses oxycodone. Which statement is correct?
  1. The patient-history query and pharmacy dispensing submission are different legal actions
  2. The query automatically sends the new prescription report
  3. Every prescriber must request pharmacist permission to query
  4. Pharmacy reporting makes all clinical due diligence unnecessary

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. A clinical query and a dispensation data report have distinct triggers.

B: PMP lookup is not dispensing-file submission.

C: That is not how prescriber lookup authority operates.

D: Reporting does not replace professional patient-safety review.

Question 2. A Massachusetts practitioner plans to prescribe lorazepam. Which PMP rule ordinarily applies?
  1. Only CII opioid prescriptions trigger mandatory PMP review
  2. Prescriber must generally check MassPAT before a benzodiazepine order unless a specified exception applies
  3. Pharmacist must check PMP for every noncontrolled prescription
  4. No PMP use is expected because lorazepam is Schedule IV

Correct answer: B.

A: Benzodiazepines can be IV and are covered.

B: Correct. The mandate is drug-category based.

C: No universal noncontrolled mandate exists.

D: Schedule IV benzodiazepines still trigger the practitioner rule.

Question 3. An authorized PMP delegate leaves the prescriber's office. What does the regulation say?
  1. Wait one month before changing access
  2. No notification is needed if no prescriptions were viewed
  3. Notify DPH within one business day of loss of delegate authorization or terms violation
  4. Submit a controlled-drug inventory instead

Correct answer: C.

A: One month is not the rule.

B: The status change itself triggers the rule.

C: Correct; 105 CMR 700.012(F)(3).

D: Controlled inventory is a separate issue.

Question 4. A retail MA pharmacy dispenses gabapentin. Which statement is accurate?
  1. Gabapentin is federally Schedule V
  2. Every other Schedule VI product must be reported the same way
  3. Gabapentin appears on PMP only after an X-waiver prescription
  4. Gabapentin is MA Schedule VI and separately reportable as an Additional Drug

Correct answer: D.

A: Gabapentin is not federally Schedule V.

B: The reporting requirement is selective.

C: X-waiver was removed and does not govern gabapentin reporting.

D: Correct. Its MA Additional Drug designation is key.

Question 5. A Massachusetts pharmacy dispenses no II–V drugs or gabapentin in its reporting period. Which is correct?
  1. A zero report is ordinarily expected unless a qualifying reporting waiver applies
  2. A fictitious transaction should be created
  3. No PMP report can ever be required without dispensing
  4. Monthly reporting is always sufficient

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. Zero report tells DCP no reportable activity occurred.

B: False reporting would be inappropriate.

C: Zero-report rules specifically exist.

D: Ordinary filing is much more frequent.

Question 6. A patient without government ID needs a Schedule II medicine urgently; denial would cause serious hardship. What is a permitted pharmacist approach?
  1. Never dispense any controlled substance without a card under any circumstances
  2. Document the serious hardship and obtain the required printed name/address and signature alternative and correct reporting entry
  3. Use the patient's SSN as the customer identifier
  4. Give unlimited supplies if the patient reports hardship

Correct answer: B.

A: A narrow hardship exception exists.

B: Correct. The regulation specifies case-by-case documentation and signature.

C: Not a substitute for the defined customer identifier.

D: Hardship does not authorize unlimited quantities.

Question 7. Which statement about naloxone PMP data in Massachusetts is correct?
  1. Naloxone is automatically treated like federally controlled gabapentin
  2. Naloxone records must appear on each patient's MassPAT profile
  3. Naloxone is an aggregate-data reporting category that does not appear on the patient's profile, and customer ID is not required by DCP guidance
  4. Every naloxone dose requires an XDEA number

Correct answer: C.

A: Neither characterization is accurate.

B: DCP says naloxone data do not populate individual PMP profiles.

C: Correct. Preferred daily PMP or annual reporting routes may apply.

D: The X-waiver is abolished and unrelated.

Question 8. Which Massachusetts opioid duty applies when dispensing a Schedule II opioid?
  1. Offer an antagonist only if the patient has overdosed
  2. Refuse to dispense unless the patient purchases naloxone
  3. Automatically issue a 90-day opioid pain supply
  4. Inform the patient of adverse opioid risks and offer to dispense an opioid antagonist

Correct answer: D.

A: The §18D duty is not limited to overdose events.

B: Offering does not require compulsory purchase.

C: §18D has nothing to do with automatic quantity increases.

D: Correct. §18D(b) added a pharmacist-specific offer/information rule.

Question 9. A legally qualified practitioner prescribes buprenorphine for OUD without an old XDEA number. Which statement is true?
  1. The former DATA X-waiver was abolished, but ordinary DEA/state prescribing authority and legitimate purpose still apply
  2. The X-waiver remains mandatory
  3. All controlled-substance registration is abolished
  4. A pharmacist must obtain a MATE certificate for each prescription

Correct answer: A.

A: Correct. X-waiver/caps ended, not DEA registration or state scope.

B: The old requirement was removed.

C: Registration remains necessary.

D: MATE is a separate practitioner attestation, not a per-prescription pharmacy certificate.

Question 10. Under the specific 21 CFR 1306.51 audio-only OUD telemedicine pathway, which is correct?
  1. The rule automatically authorizes 12 months of therapy with no safeguards
  2. The prescriber has a six-calendar-month initial period with PDMP checks, seven-day limits when unavailable; pharmacy verifies identity before fill
  3. It applies identically to all Schedule II stimulants
  4. The general temporary rule stays in effect without limit past 2026

Correct answer: B.

A: The special rule is limited and has concrete safeguards.
